# email-address-internationalization-eai

**Issue:** Email Address Internationalization (EAI) allows addresses with non-ASCII characters — Unicode local parts and internationalized domain names (IDNs) — so users can have addresses in their own script, e.g. a Chinese, Arabic, or Devanagari local part. As of 2025-2026, Gmail and Microsoft Outlook support SMTPUTF8 (RFC 6531) on the receiving side, and universal-acceptance advocacy (ICANN UA, ISOC training programs) keeps pushing adoption, but the ecosystem is uneven: many smaller and self-hosted MTAs still reject or mangle internationalized addresses, and client-side support (IMAP/SMTP clients, form widgets, databases) lags the servers. Engineering systems that capture, validate, store, and send to email addresses must therefore handle UTF-8 addresses correctly without breaking delivery to the parts of the network that cannot.

## Protocol mechanics

1. **SMTPUTF8 negotiation (RFC 6531).** A sending MTA that wants to transmit UTF-8 addresses announces SMTPUTF8 on the MAIL FROM command. If the receiving server does not advertise SMTPUTF8 in its EHLO response, the sender must not send non-ASCII addresses on that connection — it should downgraded-deliver, defer, or bounce per local policy. There is no universal fallback: RFC 6533 defines DSN handling for UTF-8, and older downgrade proposals (RFC 6152/5504-style address mapping) were never broadly implemented.
2. **Message headers are 8-bit UTF-8 (RFC 6532).** In an EAI message, the To/From/Cc header fields carry raw UTF-8 addresses rather than encoded-word escapes (encoded-words were never legal inside addresses). MIME parameters and body content follow RFC 6532's global 8-bit encoding. A non-EAI-aware library that re-encodes headers to RFC 2047 encoded-words inside an address will produce syntactically invalid headers that strict receivers reject.
3. **Domains are IDNA, local parts are free-form Unicode.** The domain side must be a valid IDN registered under IDNA2008/UTS-46 rules and resolves via its A-label (punycode) form, e.g. xn--bcher-kva.example for a Unicode label. The local part has no normalization standard at all: it is an opaque byte string (case-sensitive by RFC 5321, though almost everyone treats it case-insensitively), which is the source of most identity-handling bugs.
4. **Bounces and DSNs carry UTF-8 too (RFC 6533).** Delivery status notifications for EAI mail use UTF-8 addresses in their own headers. Your bounce processor must accept non-ASCII in the failing-address field it parses; a regex built from `[a-zA-Z0-9._%+-]+` will silently drop every EAI bounce, keeping dead internationalized addresses on your list.

## Sender-side engineering

1. **Stop rejecting non-ASCII at validation time.** The classic frontend/backend regex plus a hidden input field rejecting anything outside ASCII guarantees internationalized users cannot sign up. Validate structure (one @, sane lengths: 64 octets local part per RFC 5321 — note octets, not characters, since UTF-8 is multi-byte — 255 for domain, 254 total) and verify deliverability with an SMTP RCPT probe or confirmation email rather than trying to encode grammar in a regex.
2. **Normalize consistently before storing, and store both forms.** Apply Unicode NFC normalization to the local part and lowercase it (matching universal receiver behavior), and store the domain in both its U-label (Unicode) and A-label (punycode) forms. Lookups for duplicates and suppression checks must compare normalized forms; otherwise 用户@example.com and its NFD or case-variant twins become three different "users."
3. **Probe receiver capability before relying on EAI delivery.** Because a large fraction of MTAs still lack SMTPUTF8, transactional senders should treat EAI addresses as a delivery-risk tier: attempt SMTPUTF8 delivery, and if the receiver does not advertise it, record the failure distinctly (capability mismatch, not a bad address) instead of hard-bouncing the user. Gmail and Outlook/Exchange Online accept SMTPUTF8, which covers the bulk of consumer traffic, but corporate and self-hosted Postfix defaults vary.
4. **Keep DKIM, SPF, and DMARC ASCII-only.** The d= domain in DKIM signatures, SPF mechanisms, and DMARC records operate on the A-label (punycode) form. Sign EAI mail from your ASCII infrastructure domain; alignment rules in DMARC compare against the From domain's A-label. Never publish punycode/Unicode confusion in DNS — the IDN must be registered and DNSSEC/DKIM keys published against the A-label.

## Receiver-side engineering

1. **Accept SMTPUTF8 in your MTA and IMAP stack.** Postfix enables this with smtputf8_enable=yes, which advertises SMTPUTF8, accepts UTF-8 envelope addresses, and generates RFC 6533 DSNs. Verify the full path, not just the MTA: IMAP servers and clients must store and render UTF-8 mailbox names and addresses (UTF8=ACCEPT capability), and several popular IMAP clients still mishandle it, so test with real non-ASCII messages end to end.
2. **Handle sub-addressing and case in canonicalization.** If your system deduplicates or suppresses on address, canonicalize Unicode after NFC normalization, lowercase the domain always, and lowercase the local part to match majority MTA behavior — but remember RFC 5321 makes the local part case-sensitive by the letter of the spec, so a few exotic providers may treat Móricka and móricka as different mailboxes. When in doubt for identity binding, verify via confirmation email rather than string equality.
3. **Test against real-world EAI corpora.** Use the ICANN/UTA test suites and addresses combining: pure non-ASCII local parts, mixed-script local parts, emoji (technically possible in local parts, universally a bad idea to generate), RTL scripts, and IDN domains with confusable characters. Automated tests should assert your capture form, storage encoding (UTF-8 end to end — a Latin-1 database column corrupts these silently), SMTP submission, and bounce parsing all survive.

## Operational pitfalls

1. **Client-side lag is the real blocker.** Even where servers support EAI, desktop and mobile mail clients frequently cannot address, render, or reply to internationalized addresses without mangling them. Senders of critical transactional mail (password resets, receipts, legal notices) should still encourage or require an ASCII alias for account-recovery purposes where the user has one.
2. **Confusable-script fraud (homograph attacks).** Cyrillic а versus Latin a in a visible From domain is a phishing classic. Receiving systems should apply confusable detection or hold-and-warn on mixed-script From domains; sending systems should simply never generate mixed-script identifiers. BIMI/ logo display makes this worse — verify Mark/CVC only for exact-match domains.
3. **Legacy middleware corrupts UTF-8 addresses silently.** Database drivers, log pipelines, analytics tags, and URL-encoding of email in query strings (must be percent-encoded as UTF-8) each present a mangling opportunity. Fuzz your pipeline: inject an EAI address at signup and follow it through storage, send, bounce, unsubscribe link, and analytics event; any hop that shows ? or mojibake is a latent data-corruption bug.
4. **Do not encode EAI addresses as RFC 2047 encoded-words in address positions.** Encoded-words are for display names and unstructured headers only. If your library forces MIME-encoding of headers, configure the address headers to pass through as raw UTF-8 on SMTPUTF8 sessions, or the message will be rejected by strict EAI receivers while looking superficially fine in permissive clients.
