# esp-migration-zero-downtime

**Issue:** Moving production email traffic from one email service provider (ESP) to another is one of the highest-risk infrastructure changes a sending program can make. Done naively — flip the DNS, point the API at the new vendor, send full volume — you lose sender reputation, spike bounces, drop webhooks, and can burn a domain that took years to warm. The migration has to be executed as a gradual traffic shift with authentication pre-staged, suppression lists merged, event streams dual-consumed, and a rollback path. Current drivers make this urgent for many teams: Microsoft begins rejecting Basic-auth SMTP AUTH in Exchange Online between March 1 and April 30, 2026 (disabled by default for all tenants by end of 2026), forcing apps off legacy submission paths and onto Graph API, XOAUTH2, or a dedicated ESP — which is exactly when a botched migration hurts most.

## Pre-migration groundwork

1. **Inventory every sending surface before touching DNS.** Catalog transactional sends, marketing campaigns, system notifications, cron-driven digests, and hardcoded SMTP clients (printers, CI, ERP). Involuntary migrations (like the Microsoft SMTP AUTH deprecation) routinely surface forgotten senders that were never in the docs; anything you miss keeps firing from the old path after cutover and fragments your reputation across two providers.
2. **Stage the new provider's authentication records without activating them.** Add the new ESP's SPF include, DKIM selectors, and return-path CNAMEs alongside (not replacing) the old provider's records. SPF permits multiple includes up to the 10-lookup limit, so both providers can coexist; validate with a checker before any real mail flows. Only remove the old provider's records after the final cutover, weeks later.
3. **Migrate the suppression list first, not last.** Export bounces, spam complaints, and unsubscribes from the old provider and import them into the new one before the first message is sent. The suppression file is more valuable than the subscriber list itself: re-sending to known complainers on a cold IP reputation is the single fastest way to get the new provider's pool blocked or your domain flagged.
4. **Dual-consume event webhooks during the transition.** Keep the old provider's bounce/complaint/delivery webhooks flowing while adding the new provider's, and normalize both into one event schema. Bounce categorization differs between vendors (what SendGrid calls "blocked" another calls "soft bounce"), so map to your own taxonomy rather than trusting either vendor's labels.
5. **Decide shared pool versus dedicated IP on the new provider deliberately.** Small-to-mid volume senders (under roughly 50k/month) usually start on the new provider's shared pool because a fresh dedicated IP carries zero reputation and needs weeks of warming; only migrate to dedicated once volume justifies it. If you do take a dedicated IP, plan a warm-up schedule exactly as you would for a new domain.

## Cutover strategy: parallel sending

1. **Shift traffic gradually, never big-bang.** The proven pattern is percentage-based traffic splitting: 5 percent to the new provider for several days, then 25, 50, and 100 as metrics hold. Run the split at the routing layer (your own send abstraction or a feature flag), so you can tune percentages per template or per traffic class — transactional receipts can move earlier than marketing campaigns because they have the highest engagement.
2. **Warm the new path with your most engaged segment.** For the first marketing sends on the new provider, target the recently-engaged cohort (opens/clicks in the last 30-60 days). Positive engagement signals (opens, replies, non-junk moves) are what mailbox providers use to build reputation for an unknown sending fingerprint; blasting your oldest, coldest list first is how migrations fail.
3. **Keep both providers fully operational until the old one is dark.** Maintain the old account, its DNS records, and its inbound bounce processing for the overlap window (typically 2-6 weeks). Mail in flight, delayed bounces, and out-of-band DSNs will still arrive at the old provider's return-path domain for days after your last send; tearing it down early silently loses bounce data.
4. **Match the old provider's sending identity on the new one.** Use the same From domain, same friendly-from, same reply-to, and equivalent DKIM signing domain where possible. Mailbox providers correlate reputation partially on the visible From domain and DMARC alignment, so preserving identity lets some existing domain reputation carry over even though the IP and infrastructure fingerprint change.
5. **Define quantitative gates between stages.** Before moving 5 to 25 percent, require: bounce rate under 2 percent, complaint rate under 0.1 percent (the Gmail/Yahoo bulk-sender ceiling), no blocklist listings, and delivery rates within a couple of points of the old provider. If a gate fails, freeze the split, diagnose, and roll the percentage back — the abort path must be a one-line config change.

## Data and integration migration

1. **Treat template parity as an engineering deliverable.** Rebuild every template on the new provider (it will not import another vendor's markup), then diff rendered output side by side — subject, preheader, From, links, unsubscribe mechanics, and tracking parameters. A migration that changes unsubscribe URLs or breaks UTM tagging quietly destroys analytics and, worse, GDPR/CAN-SPAM compliance if the unsubscribe stops working.
2. **Re-point click and open tracking domains carefully.** If you use a custom tracking domain (CNAME), plan its move: the new provider needs its own tracking CNAME, and links in already-sent mail keep pointing at the old domain for weeks. Keep the old tracking domain resolving and redirecting until clicks on legacy sends decay to zero, or historical email links will 404.
3. **Migrate scheduled and triggered campaigns as code, not screenshots.** Automation flows (welcome series, abandoned cart, re-engagement) must be rebuilt on the new provider's automation engine, with entry conditions, waits, and exit criteria reproduced exactly. Backfill-trigger test entries for each flow and assert the sequence fires in the right order with the right content before redirecting production entries.
4. **Handle the Microsoft-hosted application mail separately.** If part of your migration is driven by Exchange Online's SMTP AUTH deprecation (Basic auth begins being rejected March 2026, fully by April 30, 2026), decide per workload between Graph API sendMail, SMTP with OAuth 2.0 XOAUTH2, High Volume Email (HVE) for internal app mail, or offloading to the ESP entirely. Do not plan on the temporary tenant re-enablement that Microsoft allows into late 2026 — it is a bridge, not a destination.

## Post-migration verification

1. **Compare provider dashboards for the same send.** After cutover, send a canary campaign through the new provider and compare delivered, bounced, opened, and complained counts against the old provider's historical baselines for the same list shape. A delivery rate drop of more than 1-2 percentage points at equal list quality means the new path has a problem (authentication, content filtering, or pool reputation), not "normal variance."
2. **Verify authentication from the wild.** Inspect DMARC aggregate reports during and after the switch: every message from both providers during the overlap must show SPF and DKIM alignment with your From domain. A mis-signed message class from the new provider shows up here days before users complain.
3. **Watch blocklists and postmaster tools on the new footprint.** Monitor Google Postmaster Tools and Microsoft SNDS for the new sending IPs/domains, plus automated blocklist checks, for at least 30 days after full cutover. Catching a CSS-style listing early (which typically auto-expires about 24 hours after the spam stops) is far cheaper than discovering it from customer bounce complaints.
4. **Only then decommission the old provider.** Export final suppression deltas, archive templates and event logs for audit, remove the old SPF include and DKIM records (this is also your security hygiene moment — a stale SPF include for a provider you no longer use is an attack surface), and close the account. Keep the exports forever; suppression history is compliance evidence.
