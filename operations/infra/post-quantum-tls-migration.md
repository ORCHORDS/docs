# post-quantum-tls-migration

**Issue:** Post-quantum TLS stopped being theoretical in 2025–2026: OpenSSL 3.5 enabled the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange by default, Cloudflare and Akamai turned it on for browser traffic in late 2025, AWS enabled ML-KEM hybrid PQ-TLS by default across KMS, Secrets Manager, ACM and S3 in April 2026, and roughly half the web is already negotiated PQ-safe. Teams that ignored this now hit real symptoms — oversized ClientHellos breaking middleboxes, CPU spikes on handshake-heavy proxies, and compliance roadmaps demanding a migration inventory. This article covers what actually changed, how to enable and control hybrid key exchange on proxies and servers, the operational risks of turning it on, and how to build the crypto-agility that NIST-aligned migration plans require. It focuses on key exchange; certificate lifecycle stays in `ssl-tls-certificate-management.md`.

## What Changed in 2025–2026

1. **FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) landed in August 2024.** NIST finalized ML-KEM (the standardization of Kyber) as the Key Encapsulation Mechanism replacing classical ECDH key exchange, with ML-DSA (FIPS 204) following for signatures — the regulatory anchor every migration roadmap now cites.
2. **Hybrid, not pure-PQ, is the deployment model.** The standardized first-generation construction combines classical X25519 with ML-KEM-768 into one hybrid group (`X25519MLKEM768`, IETF `draft-ietf-tls-mlkem`), so a quantum-capable attacker must break both primitives — this is what "PQ-safe" means in 2026 measurements of web adoption.
3. **OpenSSL 3.5 flipped the default (April 2025).** OpenSSL 3.5 ships X25519MLKEM768 enabled by default for TLS 1.3 at security level 1, which means any server, proxy, or client rebuilt on 3.5+ (or on distros shipping it — Fedora 42, Ubuntu 25.10 era) began offering hybrid PQ without any config change.
4. **Browsers and CDNs went first.** Chrome shipped X25519MLKEM768 by default from Chrome 141 (September 2025), with Cloudflare, Akamai, and Fastly enabling it server-side around the same window — that combination alone pushed a large fraction of browser traffic to PQ-negotiated handshakes during late 2025.
5. **Cloud defaults made it an infrastructure fact.** AWS enabled ML-KEM hybrid PQ-TLS by default on major SDK-facing services in April 2026; GCP and Azure followed with opt-in-then-default tracks, so "our stack does PQ" is increasingly true by default rather than by decision — which is exactly why you must understand failure modes you never opted into.

## Enabling and Controlling It on Your Stack

1. **Check what you already negotiate.** `openssl s_client -connect host:443 -groups X25519MLKEM768` (and reading the negotiated group from output) plus client-side telemetry reveal whether your OpenSSL 3.5+ rebuild already speaks hybrid; most teams discover some endpoints are PQ and some are not, depending on which image built against which OpenSSL.
2. **Pin groups explicitly on proxies.** In NGINX, `ssl_ecdh_curve X25519MLKEM768:X25519;` controls TLS 1.3 group preference; HAProxy exposes the equivalent via OpenSSL defaults or `curves`/`groups` directives, and Envoy via `supported_groups` in its common TLS context — pinning makes behavior deterministic across OpenSSL upgrades instead of inheriting whatever the new default is.
3. **TLS 1.3 is a hard prerequisite.** Hybrid ML-KEM groups only exist for TLS 1.3 key exchange; any endpoint still limited to TLS 1.2 cannot participate, so the first milestone of any PQ migration is finishing the TLS 1.3 cutover (including internal east-west traffic, which is usually the laggard).
4. **Keep classical fallback configured, not accidental.** Order groups so classical X25519 (and `secp256r1` where needed for legacy peers) remain offered after the hybrid group; deliberate fallback ordering turns "some old clients fail" into "old clients negotiate classical and keep working" — Cloudflare's 2025 rollout data showed a small but non-zero fraction of clients only succeed on the classical retry.
5. **Version-pin via crypto policies, not vendored flags.** On Fedora/RHEL use `update-crypto-policies` (which sets group defaults system-wide) and on Debian-family track the OpenSSL version; embedding one-off `ssl_ecdh_curve` lines in fifty server blocks is how fleets end up with divergent TLS behavior that nobody can audit.

## Operational Risks and Mitigations

1. **The bigger ClientHello breaks middleboxes.** The hybrid ClientHello carries a ~1.1–2.3 KB ML-KEM share, and deployments (Cloudflare's measurements especially) reported a small fraction of connections — on the order of hundredths to tenths of a percent — failing against old middleboxes, IPS boxes, and TCP stacks with small MSS or buggy fragmentation handling.
2. **Mitigate with retries and MTU awareness, not by disabling.** The working mitigation pattern from the 2025 rollouts: keep hybrid as the first offer, ensure clients/proxies fall back to classical X25519 on retry, and fix PMTUD/MSS clamping on paths where the large ClientHello fragments; wholesale disabling PQ to please one broken middlebox re-opens the harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure.
3. **Handshake CPU is noticeable at proxy scale.** ML-KEM encapsulation/decapsulation is fast (microseconds), but hybrid key exchange adds work per handshake and enlarges handshakes; at a front proxy doing tens of thousands of handshakes per second, measure p99 handshake latency and CPU before/after — and lean harder on session resumption, TLS tickets, and connection reuse to keep handshake volume down.
4. **Session tickets and resumption still matter — with caveats.** Resumed sessions skip key exchange entirely (so no PQ cost), but resumption keys descend from the original handshake; a fully PQ-safe long-lived connection story means limiting ticket lifetimes rather than riding one pre-PQ handshake for hours, and 0-RTT data is (as always) replayable and should stay off for non-idempotent requests.
5. **Client diversity is the long tail.** Java runtimes, Python `ssl` on older OpenSSL, embedded agents, and old curl builds negotiate classical only — which is fine (hybrid is backward compatible), but per-client telemetry must distinguish "classical by capability" from "classical because PQ attempt failed", or your PQ adoption dashboard will silently overstate protection.

## Inventory and Crypto-Agility

1. **Build a cryptographic inventory first.** NIST-aligned migration starts with knowing where TLS is spoken: external LBs, service-mesh sidecars, database drivers, object-storage clients, internal APIs. Scan with tools that report negotiated groups (nmap NSE, testssl.sh with PQ checks, or your CDN's TLS analytics) and record per-endpoint status — the inventory itself is a compliance deliverable (NIST IR 8547-style milestones).
2. **Hash-and-sign for long-lived data comes later.** ML-KEM protects key exchange (confidentiality against harvest-now-decrypt-later); authentication still uses classical signatures until ML-DSA certificate adoption matures — hybrid ML-DSA/classical certificates and composite approaches are the 2026 planning track, so do not block KEM rollout waiting for PQ certs.
3. **Centralize TLS configuration.** Whether via crypto policies, a base NGINX/HAProxy module in Terraform/Ansible, or a service-mesh mesh-wide TLS settings object, TLS group and protocol policy must be one change in one place — the migration's real lesson is that fleets with per-server TLS stanzas could not turn PQ on in weeks.
4. **Test in staging against real client matrix.** Replay your production client mix (browser versions, mobile SDKs, health checkers, partner integrations) against a PQ-enabled staging endpoint and watch handshake failure rates per client class; the failure modes are always specific old clients or specific middlebox paths, and they are cheap to find in staging and expensive in production.
5. **Plan for group churn.** X25519MLKEM768 is the first standardized group, not the last (ML-KEM-1024 variants, ML-DSA in certificates, and future revisions are expected); treat groups as rotating configuration with a tested rollback, and alert when negotiated-group distribution shifts sharply — that is your early warning that a client population or upstream default changed underneath you.
