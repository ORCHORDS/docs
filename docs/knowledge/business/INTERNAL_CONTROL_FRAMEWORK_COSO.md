# Internal Control Framework Governance

## Scope

This article addresses the umbrella corporate discipline of internal control — the system of processes that an organisation's board, management, and personnel use to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in effectiveness and efficiency of operations, reliability of reporting, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations. It draws on the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission's 2013 *Internal Control — Integrated Framework* and is intended to sit above commercial, customer-success, marketing, partnerships, strategy, and support topics. It does not address external audit standards or financial-reporting standards, which are covered in separate articles in this directory.

## Workflow

COSO's framework defines internal control as a process effected by people, operating through five interrelated components and three categories of objectives. A working internal-control programme follows a recurring cycle:

1. **Set objectives in each category.** The organisation articulates objectives for operations, reporting, and compliance. Reporting objectives may further be divided into financial and non-financial reporting, with the framework noting that external financial reporting typically warrants greater rigour because of its public-issuer implications.
2. **Identify and assess risks.** Risks to the achievement of objectives are identified and analysed, including the likelihood of occurrence and the potential magnitude of impact. COSO treats risk assessment as a precondition for selecting controls.
3. **Select and develop control activities.** Control activities include approvals, authorisations, verifications, reconciliations, segregation of duties, and physical or logical safeguards. They are chosen to operate at the entity, division, operating-process, and transaction levels.
4. **Communicate and monitor.** Information necessary to support the functioning of internal control is identified, captured, and communicated in a form and timeframe that enables people to carry out their responsibilities. Ongoing and separate evaluations surface issues in a timely manner, and deficiencies are reported upward.
5. **Document and re-evaluate.** The framework expects documentation that supports the effective operation of internal control, with the level of formality suited to the organisation's size, complexity, and regulatory exposure.

## Controls and evidence

Effective documentation of internal control relies on:

- A narrative description of processes, control points, and responsible roles.
- Process flowcharts or risk-and-control matrices that map risks to controls and to the assertions they support.
- Documented testing of control design and operating effectiveness, with sample sizes and exception-handling conventions clearly described.
- Evidence of segregation of duties between authorisation, custody, recording, and reconciliation.
- A policy on information and communication that identifies what information is needed, who prepares it, and how it is distributed.
- Records of remediation actions, owners, target dates, and verification of completion.

## Validation

Independent and internal assurance work provides feedback on the design and operating effectiveness of controls. Sources of assurance include:

- Internal audit programmes aligned with the COSO framework and with the IIA's *International Standards for the Professional Practice of Internal Auditing*.
- External auditor reports on internal controls relevant to the audit of the financial statements.
- Self-assessments completed by process owners against a published control questionnaire.
- Regulatory examinations and inspections, where applicable.
- Periodic third-party readiness assessments before significant transactions such as initial public offerings or major system implementations.

## Failure correction

The framework identifies two categories of deficiency:

- **Control deficiency** — a deficiency in internal control exists when the design or operation of a control does not allow management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent or detect misstatements on a timely basis.
- **Significant deficiency and material weakness** — COSO uses these terms for deficiencies whose severity warrants escalation to the audit committee, with material weakness being the most serious classification.

When deficiencies are identified, documented remediation should include the root cause, the corrective control or compensating control, the responsible owner, the implementation date, and the procedure used to retest the control after implementation. Persistent exceptions or management override of controls require escalation to the board or audit committee and may require disclosure.

## Roles and responsibilities

Effective internal control requires clear assignment of responsibility across the three lines model commonly used in governance practice:

- **First line — operational management.** Owns the risks and the controls within its processes, executes controls in the normal course of work, and reports control performance and exceptions upward.
- **Second line — risk management, compliance, and other control functions.** Sets policies and standards, monitors first-line control performance, provides advice and challenge, and consolidates reporting to senior management and the board.
- **Third line — internal audit.** Provides independent assurance on the design and effectiveness of governance, risk management, and internal control, and reports to the audit committee or equivalent body.
- **Board and audit committee.** Approves the control framework, oversees the work of internal and external assurance providers, and considers significant deficiencies and material weaknesses.

## Limitations

The COSO framework is principles-based and prescribes outcomes rather than specific controls. Implementation depends on judgement about materiality, on the legal and regulatory environment, and on the cost-benefit trade-off of competing control options. Smaller organisations may find that some of the formal documentation expected by larger entities is disproportionate, and they may rely on simplified procedures as long as the principles are met. The framework is also distinct from, although complementary to, the *Enterprise Risk Management — Integrated Framework* published by COSO in 2017 and from sector-specific frameworks such as those used in banking or healthcare.
