# Advertising Substantiation

## Purpose

Advertising claims should be supported before they are published. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's advertising-substantiation policy describes a reasonable-basis requirement: advertisers should possess support appropriate to the claim before dissemination rather than create the evidence only after a challenge arises.

## Match evidence to the claim

The strength and type of support should reflect what the advertisement communicates. Factors that can matter include the type of claim, the product or service, the consequences of an inaccurate claim, what consumers reasonably expect, and the amount and quality of evidence experts in the field would consider reasonable.

A practical review should distinguish:

- objective claims that can be tested or verified;
- subjective opinion or obvious puffery;
- comparative claims about competitors;
- performance, safety, health, scientific, or quantitative claims that imply specific evidence; and
- qualifications or limitations needed to prevent an otherwise true statement from becoming misleading.

## Evidence workflow

1. Write down the exact claim and the reasonable interpretations a customer could take from it.
2. Identify what evidence would support each material interpretation.
3. Confirm the evidence exists before publication and is applicable to the product, audience, and conditions represented.
4. Preserve the source, date, methodology, and limitations of material substantiation.
5. Re-check recurring claims when the underlying product, evidence, or market conditions change.
6. Remove or qualify claims when the available evidence no longer supports them.

## Disclosures are not a substitute for evidence

A disclaimer can clarify a claim, but it should not be used to contradict the main message or compensate for an unsupported representation. The overall impression of the advertisement matters.

This article summarizes reusable advertising-review principles. Advertising, sector-specific, and consumer-protection requirements differ across jurisdictions and products.
