# Made in USA Origin Claims

## Purpose

Unqualified U.S.-origin claims should be made only when the product satisfies the Federal Trade Commission's Made in USA standard. The FTC's Made in USA Labeling Rule, 16 CFR Part 323, applies to unqualified U.S.-origin claims on product labels and requires marketers to be able to substantiate that the product is "all or virtually all" made in the United States.

## Separate unqualified and qualified claims

An **unqualified** claim such as "Made in USA" communicates that the product is all or virtually all made in the United States. A **qualified** claim describes a narrower fact, such as that a product is assembled in the United States or contains a stated amount of U.S. content.

Do not use a qualified claim to imply a broader origin representation than the evidence supports. Evaluate the complete presentation, including logos, maps, flags, imagery, headlines, and nearby statements, because the overall impression can communicate an origin claim even when no single sentence does so explicitly.

## Review workflow

1. Identify every express and implied U.S.-origin representation in packaging, product pages, advertising, and marketplace listings.
2. Determine whether each claim is unqualified or qualified.
3. Preserve evidence supporting the origin of significant parts, processing, assembly, and other material inputs relevant to the claim.
4. Check that qualifying language is accurate, specific, and prominent enough to prevent a misleading net impression.
5. Re-review claims when suppliers, manufacturing locations, component sourcing, or production processes change.
6. Remove or revise claims when the evidence no longer supports them.

## Enforcement context

The FTC's Made in USA Rule page was updated in July 2026 and continues to state that marketers making unqualified Made in USA claims on labels should be able to prove that their products are all or virtually all made in the United States. The FTC also maintains business guidance on U.S.-origin claims and enforcement materials.

## Scope note

This article summarizes U.S. federal advertising and labeling principles. Product-specific statutes, customs rules, procurement requirements, state laws, and non-U.S. origin-marking regimes may impose additional or different requirements.
