# Home Insulation R-Value Advertising Disclosures

## Purpose

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's R-Value Rule governs labeling and advertising claims for home insulation. It requires manufacturers, professional installers, retailers, and new-home sellers to provide consumers with standardized information about insulation performance before purchase.

Marketing teams should treat R-value, thickness, price, coverage, comparison, and energy-savings claims as controlled technical claims linked to the product's tested performance rather than as ordinary promotional copy.

## What R-value means

R-value expresses resistance to heat flow: the higher the R-value, the greater the insulating power. The Rule requires covered R-value claims to be based on specified test methods.

Do not infer an R-value from marketing language, a competitor's product, or a different thickness of the same material. The claimed value should trace to the test basis applicable to that product and configuration.

## Advertising triggers

FTC materials explain that advertising and promotional materials, including Internet advertising, can trigger specific disclosures when they include claims about a specific insulation product, such as:

- R-value;
- thickness;
- price;
- energy savings; or
- comparisons between insulation products.

Marketing templates should identify these trigger categories before publication so the required accompanying information is not added only after a complaint.

## R-value, thickness, and product type

When promotional material uses R-value or thickness claims, ensure the presentation identifies the insulation type and connects the R-value to the thickness needed to obtain that performance when the Rule requires it.

Avoid claims such as "R-38 insulation" if the surrounding page could cause customers to misunderstand which thickness, product, assembly, or installation condition supports that value.

## Price claims and coverage

When an advertisement states a price, the required context can include the coverage area at the stated thickness. FTC materials distinguish price-per-square-foot claims from other price presentations.

A pricing feed should therefore preserve enough product data to calculate or display:

- the stated price basis;
- the thickness associated with the R-value;
- applicable coverage area; and
- units of measure.

Do not compare package prices without accounting for differences in coverage and required thickness.

## Comparative claims

If an advertisement compares one insulation product or type to another, compare equivalent coverage bases and identify the R-value at the specific thickness as required.

Marketing review should reject comparisons that:

- compare unlike thicknesses without explanation;
- compare package prices while ignoring coverage;
- use a competitor's outdated R-value;
- imply that different R-values are equivalent; or
- extrapolate a tested product claim to an untested assembly.

## Required explanatory language

The R-Value Rule requires specified explanatory disclosures for certain advertising claims. The exact required wording should be taken from the current Rule rather than rewritten from memory.

Maintain mandatory statements as controlled content. If the FTC changes the prescribed language, update templates centrally and identify pages or campaigns using the previous version.

## Energy-savings claims

Claims such as "save 25% on heating" create additional substantiation and disclosure concerns. FTC materials require a reasonable basis for energy-savings claims and describe mandatory explanatory language in covered contexts.

Before publishing a savings claim, document:

- the tested or analytical basis;
- assumptions about climate, building condition, installation, and baseline;
- whether the result is typical, modeled, or conditional;
- required explanatory disclosures; and
- the person responsible for approving the claim.

Do not present modeled savings as guaranteed household results.

## Online retail requirements

FTC amendments to the Rule require insulation retailers selling covered products directly to consumers online to make required labels and fact sheets available on their websites.

A product-page release process should therefore verify:

- the correct fact sheet is linked to the correct product;
- required labels are available where applicable;
- R-value and thickness data on the page match the fact sheet;
- downloadable assets are current; and
- a mobile customer can reach the disclosures without hidden or broken links.

## Manufacturer and seller data handoff

Insulation information often passes from manufacturer to distributor, retailer, installer, marketplace, and customer. Establish a controlled handoff for:

- product identifier;
- insulation type;
- tested R-value;
- corresponding thickness;
- package coverage;
- fact-sheet version;
- required statements; and
- effective date of corrections.

Downstream sellers should have a reliable way to receive corrected technical information instead of copying marketing text permanently into their own systems.

## Non-insulation products with R-value claims

FTC amendments clarified treatment of R-value claims for products that are not traditionally marketed as insulation. If a product makes an R-value claim for residential use, confirm whether the Rule and applicable test requirements apply before advertising the value.

Do not use R-value as a generic "energy efficient" badge without a valid test basis.

## Installer communications

Professional installers should not contradict the manufacturer's controlled product information in quotes, proposals, or sales scripts. If an installer recommends a target R-value, distinguish the target from the tested R-value of the material and from the installed assembly outcome.

Installation quality can affect actual energy performance. Avoid promising savings that depend on conditions outside the tested product claim without appropriate qualification and substantiation.

## Quality-control review

Periodically sample product pages, ads, quotes, and comparison tools for:

- unsupported R-values;
- missing thickness context;
- price claims without required coverage information;
- outdated fact sheets;
- comparisons using inconsistent bases;
- missing mandatory explanatory language;
- unsupported energy-savings percentages; and
- product data that differs across seller channels.

Treat recurring discrepancies as source-data or workflow failures rather than isolated copy errors.

## Records

For material claims, retain the applicable test basis, product data, approved fact sheet, advertising version, and substantiation for savings or comparison claims in accordance with the organization's records policy.
