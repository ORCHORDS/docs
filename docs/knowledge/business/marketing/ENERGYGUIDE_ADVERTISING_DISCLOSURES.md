# EnergyGuide Advertising Disclosures

## Purpose

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's Energy Labeling Rule requires energy-use and cost information for covered consumer products. The familiar yellow EnergyGuide label is not only a package or showroom control: websites and print catalogs that advertise covered products also have disclosure responsibilities.

A marketing workflow should connect product data, the current EnergyGuide image, catalog content, and retailer pages so energy claims remain consistent with the information required by the Rule.

## Covered-product boundary

The Energy Labeling Rule covers specified product categories and has category-specific requirements. Before applying this workflow, confirm that the product and advertising format are within the current Rule.

Do not assume that every energy-related product uses the same label, test procedure, or disclosure format. Product teams should maintain a controlled mapping from product category and model to the applicable FTC and Department of Energy requirements.

## Online and catalog disclosure

FTC guidance states that a manufacturer, distributor, retailer, or private labeler that advertises a product with an EnergyGuide label on a website or in a print catalog must disclose clearly and conspicuously, on the page listing the product, either:

- all information contained on the product's EnergyGuide label; or
- an image of the EnergyGuide label itself.

Do not rely on a generic energy-information page several clicks away when the rule calls for disclosure on the page listing the product.

## Label-image availability

Manufacturers must make EnergyGuide label images available online in a way that allows retailers to hyperlink to or download them. FTC guidance says the label for a model must remain available for six months after production ends.

A manufacturer workflow should therefore:

1. generate the final label from controlled product data;
2. publish the correct model-specific label image;
3. provide a stable location usable by downstream sellers;
4. retain the image for the required post-production period; and
5. prevent model replacements from silently changing historical label content.

## Model matching

The label shown online must correspond to the actual model offered. Common errors include:

- linking a family-level label to several models with different energy characteristics;
- using a prior model-year label after a product update;
- showing a label for a different capacity or configuration;
- displaying a cached retailer image after the manufacturer has corrected the label; or
- using an illustrative sample label in place of the actual model label.

Automated catalog feeds should use a stable model identifier to bind the product record to the label asset.

## Clear and conspicuous presentation

When a page uses the label image, ensure customers can actually view it. Avoid thumbnails too small to read, low-resolution assets, hidden accordions whose labels imply unrelated technical details, or links whose destination is unclear.

When reproducing the information instead of the image, preserve the required information accurately and present it together on the product-listing page.

Responsive layouts should be tested on mobile and desktop so required information does not disappear at smaller breakpoints.

## Energy-cost and efficiency claims

Marketing claims should remain consistent with the EnergyGuide data and applicable standardized testing. Avoid turning an estimated annual operating cost into a guaranteed customer bill or presenting a comparison range as a promise of actual savings.

Where copy says a model "saves" a particular amount, document the baseline, calculation, test basis, assumptions, and required disclosures. General FTC advertising-substantiation principles still apply in addition to Energy Labeling Rule requirements.

## Retailer and marketplace feeds

If product data is syndicated to retailers, marketplaces, affiliates, or distributors, define which fields and assets carry EnergyGuide information.

The feed should support:

- exact model identification;
- current label URL or image;
- applicable energy-use or cost values;
- effective date or version where needed; and
- correction notices when source data changes.

A supplier should not assume downstream sellers will discover a corrected label without a defined update mechanism.

## Product changes

Trigger label and advertising review after changes to:

- model configuration;
- capacity or size;
- components that affect tested energy use;
- Department of Energy test procedures;
- FTC label requirements; or
- manufacturer-reported energy data.

Do not update marketing copy independently while leaving an obsolete label attached to the listing.

## Pre-publication checklist

Before publishing a covered product page or catalog entry, verify:

- the product is correctly classified under the Rule;
- the model identifier matches the label;
- the current EnergyGuide image or complete required information is present;
- presentation is clear and readable;
- energy-cost or savings claims are substantiated and consistent with the label;
- downstream feed data uses the same controlled source; and
- links to manufacturer label assets resolve successfully.

## Monitoring

Periodically scan active listings for:

- broken label links;
- missing images;
- mismatched models;
- unreadable mobile presentation;
- obsolete labels on discontinued models;
- unsupported energy-saving claims; and
- discrepancies between manufacturer and retailer data.

Prioritize correction where a mismatch could change a customer's comparison or purchase decision.

## Records

For material product launches or corrections, retain the source energy data, label version, model mapping, publication date, and evidence of significant corrections according to the applicable records policy.

The purpose is to preserve traceability without maintaining unnecessary duplicate product data indefinitely.
