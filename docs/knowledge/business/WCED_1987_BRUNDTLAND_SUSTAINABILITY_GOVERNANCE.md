# WCED 1987 Brundtland Sustainability Governance

## Purpose

The World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED, also known as the Brundtland Commission) report "Our Common Future" (1987) introduced the most widely used definition of sustainable development: "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." The report frames sustainability through three interconnected dimensions: economic, social, and environmental. This article governs the application of Brundtland sustainability principles so an organization can integrate sustainability into its strategy, decisions, and reporting in a way that is anchored to the Commission's framing. Note: this is a project-neutral summary of the WCED report; it does not assert any specific sustainability certification outcome.

## Scope

The Commission's framework applies to governments, organizations, and communities. Within this knowledge base, the article covers the Brundtland definition, the three-dimension framing, the implications for organizational strategy, and the integration of sustainability with decision-making and reporting. It does not replace more specific sustainability frameworks (GRI, SASB/ISSB, TCFD, EU CSRD); readers should overlay their sector and reporting requirements.

## Workflow

1. Adopt the Brundtland definition as the conceptual anchor for the organization's sustainability practice. The definition supports the integration of economic, social, and environmental considerations.
2. Identify the organization's sustainability issues: environmental (climate, biodiversity, water, waste, pollution), social (human rights, labor practices, community impact, customer impact), and economic (governance, anti-corruption, fair competition, value creation).
3. Integrate sustainability into the organization's strategy: align sustainability objectives with strategic objectives, allocate resources, set targets, and assign responsibility.
4. Apply the three-dimension framing to decisions: assess environmental and social impacts alongside financial impacts; consider both short-term and long-term effects.
5. Report on sustainability: communicate performance across the three dimensions; use recognized reporting frameworks where applicable.
6. Review and improve: track performance against targets, update the assessment of materiality, and refine the integration.

## Controls and evidence

Sustainability evidence includes the strategic objectives, the materiality assessment, the sustainability targets and progress, the integrated decision records (where sustainability considerations were weighed), and the sustainability report. The reporting should be transparent about successes and shortcomings.

## Validation

Validation should confirm the Brundtland definition is the conceptual anchor, the materiality assessment reflects the organization's context, sustainability is considered in material decisions, targets are tracked and reported, and reporting uses recognized frameworks. Periodic review confirms the integration remains effective.

## Failure correction

Common failure modes: sustainability is reduced to environmental issues (corrective: apply the three-dimension framing and consider social and economic issues too); sustainability is treated as compliance rather than strategy (corrective: integrate with strategic objectives and allocate resources); reporting is selective (corrective: report successes and shortcomings with appropriate context); decisions ignore sustainability impacts (corrective: require sustainability inputs in material decisions); the materiality assessment is one-time (corrective: review on strategy change and on context change).

## Limitations

The Brundtland definition is conceptual; it does not prescribe specific metrics or targets. The framework does not replace specific sustainability regulations. The Commission's work does not certify organizational practice; subsequent frameworks (GRI, SASB/ISSB, TCFD) provide more specific guidance.

## Scope note

This article summarizes project-neutral business use of the WCED 1987 Brundtland sustainability framing. It does not assert any specific organization's sustainability conformance or claim any certification outcome.
