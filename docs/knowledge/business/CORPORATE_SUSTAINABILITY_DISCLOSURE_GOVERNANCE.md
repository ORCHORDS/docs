# Corporate Sustainability Disclosure Governance

## Scope

This article covers the umbrella governance discipline of corporate sustainability and climate-related disclosure — the processes by which an organisation identifies, measures, and reports sustainability information to investors and other stakeholders. It draws on the ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 standards issued through the IFRS Foundation, on the Integrated Reporting Framework developed by the Value Reporting Foundation (now consolidated into the IFRS Foundation), and on the earlier work of the IIRC (International Integrated Reporting Council). It addresses disclosure governance rather than environmental marketing claims, which are covered in the marketing collection.

## Workflow

Sustainability disclosure governance under the ISSB standards follows a structured cycle:

1. **Apply IFRS S1 for general requirements.** IFRS S1 requires an entity to disclose material information about its sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the entity's cash flows, access to finance, and cost of capital over the short, medium, or long term. Disclosures cover governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets.
2. **Apply IFRS S2 for climate.** IFRS S2 requires climate-related disclosures across the same four pillars, including current and anticipated financial effects, scenario analysis, transition plans, Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse gas emissions measured in accordance with the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard, and industry-based metrics derived from the SASB Standards.
3. **Identify material sustainability matters.** Materiality is assessed against the objective of providing useful information to investors and creditors, considering both likelihood and magnitude. The ISSB has issued guidance on identifying and assessing material sustainability-related risks and opportunities and on climate-related scenario analysis.
4. **Establish governance and controls.** The body charged with oversight of sustainability reporting, the role of management, and the internal controls over data collection, estimation, and consolidation are documented. Because sustainability data frequently originates outside finance, control environments must be extended to operational systems and suppliers.
5. **Measure and estimate.** Metrics are measured using defined methodologies. Greenhouse gas emissions for Scope 3 in particular require estimation across value-chain categories, with uncertainty and methodological choices disclosed.
6. **Assure and report.** Where required by the applicable regime, disclosures are subject to limited or reasonable assurance by an independent practitioner under standards such as ISAE 3000 (Revised) or ISAE 3410.

## Controls and evidence

Evidence supporting sustainability disclosure governance includes:

- A description of the governance body responsible for oversight of sustainability-related risks and opportunities, including its mandate, composition, and how it is informed.
- A materiality assessment record with methodology, inputs, stakeholder engagement, and the outcome applied to disclosure topics.
- An inventory of metrics with definitions, units, calculation methodologies, data sources, and consolidation boundaries.
- Emissions inventories by scope with the GHG accounting standard applied, emission factors used, and any exclusions with justification.
- Evidence of internal control over sustainability data — reconciliation procedures, system access controls, review sign-offs, and change management for estimation models.
- Climate scenario analysis documentation, including the scenarios selected, inputs, time horizons, and the resulting strategic implications.
- Assurance reports and practitioner findings, including any qualifications.
- Comparatives and restatement policies, with explanations for any changes in methodology.

## Validation

Validation draws on:

- Independent assurance under ISAE 3000 (Revised) or ISAE 3410, at limited or reasonable assurance level depending on the applicable regime.
- Internal audit reviews of the sustainability data pipeline, from source systems to disclosure.
- Regulatory review under mandatory regimes, such as the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) with its European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), which interact with but differ from ISSB requirements.
- Benchmarking against sector peers and against industry-based metrics in the SASB Standards.
- Preparer and investor feedback through consultation processes conducted by the ISSB and national regulators.

## Failure correction

Common disclosure failures and their remedies:

- **Scope 3 data gaps.** Value-chain emissions are commonly estimated from proxy data with wide uncertainty. Corrective action includes a supplier engagement programme, progressive replacement of proxies with primary data, and transparent disclosure of estimation approaches and uncertainty.
- **Greenwashing risk.** Claims in sustainability communications that are not supported by the underlying disclosure create legal and reputational exposure. Corrective action aligns external statements with the assured disclosure, as addressed in the marketing collection's claim substantiation topics.
- **Methodology drift.** Changing calculation approaches without restating comparatives undermines trend analysis. Correction requires documented methodology-change and restatement policies.
- **Fragmented ownership.** Sustainability data spread across functions without a single accountable owner produces inconsistent figures. Corrective action assigns disclosure ownership, defines hand-offs with operations and finance, and embeds sustainability close into the reporting calendar.
- **Assurance findings on data lineage.** Assurance providers commonly report that source data cannot be traced. Corrective action establishes documented data lineage from source systems to reported metrics.

## Limitations

The ISSB standards are focused on the information needs of investors and creditors (single materiality), whereas some regimes — notably the EU's ESRS — apply double materiality, considering impacts on people and the environment as well as financial effects. Adoption is jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction, so an entity's obligations depend on where it is listed or operates. Sustainability metrics, particularly Scope 3 emissions and scenario analysis outputs, are estimates with inherent uncertainty, and comparability across entities remains limited while methodologies mature. The Integrated Reporting Framework's broader multi-capital perspective is not fully carried into the ISSB standards, which focus on sustainability-related financial information.
