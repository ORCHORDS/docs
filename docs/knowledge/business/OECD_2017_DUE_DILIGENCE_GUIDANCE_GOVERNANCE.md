# OECD 2017 Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct Governance

## Purpose

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct (2017) provides practical guidance to enterprises on implementing the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises. The guidance describes a six-step due diligence process (embed responsible business conduct into policies and management systems; identify and assess actual and potential adverse impacts; cease, prevent, or mitigate adverse impacts; track implementation and performance; communicate how impacts are addressed; provide for or cooperate in remediation). This article governs the application of the OECD 2017 guidance so an organization can operationalize due diligence for responsible business conduct.

## Scope

The guidance applies to any enterprise that seeks to align its operations with the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises. Within this knowledge base, the article covers the six-step due diligence process, the contextual elements (severity, leverage, prioritization), the engagement of stakeholders, and the documentation of due diligence activities. It does not replace sector-specific due diligence regulations (e.g., conflict minerals, modern slavery); readers should overlay their sector requirements.

## Workflow

1. Embed responsible business conduct into the organization's policies and management systems. Articulate commitments at the policy level and translate them into operational processes.
2. Identify and assess actual and potential adverse impacts. Consider impacts across the organization's operations, its supply chain, and its business relationships. Prioritize by severity (most severe first), leverage (where the organization has most ability to influence), and the organization's responsibility.
3. Cease, prevent, or mitigate adverse impacts. Where the organization has caused or contributed to the impact, cease or prevent it; where the impact is directly linked to the organization's operations, products, or services by a business relationship, seek to prevent or mitigate the impact even without direct control.
4. Track implementation and performance of the due diligence process. Use qualitative and quantitative indicators appropriate to the context.
5. Communicate how impacts are addressed. Communication should be relevant, accessible, sufficient, and timely.
6. Provide for or cooperate in remediation when the organization has caused or contributed to adverse impacts.

## Controls and evidence

Due diligence evidence includes the policies and management system changes (Step 1), the impact assessment records (Step 2), the response actions and their outcomes (Step 3), the tracking indicators and reports (Step 4), the external communications (Step 5), and the remediation mechanisms and records (Step 6). Each significant relationship should be subject to due diligence proportionate to the risks identified.

## Validation

Validation should confirm the policies are current, impact assessments are performed at appropriate cadence and across the value chain, response actions are documented and executed, tracking produces useful information for improvement, communications reach the relevant stakeholders, and remediation is available. Periodic review confirms the process remains effective as the context evolves.

## Failure correction

Common failure modes: the due diligence process is documented but does not actually identify impacts (corrective: train the assessors and use structured methods); responses are limited to the organization's own operations and ignore the value chain (corrective: extend the scope to the value chain and use the leverage principle); tracking produces no useful information (corrective: define indicators tied to specific impacts and review them at appropriate intervals); communications are one-way (corrective: enable two-way communication channels and respond to concerns); remediation is not accessible to affected stakeholders (corrective: establish remediation mechanisms that are trusted by stakeholders).

## Limitations

The OECD guidance is non-binding and provides a framework; it does not prescribe specific impacts, sectors, or risk thresholds. Due diligence is a continuous practice; the process is iterative, not one-time. The guidance does not replace specific due diligence laws (EU CSDDD, German Supply Chain Act, US conflict minerals regulations); readers should overlay their sector requirements.

## Scope note

This article summarizes project-neutral business use of the OECD 2017 Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct. It does not assert any specific organization's due diligence conformance or claim any certification outcome.
