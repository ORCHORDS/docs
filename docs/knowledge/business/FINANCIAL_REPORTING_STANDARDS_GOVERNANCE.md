# Financial Reporting Standards Governance

## Scope

This article covers the umbrella governance discipline of corporate financial reporting — the preparation and presentation of general purpose financial statements under recognised accounting standards. It draws on the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) and the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) as the primary authorities for, respectively, IFRS Accounting Standards and U.S. GAAP, together with the IFRS Foundation's Conceptual Framework. It addresses the reporting-governance layer rather than valuation methods or financial planning topics in the strategy collection.

## Workflow

Financial reporting governance operates through an annual (and often interim) cycle:

1. **Determine the applicable reporting framework.** Entities listed or regulated in jurisdictions that require IFRS Accounting Standards prepare statements under IFRS as issued by the IASB; entities subject to U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission rules report under U.S. GAAP as codified by the FASB. Some entities reconcile or present under both, for example where a foreign private issuer accesses U.S. capital markets.
2. **Apply recognition, measurement, and presentation rules.** Transactions are recognised and measured under the applicable standards — for example, IFRS 15 on revenue, IFRS 16 on leases, and IAS 36 on impairment, or their U.S. equivalents in the FASB Accounting Standards Codification. Presentation and disclosure requirements determine the structure of the primary statements and the notes.
3. **Exercise judgement and document it.** Estimates such as expected credit losses, fair values, useful lives, and provisions involve judgement. Governance requires that significant judgements be identified, documented, and reviewed by management and, where material, communicated to the audit committee.
4. **Operate internal control over financial reporting.** Controls over transaction capture, period-end close, estimates, and disclosure support the reliability of the statements. Material weaknesses in these controls must be assessed and, in many jurisdictions, disclosed.
5. **Independent audit.** Statements are audited by an independent auditor under the applicable auditing standards (addressed in the audit assurance article in this directory).
6. **Board approval and filing.** The statements are approved by the board of directors or equivalent body and filed with the relevant regulator or registry within statutory deadlines.

## Controls and evidence

Evidence supporting financial reporting governance includes:

- Accounting policies manuals that document the entity's chosen policies and their basis in the applicable standards.
- Technical accounting memos for complex or judgemental transactions, including the standard applied, the analysis, and the conclusion.
- Close calendars and reconciliations covering subledgers to general ledger, accruals, and cut-off.
- Documentation supporting estimates, including models, assumptions, source data, and sensitivity analysis.
- Disclosure checklists mapped to each applicable standard's requirements.
- Audit committee papers and minutes on significant accounting matters, critical judgements, and auditor communications.
- Internal control over financial reporting documentation and test results, including remediation evidence for identified deficiencies.
- Board approval records and filed statements with auditor's reports.

## Validation

Validation draws on:

- The independent auditor's opinion on the financial statements, including any emphasis of matter, key audit matters, or modified opinion.
- Internal audit reviews of the close process, estimates, and ICFR.
- Regulatory review and comment letters, for example from securities regulators reviewing filings.
- Peer review and inspection of the auditor by oversight bodies such as audit-firm inspection programmes.
- IFRS Foundation and FASB pronouncements that clarify, amend, or improve standards; entities must monitor these to keep policies current.

## Failure correction

Common financial reporting failures and their remedies:

- **Misapplied revenue recognition.** Complex or multi-element arrangements are frequently misjudged. Corrective action includes a documented five-step analysis (under IFRS 15 or ASC 606), updated contract templates, and review of performance-obligation judgments by the audit committee.
- **Stale estimates.** Estimates not refreshed for current conditions — for example, impairment indicators — overstate or understate positions. Correction requires a defined estimate-refresh cycle with documented triggers.
- **Inadequate disclosure.** Omissions in the notes, particularly for judgements and risks, are frequent findings. Corrective action includes disclosure checklists owned by a named preparer and reviewed independently of the preparer.
- **Weak ICFR.** Control deficiencies that allow misstatement require root-cause analysis, remediation with owners and dates, and retesting before the deficiency can be closed.
- **Late filings.** Late filings damage credibility and may trigger regulatory action. Correction addresses close-cycle discipline, resource capacity, and the sequencing of audit evidence.

## Limitations

Accounting standards embody trade-offs between relevance, faithful representation, comparability, verifiability, timeliness, and understandability, and no set of standards can satisfy all qualitative characteristics simultaneously. IFRS Accounting Standards and U.S. GAAP differ in specific areas — including impairment, development costs, and inventory measurement — so statements prepared under one are not directly comparable with the other without adjustment. The standards are principle-based (IFRS) or more rules-based (U.S. GAAP) in character, which affects the volume of interpretive guidance available. Financial statements also reflect historical cost and estimates rather than real-time values, and users should not treat them as forecasts.
