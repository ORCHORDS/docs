# Complaint Narrative Consent and Privacy

## Purpose

Complaint programmes often need to separate the information required to resolve an individual complaint from the information that may later be shared publicly for transparency or trend analysis.

The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's current complaint-data process provides a useful model: complaints can be shared with companies and government agencies for response and oversight, but consumer narratives are published only with the consumer's consent and after steps are taken to remove personal information.

## Consent boundary

A reusable complaint process should distinguish at least three decisions:

1. information required to investigate and respond to the complaint;
2. information that may be shared with authorized regulators, service providers, or other parties for the complaint process; and
3. information the customer has affirmatively agreed may be published or used in a public narrative.

Consent to submit a complaint should not automatically be interpreted as consent to publish the customer's story.

## Narrative handling

Before a complaint narrative is made public:

- confirm the customer's publication choice;
- remove or redact information that directly identifies the customer or another person;
- check free-text fields for account numbers, addresses, contact information, or other sensitive details;
- avoid publishing supporting documents merely because the narrative itself is publishable;
- preserve the original complaint record separately from the public version; and
- provide a process for honoring an allowed withdrawal of publication consent without rewriting the underlying complaint history.

## Data minimization

Resolution teams may need personal information that has no public value. Public datasets and analytics exports should therefore use only the fields necessary for the stated purpose and should not expose the raw complaint evidence store.

When complaint data is used for trend analysis, aggregate signals should not be presented in a way that re-identifies individuals through rare combinations of geography, dates, products, or other attributes.

## Scope note

This article summarizes a reusable privacy pattern informed by the CFPB complaint programme. Consent, public-records requirements, retention, deletion rights, and regulatory sharing vary by jurisdiction and context.
