# Quality Management System Governance

## Scope

This article addresses the umbrella governance discipline of a quality management system (QMS), which directs and controls an organisation with regard to quality. It draws on ISO 9001:2015, *Quality management systems — Requirements*, and the ISO 9000:2015 vocabulary and concepts standard. It applies across commercial, customer-success, marketing, partnerships, strategy, and support activities as the system that governs consistent delivery of products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements.

## Workflow

ISO 9001:2015 is structured around the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle and the process approach with risk-based thinking. The workflow is:

1. **Context and interested parties.** The organisation determines the internal and external issues relevant to its purpose and strategic direction, together with the interested parties relevant to the QMS and their requirements. These outputs become inputs to the scope statement.
2. **Leadership and policy.** Top management demonstrates leadership and commitment, establishes a quality policy aligned with the organisation's strategic direction, and assigns roles, responsibilities, and authorities. Customer focus is an explicit leadership responsibility.
3. **Planning.** The organisation sets measurable quality objectives at relevant functions and levels, plans actions to address risks and opportunities, and plans changes to the QMS in a controlled manner.
4. **Support.** Resources, competence, awareness, communication, and documented information necessary for the operation of the QMS are provided. Competence requirements are defined, and evidence of competence is retained as documented information.
5. **Operation.** Operational planning and control cover customer communication, requirements for products and services, design and development (where applicable), control of externally provided processes, production and service provision, release of products and services, and control of nonconforming outputs.
6. **Performance evaluation.** Monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation — including customer satisfaction, internal audit, and management review — determine whether the QMS is effective.
7. **Improvement.** Nonconformities are corrected, causes analysed, and corrective action taken. The standard requires continual improvement of the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the QMS.

## Controls and evidence

Typical QMS documentation and evidence include:

- A quality manual or equivalent description of the QMS scope, processes, and their interactions (larger organisations typically maintain this; smaller ones may rely on leaner documentation as long as required information is available).
- Process maps and documented procedures for operational control, calibration, and traceability.
- Quality objectives with defined metrics, baselines, and review cadence.
- Records of competence, training, and awareness.
- Monitoring and measurement records, including customer-satisfaction data and internal KPIs.
- Nonconformity reports, root-cause analyses, and corrective-action records.
- Internal audit programmes, findings, and follow-up evidence.
- Management review minutes capturing the required inputs and outputs.

## Validation

Validation sources include:

- Certification to ISO 9001 by an accredited certification body, with initial and surveillance audits.
- Internal audits planned on the basis of process importance, changes, and past performance.
- Customer second-party audits and supplier-evaluation programmes.
- Customer satisfaction measurement and complaint analysis (as distinct from the complaint-handling workflows covered in the customer-success collection).
- Regulatory inspections for organisations operating in regulated sectors, such as medical devices, where sector-specific QMS standards such as ISO 13485 apply.

## Failure correction

Quality failures follow recognisable patterns:

- **Recurrence of the same nonconformity** suggests that root-cause analysis was superficial. Corrective action must address the cause of the nonconformity, not only the symptom, and verify the effectiveness of the action taken.
- **Customer satisfaction decline without response** indicates that monitoring outputs are not reaching decision-makers or that objectives are not connected to operational improvement.
- **Audit findings that repeat across cycles** point to weaknesses in the internal audit programme — insufficient auditor independence, narrow sampling, or inadequate follow-up verification.
- **Excessive dependence on inspection** rather than process capability signals a design or process-control weakness that final inspection cannot compensate for.

## Roles and responsibilities

QMS governance distributes accountability across leadership, management, and operational personnel:

- **Top management.** Establishes the quality policy, assigns responsibility and authority, and ensures that the QMS is integrated with strategic direction. Customer focus is an explicit leadership responsibility under ISO 9001 clause 5.
- **Quality manager or function.** Operates the QMS, maintains documentation, coordinates internal audits, and supports process owners in achieving objectives.
- **Process owners.** Accountable for the design, operation, and performance of the processes they own, including the controls that prevent nonconforming outputs.
- **Internal auditors.** Conduct audits impartially and report findings to process owners and management. Independence from the audited activity is required.
- **All employees.** Required to follow documented procedures, identify nonconformities, and suggest improvements, with the awareness and competence needed to do so.

## Limitations

ISO 9001 certifies conformity of the quality management system; it does not certify product quality and is not a mark of product safety or performance. The standard's requirements are generic and apply to organisations of any size and industry, so the depth of documentation varies widely. Certain industries impose additional QMS requirements — for example, IATF 16949 in automotive, AS9100 in aerospace, and ISO 13485 in medical devices — which extend or reinterpret the ISO 9001 baseline. Finally, certification is time-bound and subject to surveillance; a lapse in certification removes external assurance until it is restored.
