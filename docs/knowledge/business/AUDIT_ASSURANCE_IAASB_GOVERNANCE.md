# Audit and Assurance Governance

## Scope

This article covers the umbrella governance discipline of external audit and assurance — the independent examination of information that yields a conclusion designed to enhance the confidence of intended users. It draws on the International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board (IAASB) International Standards on Auditing (ISA) and the International Ethics Standards Board for Accountants (IESBA) Code of Ethics, both operating under the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC). It addresses the governance of audit and assurance engagements rather than internal audit, which is covered in the internal control article in this directory.

## Workflow

An audit conducted under the ISAs proceeds through distinct phases:

1. **Engagement acceptance.** The auditor evaluates the integrity of management, independence requirements, competence, and the ability to comply with ethical and legal requirements before accepting or continuing the engagement. A written engagement letter records the terms.
2. **Risk assessment.** The auditor obtains an understanding of the entity, its environment, and its internal control, and identifies and assesses risks of material misstatement at the financial-statement and assertion levels. Significant risks receive special audit consideration.
3. **Planning and materiality.** The auditor sets materiality for the financial statements as a whole, performance materiality, and a clearly trivial threshold, and plans the nature, timing, and extent of further procedures.
4. **Response to assessed risks.** The auditor designs and performs further audit procedures — controls testing where the auditor intends to rely on controls, and substantive procedures including tests of details and substantive analytical procedures — responsive to the assessed risks.
5. **Evaluation and completion.** The auditor accumulates misstatements, evaluates uncorrected misstatements against materiality, reviews subsequent events, obtains written representations from management, and concludes on the sufficiency and appropriatenence of evidence obtained.
6. **Reporting.** The auditor forms an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework, and issues a report that may be unmodified or modified (qualified, adverse, or disclaimer of opinion). Key audit matters are communicated for listed entities.

For limited assurance and other assurance engagements, the IAASB issues separate standards — for example, the International Standard on Assurance Engagements (ISAE) 3000 (Revised) for assurance engagements on non-historical-financial information and ISAE 3410 for greenhouse gas statements — which require a narrower evidence base than a reasonable-assurance audit.

## Controls and evidence

Governance of audit quality relies on:

- A system of quality management within the audit firm, as required by ISQM 1 (International Standard on Quality Management 1), covering governance and leadership, relevant ethical requirements, acceptance and continuance, engagement performance, resources, and information and communication.
- Engagement-level quality reviews (ISQM 2) for audits of listed entities and other engagements meeting specified criteria.
- Independence documentation, including threats identified, safeguards applied, and confirmations of compliance with the IESBA Code of Ethics, which requires auditors to comply with the fundamental principles of integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional behaviour.
- Audit files that evidence the work performed, the evidence obtained, and the conclusions reached, in sufficient detail for an experienced auditor to understand the significant judgments.
- Communications with those charged with governance, including significant findings, significant difficulties encountered, and uncorrected misstatements.
- Written representations from management acknowledging responsibility for the financial statements and for the completeness of information provided.

## Validation

Validation of audit quality draws on:

- Independent inspection of audit firms by oversight bodies, which examine selected audit files and publish findings on common deficiencies.
- External quality reviews and peer review regimes applicable in various jurisdictions.
- The auditor's own monitoring and remediation programmes under ISQM 1.
- Public reporting by audit regulators and oversight bodies on inspection results and thematic reviews.
- Academic and market evidence on audit quality indicators, such as partner involvement, industry expertise, and tenure, used as supplemental (not determinative) signals.

## Failure correction

Audit and assurance failures commonly present as:

- **Insufficient evidence for significant risks.** Where procedures were not responsive to the assessed risk — for example, relying on inquiry alone for a revenue assertion — corrective action requires redesigning procedures and documenting the linkage between risk and response.
- **Inadequate documentation.** Work performed but not documented cannot support the opinion. Corrective action includes contemporaneous documentation standards and file review before report issuance.
- **Independence threats unmanaged.** Familiarity, self-interest, and advocacy threats require identified safeguards or, where no safeguard reduces the threat to an acceptable level, declining or ending the engagement.
- **Quality-management weaknesses.** Findings under ISQM 1 inspections typically require root-cause analysis, remediation plans, and monitoring of the effectiveness of the remediation.

## Limitations

An audit provides reasonable — not absolute — assurance. It is conducted on a test basis and is subject to the inherent limitations of sampling, management representation, and the possibility of fraud that is sophisticated, collusive, or perpetrated by senior management. The opinion is framed in terms of materiality, so it does not provide assurance that the statements are free of immaterial misstatements. Reasonable assurance and limited assurance engagements differ in depth: a limited assurance conclusion is expressed in negative form and provides a meaningfully lower level of assurance. Cross-border differences also persist, because national auditing standards or adoption timelines may diverge from the IAASB's pronouncements in specific jurisdictions.
