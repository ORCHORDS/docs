# Supplier Social Responsibility Governance

## Scope

This article covers the umbrella governance discipline of managing social responsibility across the supply chain, focusing on the controls an organisation applies to its suppliers and business partners. It draws on ISO 26000:2010, *Guidance on social responsibility*, and ISO 20400:2017, *Sustainable procurement — Guidance*, together with the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct as the intergovernmental baseline. It addresses the management-governance layer; specific procurement and partnership operational practices are covered in the commercial and partnerships collections.

## Workflow

Supply-chain social responsibility operates as a cycle embedded in procurement and contract management:

1. **Define expectations.** The organisation establishes a supplier code of conduct that articulates expectations on human rights, labour practices (including forced labour, child labour, working hours, wages, freedom of association, and non-discrimination), health and safety, the environment, fair operating practices, consumer issues, and community involvement. ISO 26000 identifies seven core subjects and integrates them through the principles of accountability, transparency, ethical behaviour, respect for stakeholder interests, respect for the rule of law, respect for international norms of behaviour, and respect for human rights.
2. **Integrate into procurement.** ISO 20400 expects social and environmental criteria to be incorporated into procurement policy, category strategies, supplier selection, and contract terms rather than treated as a separate compliance exercise. This includes defining sustainability objectives for each category and weighting them in evaluation criteria.
3. **Assess risk.** Risk assessment considers country, sector, commodity, and supplier characteristics to identify where risks of adverse impacts are most likely and most severe. Higher-risk categories — for example, those involving raw materials with documented forced-labour exposure — receive enhanced due diligence.
4. **Due diligence on suppliers.** Organisations assess suppliers through self-assessment questionnaires, third-party audits, certifications, and site visits, prioritised by risk. The OECD Guidelines frame this as risk-based due diligence, meaning the thoroughness of the process is proportionate to the severity and likelihood of the adverse impact.
5. **Contract and remediate. Contracts flow down the code of conduct, require corrective action for violations, and preserve audit and termination rights.** Where violations are found, the organisation works with the supplier on remediation, using disengagement as a last resort where severe harm persists — a sequencing the OECD Guidelines emphasise to avoid simply displacing harm.
6. **Monitor and report.** Ongoing monitoring, re-assessment at defined intervals, escalation for incidents, and public reporting on due diligence close the loop.

## Controls and evidence

Typical evidence supporting supply-chain social responsibility governance includes:

- A published supplier code of conduct aligned to recognised instruments, including the ILO core conventions referenced by ISO 26000.
- A supply-chain map identifying tier-1 suppliers and, for priority commodities, sub-tier exposure.
- A risk-assessment methodology and results, with criteria for country, sector, and supplier risk.
- Supplier self-assessment records and third-party social audit reports with findings, corrective-action plans, and closure evidence.
- Contract clauses incorporating the code of conduct, audit rights, subcontractor disclosure, and remediation obligations.
- A grievance mechanism accessible to workers in the supply chain and to affected communities, with case records and outcomes.
- Training records for procurement staff on sustainable procurement, as recommended by ISO 20400.
- Public reporting on due diligence processes, findings, and remediation outcomes.
- Records of disengagement decisions where remediation failed, including the assessment of consequences for affected workers.

## Validation

Validation draws on:

- Third-party social audits against recognised schemes, noting that audit methodologies have known limitations and should not be the sole source of assurance.
- Independent certification or verification, such as sector-specific schemes where applicable.
- Worker-voice tools and grievance channels that provide signals independent of management-controlled audit paths.
- Civil society and media reporting, which has repeatedly surfaced issues that audits missed.
- National Contact Point proceedings under the OECD Guidelines, which handle complaints about alleged non-observance by multinational enterprises.
- Regulatory due-diligence regimes, such as forced-labour import controls and human-rights due-diligence legislation, which impose binding requirements in certain jurisdictions.
- Internal audit reviews of procurement decision records to confirm that sustainability criteria were applied in award decisions as policy requires.

## Failure correction

Common failures and their remedies:

- **Audit blindness.** Social audits can be defeated by coached workers, falsified records, and unannounced-notice practices. Corrective action includes unannounced audits, worker-voice channels outside the factory floor, and corroboration across sources.
- **Tier-1-only visibility.** Stopping at direct suppliers misses where most severe harm typically occurs. Corrective action maps sub-tier exposure for priority commodities and extends due diligence accordingly.
- **Cut-and-run disengagement.** Immediate termination can worsen conditions for affected workers. OECD-aligned practice prioritises remediation, uses responsible disengagement where harm is severe and unremediated, and documents the decision.
- **Policy–practice gap.** A code of conduct that is not embedded in award criteria is decorative. Correction weights social criteria in tenders, trains evaluators, and audits a sample of award decisions for consistency.
- **Grievance channels without follow-through.** Channels that collect complaints without visible remedy destroy trust. Corrective action includes case management with timelines, feedback to complainants, and trend reporting to governance.

## Limitations

ISO 26000 and ISO 20400 are guidance standards and are not intended for certification; claims of "ISO 26000 certification" misrepresent the standard's intent. The OECD Guidelines are recommendations addressed by governments to enterprises and are implemented through National Contact Points rather than courts, though they increasingly inform binding legislation. Supply-chain visibility is inherently incomplete, sub-tier mapping is resource-intensive, and audit-based assurance has documented blind spots. Social expectations and legal requirements — including import bans tied to forced labour and mandatory human-rights due diligence — continue to evolve, so governance frameworks require periodic refresh.
