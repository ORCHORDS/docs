# Partner Marketing Claim Approval

## Purpose

When a partner, affiliate, agency, reseller, influencer program, or other third party markets a product or service using shared facts or branding, the relationship should define how claims are created, substantiated, approved, monitored, corrected, and retired.

Delegating promotion does not make unsupported or deceptive claims safe. A partner approval process should reduce the risk that outdated facts, missing disclosures, unverified endorsements, or uncontrolled creative changes reach customers.

## FTC basis

Federal Trade Commission business guidance states that advertising claims must be truthful, not deceptive or unfair, and evidence-based. The level of substantiation depends on the claim, and advertisers should have an appropriate reasonable basis before dissemination.

The FTC's Endorsement Guides were revised in 2023. They address endorsements, material connections, influencers, intermediaries, and advertiser responsibilities. The separate Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials became effective on October 21, 2024 and prohibits specified deceptive or unfair review and testimonial practices, including certain fake or false reviews and incentives conditioned on review sentiment.

These sources are U.S.-specific. Other jurisdictions can impose different or additional advertising rules.

## Approval scope

Define which partner-created materials require prior approval. Higher-risk examples can include:

- objective product-performance or comparative claims;
- health, safety, financial, environmental, origin, certification, or compliance claims;
- pricing, discount, savings, or availability statements;
- guarantees or expected-results claims;
- customer reviews or testimonials selected for advertising;
- influencer, affiliate, or employee endorsements;
- claims using another organization's trademarks, logos, security posture, certifications, or audit statements;
- statements about roadmap, future functionality, or availability; and
- translated or localized claims where meaning could materially change.

Routine reuse of pre-approved assets may follow a lighter workflow if the partner cannot alter controlled claims or disclosures.

## Claim package

A request for approval should identify, where relevant:

- exact claim text and surrounding context;
- channel and target audience;
- evidence supporting each objective claim;
- date and scope of the evidence;
- required qualifications or disclosures;
- material connections to endorsers or reviewers;
- images, demonstrations, comparisons, or footnotes that affect the overall message;
- geographic or audience limitations; and
- campaign duration or expiry date.

Review the overall net impression rather than approving isolated sentences that become misleading when combined with visuals, omissions, or surrounding language.

## Substantiation control

Before an objective claim is approved:

1. Identify what a reasonable customer is likely to understand the claim to mean.
2. Identify the evidence needed to support that meaning.
3. Verify that the evidence exists before dissemination.
4. Confirm that the evidence actually covers the advertised product, configuration, population, period, and conditions.
5. Avoid implying a stronger level of proof than the evidence provides.
6. Record material limitations that must accompany the claim.

Customer enthusiasm, internal belief, sales success, or a partner's reputation is not a substitute for appropriate evidence.

## Endorsements and material connections

When partners use endorsements, testimonials, influencers, affiliates, employees, or other intermediaries:

- require truthful representations of the endorser's actual experience or opinion;
- prohibit claims through an endorser that would be deceptive if made directly by the advertiser;
- identify material connections that could affect the weight or credibility consumers give the endorsement;
- require disclosures to be clear and conspicuous where disclosure is needed;
- do not assume a platform's built-in disclosure tool is always sufficient; and
- define monitoring and remediation responsibilities for programs involving multiple endorsers.

The FTC's guidance notes that intermediaries can face liability when they participate in creating or disseminating endorsements they know or should know are deceptive.

## Reviews and testimonials

Partner programs involving reviews or testimonials need a separate check against the FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule where U.S. law applies.

Among other things, avoid arrangements that:

- create, buy, or disseminate fake or false reviews or testimonials in circumstances prohibited by the rule;
- condition compensation or incentives on a consumer review expressing a particular positive or negative sentiment;
- conceal insider relationships where disclosure is required; or
- misrepresent independent review sites or suppress reviews through prohibited threats or intimidation.

Do not treat the Endorsement Guides and the 2024 rule as interchangeable. The Guides provide FTC interpretations and examples concerning endorsements; the rule creates specific legally enforceable prohibitions.

## Change and expiry control

Approved claims should not remain valid indefinitely. Define triggers for review or withdrawal, such as:

- product specifications change;
- price or promotion terms change;
- evidence expires or is superseded;
- an endorsement no longer reflects the endorser's experience;
- certification or approval status changes;
- availability changes;
- a regulator or court changes the relevant legal interpretation; or
- monitoring shows consumers are likely to take away a misleading message.

Use expiry dates or controlled asset libraries for claims that are likely to become stale.

## Monitoring

Risk-based monitoring can include:

- sampling partner websites, ads, social posts, email campaigns, marketplace listings, and influencer content;
- checking that required disclosures remain visible in the actual published format;
- detecting unauthorized edits to controlled claims;
- checking high-risk affiliates or intermediaries more frequently;
- reviewing complaints or customer confusion that indicate misleading promotion; and
- confirming withdrawn claims have actually been removed from active channels.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the program; it should not be represented as a guarantee that every partner statement has been reviewed.

## Correction workflow

When a problematic claim is found:

1. Preserve the published material and relevant evidence.
2. Assess customer impact, legal risk, and distribution scope.
3. Pause or remove the claim when continued publication creates material risk.
4. Notify the responsible partner owner and claim approver.
5. Correct required disclosures, wording, evidence, or targeting.
6. Determine whether customer, marketplace, platform, or regulator communication is required.
7. Check materially similar assets or campaigns for the same defect.
8. Record root cause and any control change needed to prevent recurrence.

## Evidence to retain

For material claims, retain where appropriate:

- approved creative or exact claim text;
- supporting evidence and its date;
- approver and approval date;
- disclosures and placement requirements;
- permitted channels and audience;
- expiry or review date;
- monitoring results;
- correction or withdrawal records; and
- partner acknowledgement of material restrictions.

## Scope note

This article describes reusable partner marketing-governance practices. It is not legal advice and does not establish that a particular claim, disclosure, endorsement, review program, or campaign complies with any jurisdiction's law.
