# Collaborative Value Creation Governance

## Purpose

A collaborative relationship should exist because the parties expect to create value that would be difficult, slower, more expensive, or riskier to achieve independently. ISO describes value creation as one stage in the eight-stage life cycle used by ISO 44001 for disciplined collaborative business relationship management.

Value should therefore be made explicit before a relationship becomes dependent on vague expectations. A reusable governance process defines the value hypothesis, contributions, measures, allocation principles, risks, and conditions under which the relationship should be changed or exited.

## Current standards context

ISO 44001:2017 remains the current published international standard for collaborative business relationship management. ISO shows a replacement edition, ISO/DIS 44001, under development; the draft should not be represented as a final published replacement.

ISO's public material describes the ISO 44001 life cycle as including operational awareness, value creation, knowledge, internal assessment, partner selection, working together, staying together, and exit strategies. ISO 44002:2019 provides implementation guidance for that life-cycle approach.

This article uses that public standards context as a management framework; it does not reproduce or claim conformity with the standards.

## Define the value hypothesis

Before making major commitments, state why collaboration is expected to create value. The value hypothesis should be specific enough to test later.

Potential value can include:

- access to a market, customer segment, capability, asset, or distribution channel;
- faster or lower-risk delivery;
- reduced duplicated cost;
- improved resilience or capacity;
- stronger service coverage;
- shared innovation or research;
- better use of complementary expertise; or
- other measurable strategic outcomes.

Avoid using "strategic partnership" as the value proposition. The relationship label does not explain what the parties expect to achieve.

## Contributions and dependencies

Map what each party must contribute for the value hypothesis to work. Depending on the relationship, contributions can include:

- personnel and specialist expertise;
- intellectual property or licensed rights;
- technology, facilities, equipment, or data;
- market access and customer relationships;
- financing or commercial commitments;
- operational capacity;
- regulatory or sector knowledge; and
- management attention.

Identify dependencies between contributions. A contribution that has no practical substitute can create concentration or bargaining risk even when its direct financial cost is small.

## Separate value from revenue

Collaborative value is broader than gross revenue. A relationship can increase revenue while destroying value through excessive implementation cost, support burden, risk, working-capital requirements, or opportunity cost.

For material relationships, consider:

- incremental revenue or savings;
- cost to acquire and operate the relationship;
- investment required from each party;
- time to expected benefit;
- capacity consumed;
- risk created or reduced;
- strategic options created; and
- exit or switching cost.

Do not treat projected benefits as guaranteed outcomes.

## Value allocation

Where value is jointly created, define how economic and non-economic benefits are expected to be allocated. Allocation can occur through pricing, revenue sharing, licensing, cost sharing, exclusivity, service commitments, access rights, or other contractual arrangements.

A governance review should ask:

- whether allocation reflects the actual contributions and risks;
- whether one party bears costs before receiving benefits;
- whether incentives encourage behavior that harms joint outcomes;
- whether changes in scope alter the original allocation logic; and
- whether the arrangement creates unresolved disputes over ownership or credit.

The governance process should identify allocation issues for commercial or legal resolution rather than improvising them after value has already been created.

## Joint value register

For important collaborations, maintain a lightweight value register containing:

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Value hypothesis | What the collaboration is expected to improve |
| Baseline | What would likely happen without the collaboration |
| Contributions | What each party commits |
| Measures | Evidence used to evaluate progress |
| Owner | Who maintains each measure or workstream |
| Dependencies | Conditions required for the benefit to occur |
| Allocation | How benefits/costs are shared or recognized |
| Review date | When the hypothesis is reassessed |
| Decision threshold | When to expand, correct, renegotiate, or exit |

The register is a decision aid, not an accounting statement.

## Measurement discipline

Use measures that reflect the value hypothesis rather than generic partnership activity. Meeting counts, messages exchanged, or joint announcements show activity but do not necessarily show value.

Useful measures can include:

- incremental qualified demand;
- cycle-time reduction;
- service availability or coverage improvements;
- validated cost reduction;
- customer outcomes;
- delivery capacity;
- risk reduction supported by evidence;
- reusable intellectual assets created; or
- agreed innovation milestones.

Preserve the baseline and assumptions used to calculate material benefits so later reviews do not compare incompatible definitions.

## Value leakage

Value leakage occurs when the expected joint benefit is reduced by friction, duplicated work, rework, unclear rights, delays, misaligned incentives, or unplanned cost.

Review recurring leakage such as:

- unresolved ownership or approval boundaries;
- repeated handoff failure;
- duplicated tooling or reporting;
- excessive coordination overhead;
- delayed data or information access;
- incentives that optimize one party at the other's expense;
- customer confusion over responsibility; and
- scope expansion without corresponding funding or capacity.

A relationship can appear healthy socially while value leakage makes it commercially unsustainable.

## Risk-adjusted value

Value decisions should include risk. Consider whether expected benefit depends on:

- a single supplier, channel, customer, or platform;
- unproven technology;
- sensitive data access;
- uncertain intellectual-property rights;
- regulatory approval;
- geographic or geopolitical exposure;
- financial stability of a participant; or
- a dependency that is expensive to reverse.

Where a material risk is accepted, record the rationale and mitigation owner rather than silently inflating the value estimate.

## Review cycle

Review collaborative value at defined milestones and when material circumstances change.

A useful review asks:

1. Is the original value hypothesis still relevant?
2. Are required contributions being delivered?
3. Are benefits appearing at the expected time and scale?
4. Has cost or risk changed materially?
5. Is benefit allocation still workable?
6. Is value leakage recurring?
7. Should the relationship expand, stay stable, be corrected, be renegotiated, or exit?

Avoid continuing a relationship solely because significant effort has already been invested.

## Change governance

When the relationship changes, update the value model. Trigger review after events such as:

- new products or services;
- material pricing changes;
- new geographic scope;
- acquisitions or ownership changes;
- new data or technology access;
- major regulatory change;
- a new subcontractor or dependency; or
- sustained underperformance.

A scope change without a value review can convert a viable collaboration into an unmanaged obligation.

## Exit and residual value

An orderly exit should consider value that survives the relationship, including reusable knowledge, jointly created materials, customer obligations, licenses, data, and intellectual property.

Before exit, identify:

- which benefits or capabilities can continue independently;
- which customer commitments require transition;
- which shared assets must be returned, transferred, licensed, or retired;
- which access must end; and
- which lessons should inform future collaborations.

The purpose is not to preserve a relationship at all costs; it is to preserve legitimate value while ending dependencies safely.
