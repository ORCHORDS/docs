# Partner Due Diligence Scope

## Purpose

Partner due diligence should establish enough evidence about a prospective or existing partner to make an informed relationship decision before access, dependency, data exchange, or customer impact becomes difficult to reverse.

The depth of review should be risk-based. A low-impact partner should not automatically receive the same assessment burden as a critical technology supplier, but material access or dependency should not be accepted solely because a standard questionnaire was completed.

## Current NIST basis

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 includes supply-chain outcomes requiring planning and due diligence before formal supplier or third-party relationships and continued understanding and monitoring of supplier risk over the relationship.

NIST SP 1326, finalized in July 2026, provides a dedicated Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management Due Diligence Assessment Quick-Start Guide. It is aligned with NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 and is scoped specifically to ICT suppliers. Its assessment components are:

- foreign ownership, control, or influence (FOCI);
- provenance;
- resilience;
- foundational cybersecurity practices; and
- supply-chain tiers.

These components are useful reference points for ICT supplier assessment, but they do not mean every business partnership requires the same investigation or that a completed assessment removes residual risk.

## Scoping the review

Before requesting evidence, classify the relationship by factors such as:

- access to systems, networks, source code, credentials, or facilities;
- access to customer, personal, confidential, or regulated information;
- operational criticality and difficulty of replacement;
- ability to affect product or service integrity;
- subcontractor or lower-tier dependency;
- geographic, ownership, or jurisdictional exposure where relevant;
- financial and operational resilience;
- customer-facing authority or brand representation; and
- concentration risk created by the relationship.

Use the classification to determine the depth, evidence, reviewer expertise, and refresh frequency required.

## Due-diligence workflow

1. Define the purpose, scope, and criticality of the proposed relationship.
2. Identify the risks the partner could introduce rather than starting with a generic evidence list.
3. Gather independently verifiable information where practical, not only self-attestations.
4. Review ownership, provenance, resilience, cybersecurity practices, and supply-chain dependencies when relevant to the relationship.
5. Review financial, legal, operational, privacy, compliance, customer, and continuity considerations where they are material to the partnership.
6. Distinguish confirmed evidence from claims that remain unverified.
7. Record gaps, exceptions, compensating controls, and residual risk.
8. Assign an accountable decision owner for acceptance, remediation, rejection, or restricted onboarding.
9. Translate material requirements into contracts, access design, monitoring, or operating procedures rather than leaving them only in the assessment record.
10. Define refresh triggers before approval.

## Evidence quality

Evidence should be proportionate to the decision. Depending on scope, useful sources may include:

- public regulatory or corporate records;
- independently issued certifications or audit reports, when relevant and verified;
- architecture, security, privacy, continuity, or incident-management documentation;
- product provenance or software-development information;
- contractual commitments;
- references or operating history;
- financial or insurance evidence where appropriate; and
- direct technical or operational validation.

Do not treat a certification badge, questionnaire answer, marketing statement, or historical audit report as proof of current implementation beyond its documented scope and date.

## Lower-tier and concentration risk

A direct partner may depend on other suppliers that materially affect the service. When criticality warrants it, understand important lower-tier dependencies, single points of failure, geographic concentration, and whether substitutions are realistically available.

The objective is not to enumerate every supplier indefinitely. It is to identify dependencies capable of changing the risk decision.

## Refresh triggers

Reassess due diligence after events such as:

- ownership or control changes;
- acquisitions or major financial distress;
- material security incidents;
- significant service or architecture changes;
- new sensitive-data access;
- new critical subcontractors;
- expansion into new jurisdictions;
- material adverse regulatory findings; or
- renewal of a high-criticality relationship.

Risk-based periodic review may also be appropriate when no trigger occurs.

## Decision record

For a material relationship, retain where appropriate:

- relationship classification and rationale;
- evidence reviewed and its date;
- unresolved findings;
- risk owner and decision;
- required remediation or contractual conditions;
- monitoring and reassessment triggers; and
- restrictions imposed on access, data, scope, or subcontracting.

## Scope note

NIST SP 1326 is focused on ICT suppliers. This article generalizes the risk-based due-diligence discipline to business partnerships while preserving that scope distinction. It does not provide legal advice, sanctions screening, investment advice, or assurance that any particular partner is suitable.
