# Collaboration Agreement Scope Selection

## Purpose

A collaboration should use an agreement structure that matches what the parties actually need to exchange or create. An overly broad agreement can grant unnecessary rights; an overly narrow one can leave data, materials, software, results, funding, or intellectual property without clear treatment.

## Match the agreement to the exchange

NIST's Technology Partnerships Office illustrates several distinct agreement purposes in its own collaboration framework:

- a non-disclosure agreement for confidential information;
- a material transfer agreement for research materials;
- a software transfer agreement for software exchange;
- a data transfer agreement for research data;
- a research collaboration agreement for two-way collaborative research; and
- a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement for a more formal co-development relationship under federal authority.

These NIST instruments are agency-specific examples, not universal contract forms. The reusable lesson is to identify the actual exchange and collaboration model before selecting or drafting the agreement.

## Scoping workflow

1. List what each party will provide: information, data, software, materials, equipment, personnel, facilities, funds, services, or other resources.
2. Identify what the collaboration is expected to create: results, inventions, software, datasets, reports, prototypes, know-how, or other outputs.
3. Separate one-way transfer from two-way collaboration. A transfer agreement and a joint-development agreement solve different problems.
4. Define permitted purpose, recipients, access, handling, security, retention, return or deletion, and onward transfer for exchanged assets.
5. Address background and foreground intellectual property explicitly where creation or improvement is expected.
6. Define publication, disclosure, confidentiality, and review rights before work begins when research or public communications are possible.
7. State whether funding, in-kind contributions, facilities, equipment, or personnel are part of the arrangement and who bears related costs and risks.
8. Define duration, milestones, change control, termination, transition, surviving obligations, and ownership or disposition at exit.
9. Review whether several narrow agreements are clearer than one omnibus document, or whether a single integrated agreement is needed to avoid conflicting terms.
10. Obtain appropriate legal review for the applicable jurisdiction, sector, and transaction.

## Avoid agreement-name assumptions

The same label can mean different things across organizations and jurisdictions. Do not infer legal effect solely from names such as "NDA," "MTA," "research agreement," or "collaboration agreement." Review the actual rights and obligations in the document.

## Scope note

NIST's CRADA and other federal agreement authorities are specific to NIST and U.S. federal law. This article uses them to illustrate agreement scoping and does not suggest that private organizations can or should use those statutory instruments.
