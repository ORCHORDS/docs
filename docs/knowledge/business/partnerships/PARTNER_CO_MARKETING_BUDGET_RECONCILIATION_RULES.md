# Partner Co-Marketing Budget Reconciliation Rules

## Scope

This article governs the financial-control discipline applied to a co-marketing budget shared between two partner organizations. It addresses how the budget is set, how joint commitments are authorized, how spend is recorded against each party's contribution, how variances are investigated, and how the reconciliation is closed. The discipline is required because co-marketing programs typically combine two pools of money, two approval chains, two procurement rules, and two audit regimes, and the absence of an explicit reconciliation process is a recurring source of dispute.

The article draws on the financial-control posture of ISO 37001:2016 — Anti-bribery management systems — Requirements with guidance for use, focusing on the discipline that anti-bribery financial controls impose on joint programs: segregation of duties, authorization limits, transparent recording, traceability of decisions, and the existence of independent review. Co-marketing budgets are not inherently bribery-exposed, but they share the same risk pattern: money moves between organizations under joint authority, and inadequate financial control creates exposure for both parties.

The scope is the financial reconciliation of the co-marketing budget. It does not address the marketing claim-substantiation discipline, the procurement rules that govern how each party sources its own services, the tax treatment of joint spend, or the legal structure of the joint program. Those are governed separately. The reconciliation also does not replace each party's own internal financial controls.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

1. Establish a written budget charter for the co-marketing program, identifying the funding sources from each party, the total envelope, the period, the named financial owner on each side, the authorization matrix, and the closing procedure.
2. Distinguish the categories of joint spend that will be reconciled. Typical categories include shared creative production, paid media placed jointly, joint events, partner enablement and training, joint content development, technology or platform fees that support the joint program, and any third-party services procured specifically for the co-marketing work. Define which categories fall inside the reconciliation and which each party funds separately.
3. Define the authorization rules for each category. State the dollar threshold above which a joint commitment must be jointly authorized, who can authorize, how authorization is recorded, and what supporting evidence is required. Reuse each party's own authorization matrix rather than inventing a parallel one.
4. Record commitments at the point of decision, not at the point of payment. A commitment made verbally in a joint meeting should be recorded in writing with an owner and a target, before the underlying purchase order or contract is signed.
5. Operate a single reconciliation ledger or a paired set of ledgers that agree at the end of each period. Each line should carry the joint program identifier, the category, the authorized owner, the approved amount, the actual spend, the variance, the supporting invoice reference, the tax treatment, and the currency.
6. Reconcile on a defined cadence — typically monthly for active programs, quarterly for lower-velocity programs. The reconciliation should be a signed statement that the two ledgers agree, with any differences identified, attributed, and resolved.
7. Investigate variances above a stated threshold before the reconciliation closes. Variance investigation should ask whether the spend was authorized, whether the spend was for the intended purpose, whether the invoice matches the agreed scope, and whether the supporting evidence supports the amount.
8. Close the period with a sign-off from the named financial owner on each side. The sign-off is an assertion that the reconciliation has been performed, variances have been investigated, and any unresolved items have been escalated or reserved.
9. Retain supporting evidence for the period required by each party's own record-retention rules and by the joint-program agreement. Evidence retention is the foundation of audit defense; lost evidence is treated as a control failure.
10. Treat the joint budget as a financial-control surface. Anti-bribery, sanctions, and export-control screening applies to the vendors and beneficiaries that receive joint spend, and the screening should be recorded as part of the commitment record.

## Controls

The reconciliation process relies on controls familiar from financial-control frameworks. Segregation of duties keeps authorization, recording, and review in different hands. Authorization limits prevent unilateral commitment of large sums. Independent review of variances prevents self-justified close-out. Evidence retention keeps the supporting documents accessible for the audit window. Restricted-access controls protect the budget data from unauthorized modification. Periodic surprise checks, where the program is material, test whether the ledger agrees with the underlying invoices and the underlying reality.

## Validation evidence

Validation evidence includes the budget charter, the joint authorization records, the joint commitment register, the periodic reconciliation statements signed by both parties, the variance investigation notes, the supporting invoice and contract references, the screen records for vendors and beneficiaries, the sign-off, and any independent audit findings. Evidence should be drawn from both parties' financial systems where the reconciliation spans them, and any discrepancies between the parties' ledgers should be traceable to a recorded resolution.

## Failure modes and correction

Common failure modes include the budget being set but never reconciled, commitments made by individuals without authority to bind the joint program, invoices paid by one party without a corresponding record on the other, variances ignored, evidence stored only with the party that paid, currency or tax treatment handled inconsistently, joint spend used to benefit one party disproportionately, and vendors screened only after payment. A particularly serious failure is the joint program being used as a vehicle for undisclosed benefit, whether to individuals or to related parties; this is the pattern that ISO 37001's financial controls are designed to detect.

Correction requires identifying the unresolved amounts, recovering evidence, and restating the reconciliation. Where unauthorized commitments have been made, the parties must decide whether to ratify the spend, recover it, or treat it as a contractual issue between the parties. Where the failure pattern is repeated, broader financial-control review is warranted, and the joint program may need to be suspended pending strengthening of the financial-control posture.

## Limitations

This article is a financial-control discipline for co-marketing programs. It is not a complete anti-bribery management system, not a procurement procedure, not a tax treatment, and not a substitute for any party's own internal financial controls. ISO 37001 is a requirement standard for anti-bribery management systems; this article borrows the financial-control posture but does not claim certification, conformity, or compliance.
