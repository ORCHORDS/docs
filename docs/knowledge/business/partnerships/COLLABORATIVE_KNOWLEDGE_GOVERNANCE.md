# Collaborative Knowledge Governance

## Purpose

Collaborative relationships depend on knowledge that crosses organizational boundaries: requirements, customer context, technical constraints, decisions, lessons, capabilities, and operating information. ISO describes "knowledge" as one stage in the eight-stage life cycle used by ISO 44001 for collaborative business relationship management.

A knowledge-governance process should make useful information available to the collaboration without assuming that every participating organization may access every record. The objective is controlled sharing, traceability, continuity, and learning.

## Current standards context

ISO 44001:2017 remains the current published international standard for collaborative business relationship management, while ISO/DIS 44001 is under development as a replacement. ISO 44002:2019 remains the published implementation-guidance edition while a second edition is also under development.

ISO's public materials describe the collaborative life cycle as including operational awareness, value creation, knowledge, internal assessment, partner selection, working together, staying together, and exit strategies.

This article uses that public life-cycle framing without reproducing copyrighted requirements or claiming conformity.

## Define collaboration knowledge domains

At the start of an important relationship, identify what information the parties expect to create, exchange, or rely on. Common domains include:

- objectives and scope;
- customer or stakeholder requirements;
- technical specifications and interfaces;
- operating procedures;
- risk and issue information;
- project decisions and rationale;
- performance measures;
- jointly created intellectual assets;
- lessons and improvement actions; and
- exit or transition information.

Knowing the domains early helps prevent both oversharing and later disputes about missing information.

## Classify before sharing

Apply each organization's information-classification and legal requirements before placing material into a shared workspace.

For each material information type, decide:

- whether it may be shared;
- with which partner roles;
- for what purpose;
- through which approved channel;
- whether onward sharing is allowed;
- how long it should be retained; and
- what happens to it when the relationship ends.

A partnership agreement does not automatically make all internal information shareable.

## Source and provenance

Collaborative decisions can fail when participants cannot tell whether information is authoritative, current, or merely a working assumption.

For important records, retain enough provenance to identify:

- originating organization or source;
- responsible owner;
- creation or effective date;
- approval or validation state;
- applicable version;
- known limitations; and
- superseding information.

Generated summaries and working notes should not silently replace approved source documents.

## Shared source of truth

Where feasible, define an authoritative location for each class of joint information rather than copying it into several uncontrolled systems.

A source-of-truth model should identify:

- the record system;
- who may update it;
- who approves changes;
- how partners receive change notifications; and
- what offline copies are permitted.

Do not call a workspace the "single source of truth" if participants routinely make decisions from untracked email attachments or local spreadsheets.

## Access governance

Shared knowledge repositories should follow least-privilege principles. Access decisions can account for:

- organization;
- role;
- project or workstream;
- information sensitivity;
- contractual need; and
- relationship phase.

Review access after staffing changes, scope changes, partner reorganizations, and transition events. Guest accounts and external identities should not remain indefinitely merely because a project once required them.

## Knowledge capture

Not all useful collaboration knowledge exists as formal documents. Important decisions can occur in meetings, support channels, design reviews, or incident calls.

Define which events require durable capture. Examples include:

- material decisions and rationale;
- accepted risks;
- scope changes;
- customer-impacting commitments;
- interface changes;
- ownership changes;
- lessons from incidents; and
- agreed exceptions.

Avoid recording every conversation. Capture what later participants need to understand obligations and decisions.

## Decision records

For high-impact joint decisions, a lightweight record can include:

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Decision | What was agreed |
| Context | Why a decision was needed |
| Options | Alternatives considered |
| Evidence | Information used |
| Participants | Responsible decision-makers |
| Date | When the decision became effective |
| Dependencies | Conditions the decision relies on |
| Review trigger | When it should be reconsidered |

The record should remain understandable even after original participants leave.

## Knowledge quality

Before relying on shared information, consider whether it is:

- relevant to the current decision;
- complete enough for its intended use;
- current;
- attributable;
- consistent with authoritative records; and
- expressed with uncertainty where evidence is incomplete.

A large shared repository is not valuable if participants cannot distinguish current material from obsolete drafts.

## Sensitive and restricted knowledge

Some information should not enter a general collaboration repository, including credentials, unnecessary personal data, privileged legal advice, regulated records, security-sensitive implementation details, or data outside the agreed purpose.

Use controlled systems appropriate to the information type. When only a conclusion can be shared, communicate the minimum result rather than copying the underlying restricted evidence.

## Intellectual property boundary

Knowledge sharing and intellectual-property ownership are related but different issues. Sharing information for collaboration does not necessarily transfer ownership or grant unrestricted reuse rights.

Define treatment of:

- pre-existing background knowledge;
- jointly created materials;
- inventions or authored works;
- confidential know-how;
- improvements;
- licensing and reuse; and
- post-exit access.

Route ownership questions to the relationship's agreed legal and IP governance rather than encoding assumptions into repository permissions.

## Continuity and personnel changes

A collaborative relationship should not depend entirely on individual memory. When key personnel change:

1. review current obligations and open decisions;
2. transfer authoritative records;
3. update owners and access;
4. identify undocumented dependencies; and
5. confirm the successor can find the information needed to continue work.

Use structured handovers for critical roles rather than relying solely on informal introductions.

## Lessons learned

Capture lessons when they can improve future joint work. Useful lessons identify:

- what happened;
- the conditions that contributed;
- what was learned;
- whether the lesson is specific to this relationship or reusable; and
- which process or decision should change.

Avoid turning lessons repositories into blame records. The goal is organizational learning supported by evidence.

## Change and obsolescence

Collaborative knowledge can become dangerous when it is stale. Define review or retirement triggers for information affected by:

- product changes;
- new regulations;
- revised interfaces;
- contract amendments;
- partner ownership changes;
- security incidents;
- organizational restructuring; or
- termination of a workstream.

Mark superseded material clearly instead of leaving conflicting versions equally discoverable.

## Exit and knowledge disposition

Before the relationship ends, identify what knowledge must be:

- transferred to support customer continuity;
- retained for legal or contractual reasons;
- returned to its owner;
- deleted;
- archived;
- converted to a continuing license; or
- preserved as a reusable lesson without confidential content.

Remove obsolete shared access and verify disposition of sensitive material where the agreement requires it.

## Review questions

A periodic knowledge-governance review can ask:

1. Can participants find the current authoritative information?
2. Are important decisions documented?
3. Are access rights still appropriate?
4. Are restricted data types appearing in general shared systems?
5. Are outdated documents influencing current work?
6. Are key-person dependencies excessive?
7. Are jointly created materials governed consistently with IP agreements?
8. Is exit disposition defined for the information currently being shared?
