# Partner Roles and Responsibilities Charter

## Purpose

A partnership charter should make responsibility, authority, handoffs, and escalation explicit before work crosses organizational boundaries. Ambiguous ownership creates duplicated effort, missed obligations, delayed incident response, and gaps where each party assumes the other is responsible.

The charter should complement—not replace—the contract, security requirements, operating procedures, and internal accountability model.

## NIST governance basis

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 includes a dedicated Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities category (`GV.RR`). It calls for cybersecurity roles, responsibilities, and authorities to be established, communicated, understood, and enforced. Its supply-chain category (`GV.SC`) also addresses supplier requirements, due diligence, monitoring, incident participation, and post-relationship activities.

These outcomes support a practical rule for partnerships: responsibilities that depend on another organization should be made explicit enough that both parties can execute and verify them.

## Charter structure

For each material responsibility, identify where appropriate:

- **accountable owner** — the person or role answerable for the outcome;
- **responsible operator** — the person or team expected to perform the work;
- **decision authority** — who can approve, reject, pause, or materially change the activity;
- **consulted parties** — roles whose input is required before the decision;
- **informed parties** — roles that must receive the result or status;
- **partner counterpart** — the corresponding external role when the responsibility crosses the boundary; and
- **escalation owner** — who takes control when the normal path fails or a material risk emerges.

Do not assume a shared responsibility means equal responsibility. State which party performs which action and what evidence demonstrates completion.

## Areas to define

The charter should cover the areas relevant to the relationship, such as:

- service delivery and service-level decisions;
- technical integration and change control;
- security and vulnerability coordination;
- identity, credential, and access administration;
- data collection, use, transfer, retention, return, and deletion;
- privacy or regulatory handoffs;
- customer support and complaint escalation;
- incident notification, response, and recovery;
- business continuity and dependency management;
- subcontractor or lower-tier supplier governance;
- financial and commercial approvals;
- public communications and marketing claims;
- intellectual-property and deliverable approvals; and
- transition, termination, and post-exit obligations.

## Retained responsibilities

Outsourcing an activity does not automatically transfer every associated responsibility. Explicitly identify obligations that remain with each organization even when the partner performs operational work.

Examples include internal risk acceptance, approval of privileged access, customer commitments, regulatory reporting, records retention, or final authorization to release a product or service. The exact retained obligations depend on the relationship and applicable requirements.

## Handoff design

For a cross-organizational handoff, define:

1. the trigger;
2. the information or evidence that must accompany the handoff;
3. the receiving role;
4. expected acknowledgement or response time;
5. decision authority if information is incomplete;
6. escalation path when the handoff is rejected or delayed; and
7. completion evidence.

Where a handoff affects customers, security, safety, compliance, or availability, avoid designs that depend on an unnamed mailbox or a single individual without backup coverage.

## Decision rights

Separate operational execution from approval authority. A partner may be authorized to perform routine actions without having authority to:

- change contractual scope;
- accept material security or compliance risk;
- disclose confidential information;
- make public claims on behalf of another party;
- approve exceptional spending; or
- waive customer or regulatory commitments.

Document exception authority and escalation rather than relying on implied permission.

## Change management

Review the charter when the relationship materially changes, including:

- new products or services;
- expanded system or data access;
- new subcontractors;
- organizational restructuring;
- ownership changes;
- significant incidents;
- regulatory or contractual changes; or
- renewal, downsizing, or exit.

Retire obsolete contacts and responsibilities promptly so operational teams do not follow an outdated ownership map.

## Evidence to retain

For material partnerships, retain where appropriate:

- approved charter version and effective date;
- named roles or functional owners;
- decision-right assignments;
- escalation contacts and backups;
- material handoff definitions;
- review history;
- unresolved ownership conflicts; and
- evidence that affected teams received the current version.

## Scope note

This article describes reusable responsibility and governance design. It does not modify contractual responsibility, legal liability, regulatory accountability, or statutory duties, and it does not assert that a documented assignment alone proves effective implementation.
