# Quality Plan Governance for Specific Engagements

## Purpose

A quality plan defines how quality will be governed for a particular engagement, such as a project, contract, product delivery, managed service, migration, or significant change. It converts broad quality expectations into engagement-specific responsibilities, controls, acceptance criteria, evidence, and change procedures.

A quality plan adds value when ordinary operating procedures are not sufficiently specific to the engagement. Common triggers include:

- Material contractual, regulatory, safety, security, or reputational exposure.
- Multiple organizations sharing delivery responsibilities.
- Customer-specific acceptance criteria or evidence obligations.
- A new service, technology, supplier, geography, or delivery model.
- Complex dependencies, handoffs, integrations, or subcontracting.
- Staged delivery with formal gates or conditional acceptance.
- A history of quality escapes, disputes, or unclear accountability.
- A requirement to tailor an established quality management system to a defined scope.

The plan should remain proportionate. A low-risk, repeatable engagement may need only a short set of engagement-specific controls. A high-risk or novel engagement may require a detailed plan with formal review and approval gates.

A quality plan is not a substitute for a quality management system. The management system provides reusable policies, processes, competencies, and controls; the quality plan identifies which of them apply, how they are tailored, and what additional controls the engagement needs. It is also distinct from a customer success plan, which normally emphasizes adoption, value realization, relationships, and outcomes rather than conformity, verification, acceptance evidence, and control of change.

## Current context and source status

ISO 10005:2018, *Quality management — Guidelines for quality plans*, is Edition 3 and was published in June 2018. The ISO public catalogue records it as an International Standard at stage 90.93, confirmed following a systematic review that closed on September 29, 2023. It therefore remains current as of the source status reviewed for this article.

At a high level, the standard provides guidance for establishing, reviewing, accepting, applying, and revising quality plans for a process, product, service, project, or contract. Its application is broad and is not limited to a particular industry or organization size. ISO 10005 is guidance and is not, by itself, a certification requirement.

This article does not reproduce ISO clauses or normative text. It provides an independent governance pattern informed only by the public catalogue status and high-level scope.

## Practical workflow and controls

### 1. Decide whether a plan is warranted

The engagement owner should perform an early, documented assessment. Consider delivery novelty, contractual complexity, customer requirements, applicable obligations, dependency count, severity of possible failure, and the maturity of existing controls.

Record one of three outcomes:

1. Existing controls are sufficient; no separate quality plan is needed.
2. A concise quality plan is required to document limited tailoring.
3. A comprehensive quality plan with formal gates and independent review is required.

The decision and its rationale should be reviewable. "No plan required" should not mean that quality responsibilities or acceptance criteria are undefined.

### 2. Define scope and interfaces

State what the plan governs and what it excludes. Identify:

- The engagement, deliverables, services, locations, and lifecycle stages covered.
- Contractual documents and approved statements of work.
- Applicable internal policies and operating procedures.
- Customer, supplier, partner, and subcontractor interfaces.
- Inputs supplied by other parties and assumptions about those inputs.
- Dependencies on platforms, data, facilities, approvals, or third parties.
- Any precedence rules when referenced documents conflict.

Avoid copying entire corporate procedures into the plan. Reference controlled sources and document only engagement-specific selections, additions, or deviations.

### 3. Assign accountable roles

Name roles rather than relying solely on personal names. At minimum, distinguish:

- **Engagement owner:** accountable for delivery and plan implementation.
- **Quality owner:** maintains the plan and monitors conformity.
- **Control owners:** execute defined reviews, tests, inspections, or approvals.
- **Evidence custodians:** retain records and preserve traceability.
- **Acceptance authority:** accepts deliverables for the providing organization.
- **Customer acceptance authority:** accepts or rejects where the agreement requires it.
- **Change authority:** approves changes according to impact and delegated limits.
- **Independent reviewer:** provides separation from delivery where risk warrants it.

A responsibility matrix can clarify who prepares, reviews, approves, performs, verifies, and receives evidence. Shared accountability without a named decision authority should be treated as a governance defect.

### 4. Translate expectations into controlled criteria

For each significant deliverable or service outcome, define:

- Required characteristics or results.
- Objective acceptance criteria.
- Verification or validation method.
- Required environment, data, tools, and tolerances.
- Responsible performer and reviewer.
- Evidence to retain.
- Timing and prerequisite conditions.
- Treatment of exceptions, defects, and conditional acceptance.

Criteria should be measurable or otherwise objectively assessable. Terms such as "high quality," "user friendly," or "production ready" need an agreed operational meaning before work reaches an acceptance gate.

Controls may include design reviews, peer review, testing, inspections, reconciliations, sampling, service-readiness checks, security review, supplier verification, or operational monitoring. Select controls based on the consequences and likelihood of failure rather than applying every available control.

### 5. Review and accept the plan

Review the draft with affected delivery, quality, commercial, security, legal, operational, supplier, and customer representatives as appropriate. Confirm that:

- Commitments and criteria agree with governing contracts.
- Controls can actually be performed with available resources and access.
- Acceptance authorities are authorized and available.
- Evidence formats and retention locations are agreed.
- Dependencies, assumptions, and exclusions are visible.
- Exceptions to standard processes have appropriate authorization.
- Disputes and escalation paths are defined.

Plan acceptance should be recorded before the relevant controlled work begins. If urgent work must start earlier, document the temporary authorization, its limits, and the deadline for full acceptance.

### 6. Apply, monitor, and escalate

Treat the accepted plan as an operating control, not a document produced only for an audit. Incorporate its gates into work tracking and delivery ceremonies. Monitor control completion, defect trends, overdue evidence, unresolved deviations, supplier performance, and acceptance status.

A failed control should result in a defined disposition: correct and repeat, accept under authorized concession, restrict release, or reject. Significant issues should be escalated according to business impact rather than hidden by schedule reporting.

### 7. Control changes and re-acceptance

Changes to scope, criteria, methods, suppliers, architecture, schedule, or applicable obligations may invalidate prior assumptions or evidence. Use a controlled change process that records:

1. The proposed change and reason.
2. Affected deliverables, controls, interfaces, and evidence.
3. Quality, contractual, security, operational, and schedule impacts.
4. Required regression or repeat verification.
5. Approval authority.
6. Whether the plan or affected deliverable requires re-acceptance.

Editorial corrections may follow a lightweight path. Material changes should not be treated as editorial merely to avoid review. Preserve version history and identify which plan version governed each accepted deliverable.

## Failure modes

Common failures include:

- Creating a generic plan that merely restates corporate policy.
- Drafting the plan after delivery, when it can no longer guide controls.
- Confusing relationship health or adoption activity with quality assurance.
- Using ambiguous acceptance criteria that encourage late disputes.
- Assigning verification to the same person who performed high-risk work without considering independence.
- Listing controls without owners, dates, evidence, or consequences for failure.
- Assuming supplier certification eliminates the need to verify engagement outputs.
- Allowing informal scope changes to bypass impact assessment.
- Reusing earlier evidence after a material change without evaluating validity.
- Treating conditional acceptance as final acceptance and losing outstanding actions.
- Retaining screenshots or approvals without traceability to the tested version.
- Producing excessive documentation that obscures the controls that matter.

## Evidence and review

The engagement evidence set should be sufficient to reconstruct key decisions and demonstrate that agreed controls operated. Depending on the engagement, it may include:

- The accepted quality plan and revision history.
- Review comments, approvals, and delegated authorities.
- Requirements-to-acceptance traceability.
- Test, inspection, reconciliation, or validation results.
- Defect, deviation, waiver, and concession records.
- Supplier verification and handoff records.
- Release or service-readiness decisions.
- Customer acceptance, rejection, or conditional acceptance.
- Change impact assessments and re-acceptance decisions.
- Closure findings and lessons incorporated into reusable controls.

Review the plan at defined milestones and when a material trigger occurs. Suitable triggers include contract amendment, major design change, supplier replacement, severe defect, missed gate, new obligation, or acceptance dispute.

At closure, confirm that required evidence is complete, open concessions have an owner, retention obligations are assigned, and lessons are routed into the broader management system. The plan may then be formally closed, but its records should remain governed by applicable retention and confidentiality rules.
