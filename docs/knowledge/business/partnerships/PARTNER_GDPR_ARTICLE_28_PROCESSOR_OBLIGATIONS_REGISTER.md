# Partner GDPR Article 28 Processor Obligations Register

## Scope

This article governs the register in which an organization records the processor obligations it has taken on for each partnership relationship that involves processing of personal data on behalf of another party. The register is the working instrument by which Article 28 of the General Data Protection Regulation is operationalized across the partner portfolio.

Article 28 establishes the obligations a processor owes to a controller: processing only on documented instructions, ensuring confidentiality of personnel, implementing Article 32 security measures, honouring sub-processor controls under Article 28(2) and (4), assisting with data-subject rights, assisting with the controller's other obligations under Articles 32 to 36, returning or deleting data at the end of processing, making available information necessary to demonstrate compliance, and submitting to audits. The register exists to ensure that each obligation is identifiable, owned, evidenced, and current for every controller relationship.

The scope is the register and its operating discipline. The article does not constitute legal advice, does not replace a data-processing agreement, and does not address sectoral rules that may add further requirements (for example in health, financial services, or telecommunications). Where the partnership is itself a controller-to-controller relationship, Article 28 does not apply and the register should not be used as the basis for that relationship.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

1. Maintain the register as the single source of truth for processor obligations across the partner portfolio. Each entry identifies the controller, the processor entity (which may be this organization), the data categories, the processing purposes, the data-subject categories, the retention period, the sub-processors, the transfer mechanisms, the security measures, and the named accountable owner.
2. For each entry, decompose Article 28 into its component obligations and assign each component to a control, a system, a process, and an evidence artifact. An obligation without an assigned control cannot be evidenced; a control without an evidence artifact cannot be verified.
3. Record the lawful instruction on which processing is performed. The instruction is the documented direction from the controller; it is the boundary of authorized processing. Where the controller's instructions change, the change should be reflected in the register, and any conflict with prior processing should be resolved before further processing continues.
4. Capture sub-processor changes through a defined authorization flow. Article 28(2) requires the controller's prior specific or general written authorization, with the right to object. The flow should distinguish between the two modes, retain evidence of authorization, and ensure that sub-processors are bound by the same data-protection obligations.
5. Capture the security measures under Article 32. The measures should be specific to the processing (not generic), evidence-based, and tied to the risks identified. Where the security measures change, the change should be documented and, where material, communicated to the controller.
6. Capture the personal-data breach handling. The processor is required to notify the controller without undue delay after becoming aware of a breach. The register should record the notification SLA, the contact points on each side, the categories of information to be communicated, and the evidence retention.
7. Capture the data-subject rights cooperation. The processor is required to assist the controller in fulfilling data-subject rights under Articles 12 to 23. The register should record the response SLA, the technical mechanism (search, extract, delete, portability), and any lawful basis for declining assistance.
8. Capture the assistance with controller obligations. Articles 33 to 36 require the controller to maintain a record of processing activities, secure processing, breach notification, and data-protection impact assessment. The processor is required to assist. The register should record the assistance mechanisms.
9. Capture the deletion-or-return obligation at end of processing. The processor is required to delete or return all personal data at the controller's choice, and to delete existing copies unless retention is required by law. The register should record the deletion-or-return procedure, the timeline, and the evidence retained.
10. Capture the audit obligation. The processor is required to make available to the controller all information necessary to demonstrate compliance and to allow audits. The register should record the audit mechanism, the cadence, the cost allocation, and the evidence retention.
11. Re-review the register on defined triggers (change of scope, change of sub-processors, change of law, breach, governance finding) and on a defined cadence.

## Controls

The register is itself a control surface. A change-management control governs amendments to entries. A sub-processor control governs authorization and notification. A security-of-processing control ensures that Article 32 measures remain in force. A breach-notification control ensures that the controller is informed within the agreed window. A data-subject rights cooperation control ensures that requests are answered within the controller's timeline. A deletion-or-return control ensures that data is not retained beyond the agreed boundary. An audit cooperation control ensures that the controller's audit rights can be exercised. An evidence-retention control ensures that evidence survives the audit window.

## Validation evidence

Validation evidence includes the register itself, the controller-by-controller instruction records, the sub-processor authorizations and notifications, the Article 32 evidence, the breach-notification records, the data-subject rights response records, the deletion-or-return confirmations, the audit cooperation records, the periodic review records, and any escalation records. Evidence should be sufficient for the controller or for a regulator to reconstruct how the obligation was discharged in a specific period.

## Failure modes and correction

Common failure modes include the register being maintained at the contract level but not at the operational level, sub-processor changes happening without the controller's authorization, the processor exceeding the controller's documented instructions, security measures being assumed rather than evidenced, breach notification slipping the agreed SLA, data-subject rights responses being silently blocked by an inability to locate data, deletion-or-return being treated as best-effort, and audit cooperation being theoretical rather than practical.

Correction requires identifying the affected entries, restoring compliance with the obligation, notifying the controller where the failure affects the controller's own obligations, and where the failure involves a personal-data breach, assessing notification duties under Article 33 and Article 34. Repeated failure indicates a structural problem with the register's operating discipline and should be escalated to the privacy-governance function.

## Limitations

This article covers the register and its operating discipline. It does not constitute legal advice, does not establish that any particular arrangement complies with the GDPR, and does not address sectoral rules that may add further requirements. Article 28 is one element of the GDPR; compliance with Article 28 does not establish compliance with the rest of the regulation.
