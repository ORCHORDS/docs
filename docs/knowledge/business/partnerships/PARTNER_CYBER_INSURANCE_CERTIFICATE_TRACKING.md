# Partner Cyber Insurance Certificate Tracking

## Scope

This article governs the tracking and use of cyber-insurance certificates held by partners, and the discipline of requesting and reviewing such certificates as part of partnership risk treatment. It applies the risk-treatment posture of ISO 31000:2018 — Risk management — Guidelines, in particular the principle that risk treatment can include transferring risk (in whole or in part) to a third party, and that any transfer should be supported by evidence that the third party is able to bear the risk being transferred.

The scope is the governance of cyber-insurance certificates as one input among several in partnership risk treatment. It does not address the design of this organization's own insurance program, the negotiation of insurance terms, the underwriting process, or the determination of whether a particular loss is covered. It does not address non-cyber insurance lines that may also be relevant (general liability, professional indemnity, crime).

Cyber insurance is not a substitute for the partner's own security controls. A certificate of insurance is evidence that the partner has arranged financial cover; it is not evidence that the partner's security posture is adequate. The certificate should be treated as part of the risk picture, not as the whole of it.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

1. Identify the partnership relationships for which cyber insurance is a relevant consideration. Criticality, data sensitivity, regulatory exposure, operational dependence, and concentration risk are useful classifiers. Insurance is most relevant where the residual loss from a partner incident would be material to this organization and where the partner's own capacity to absorb that loss is in question.
2. Define the insurance evidence required by tier. The minimum evidence is a certificate of insurance naming the issuing carrier, the policyholder, the policy period, the limits, the sub-limits (especially for cyber, privacy, and regulatory cover), the deductible or retention, the territorial scope, and any material exclusions or endorsements.
3. Request the certificate through a defined channel, not through informal email. The request should specify the partnership identifier, the purpose for which the certificate will be used, the data classification of the relationship, and the carrier-acceptable methods of verification.
4. Verify the certificate with the carrier or the broker where the relationship is material. Verification should confirm that the policy is in force, that the limits and sub-limits are as stated, and that no notice of cancellation or non-renewal is outstanding.
5. Track the certificate in a register that captures the partner, the policy reference, the carrier, the broker, the period, the limits, the sub-limits, the deductible, the territorial scope, the material exclusions, the verification reference, the next renewal date, and any conditions imposed on the partnership by this organization's reliance on the policy.
6. Set the cadence for re-verification by tier. The certificate is typically valid for twelve months; verification should occur at issuance, at renewal, and on defined triggers (carrier downgrade, claims activity, regulatory action, material change in the partner's business).
7. Re-evaluate reliance on the certificate on defined triggers: a claim under the policy, a notice of cancellation or non-renewal, a material change in the partner's business (acquisition, divestiture, financial distress), a material change in this organization's reliance on the partner, or a change in the regulatory environment that affects the cover.
8. Where the certificate is not provided or does not meet the criteria, decide on the response. The response is not automatically to terminate the partnership; it is to make an informed risk-treatment decision — accept the residual, reduce the scope of the partnership, transfer the risk through other means, or avoid the activity. ISO 31000 is explicit that risk treatment is not a single option.
9. Where reliance on the certificate is significant, consider reciprocal evidence. This organization should be able to produce comparable evidence to its own partners and customers. Asymmetric burden produces no additional assurance and erodes trust.
10. Treat the certificate as a financial-transfer instrument, not as evidence of security posture. The partner's security controls, incident-history record, and attestation posture should be reviewed separately.

## Controls

Tracking cyber-insurance certificates requires controls on evidence collection, evidence verification, register maintenance, and trigger management. A collection control ensures certificates are requested at the right time and through the right channel. A verification control ensures the certificate can be confirmed against the carrier or broker. A register control ensures the certificates are tracked alongside the partnerships they cover and alongside the policy renewals. A trigger control ensures that defined events (carrier downgrade, cancellation notice, claims) prompt re-evaluation. A reciprocity control ensures that the same standards the program applies to partners are applied to this organization's own posture when it is the partner.

## Validation evidence

Validation evidence includes the register of certificates, the certificates themselves with the carrier confirmation, the verification logs, the trigger-driven re-evaluation records, the partnership-by-partnership decisions about reliance, the carrier or broker confirmations, the renewal records, and any escalation records where reliance was reduced or withdrawn. Evidence should be sufficient for an independent reviewer to reconstruct, for any partnership, what certificate was relied on, how it was verified, and how it was tracked over time.

## Failure modes and correction

Common failure modes include accepting the certificate without verifying it, treating the certificate as evidence of security posture, ignoring the sub-limits (where the actual cover is much smaller than the headline limit), ignoring the territorial scope (where the cover does not extend to the jurisdictions relevant to the partnership), ignoring the exclusions (where the cover excludes the very loss that is most concerning), and continuing to rely on a lapsed policy. Another failure is the certificate being requested once at onboarding and never re-verified.

Correction requires restoring the register, re-verifying outstanding certificates, and re-evaluating the partnership decisions that were based on the prior reliance. Where the certificate is found to be inadequate for the partnership's exposure, the response is a risk-treatment decision under ISO 31000, not a quiet acceptance of the gap.

## Limitations

This article is a governance discipline for cyber-insurance certificates as one input to partnership risk treatment. It does not constitute insurance, legal, or actuarial advice, does not establish that any particular policy provides a defined level of cover, and does not replace the partner's own security controls or this organization's own risk analysis. ISO 31000 is a guideline standard for risk management; it does not require certification, and this article does not claim certification or conformity.
