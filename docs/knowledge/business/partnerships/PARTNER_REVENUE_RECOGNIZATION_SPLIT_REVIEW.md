# Partner Revenue Recognition Split Review

## Scope

This article governs the review of how revenue is split, recognized, and reported in a partnership arrangement where two organizations share the consideration for a transaction, a subscription, a project, or a recurring service. The split can be an explicit contractual percentage, a formula tied to usage, a tiered structure, a referral or resale commission, or a more complex arrangement involving deliverables from each side.

The review draws on the principles of FASB Accounting Standards Codification Topic 606 (ASC 606), Revenue from Contracts with Customers, as framed by the AICPA for auditors applying the standard. ASC 606 is US GAAP and does not apply directly to all organizations; nevertheless, the five-step model — identifying the contract, identifying performance obligations, determining the transaction price, allocating the transaction price, and recognizing revenue when (or as) performance obligations are satisfied — is a useful discipline for any organization that needs to reason about how consideration should be divided and reported in a partnership.

The scope of the article is the partnership's revenue-split review: how the split is determined, how it changes over time, who is responsible for recognizing what, and how the parties reconcile. It does not replace each party's own financial reporting, does not constitute audit or accounting advice, and does not address tax treatment.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

1. Open a split-review record for each partnership arrangement that allocates consideration between the parties. The record identifies the contractual basis, the named accountable owner on each side, the period of the arrangement, and the version of the split formula in force.
2. Identify the performance obligations in the joint arrangement. Each obligation should have an owner, a deliverable, a stand-alone selling price (or an equivalent basis under IFRS 15 if relevant), and a recognition pattern. Performance obligations that cross party lines should be made explicit.
3. Determine the transaction price. State the consideration expected from the customer, the variable components (usage, refunds, rebates, credits, performance bonuses), the constraint on variable consideration, and the currency. State how changes to the transaction price during the contract are reflected in the split.
4. Allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation on a stand-alone selling price basis, adjusted for the partnership's agreed split. Where the stand-alone selling price is not directly observable, document the method used (adjusted market assessment, expected cost plus margin, or residual) and the assumptions.
5. Recognize revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied. For the partnership split, the party that satisfies the obligation typically recognizes the corresponding share, subject to contractual allocation rules that may differ from the obligation-by-obligation recognition.
6. Reflect changes in the split formula over time. A revised commission rate, a revised tier, or a revised usage formula is a modification of the arrangement. Treat the modification under the chosen accounting framework — for ASC 606, treat it as a separate contract, a termination of the existing contract and creation of a new one, or a part of the existing contract, depending on its nature and pricing.
7. Reconcile the recognition between the parties on a defined cadence. Each party reports what it has recognized for the period, what it has billed, what it has collected, and any differences. Unreconciled differences should be investigated before the period is closed.
8. Retain evidence supporting the recognition: contract, amendments, performance evidence, billing records, collection records, credit notes, and any disputed items. Evidence is the auditor's foundation; missing evidence turns into audit findings even when the underlying economics were correct.
9. Review the split and the recognition on a defined cadence and on defined triggers — contract renewal, formula change, performance obligation change, dispute, or material customer event. A split agreed at signing that has not been revisited can quietly become wrong as the business evolves.
10. Disclose, where required by the parties' reporting frameworks, the existence of the partnership arrangement and the basis on which revenue is recognized.

## Controls

The split-review process requires controls similar to those of any revenue-recognition process. Authorization controls prevent unilateral changes to the split formula. Allocation controls ensure the stand-alone selling price basis is applied consistently. Reconciliation controls ensure the parties' ledgers agree. Modification controls ensure that changes to the arrangement are captured with their accounting treatment. Evidence-retention controls ensure the support for recognition survives the audit window. Segregation between the party that bills the customer and the parties that recognize revenue reduces the risk of manipulation.

## Validation evidence

Validation evidence includes the contractual split arrangement and any amendments, the performance-obligation identification and stand-alone selling price documentation, the transaction-price determination and any constraint assessments, the allocation workpaper, the recognition schedule, the periodic reconciliation between the parties, evidence of the modification treatment where the split changed, the billing and collection records, and any audit findings and their resolution.

Evidence should be sufficient for an independent reviewer to reconstruct the recognition decisions. A schedule that depends on undocumented assumptions is not assurance.

## Failure modes and correction

Common failure modes include the split formula being changed informally without a documented modification, the recognition pattern in one party's books diverging from the contractual split, variable consideration being recognized before the constraint is satisfied, performance obligations crossing party lines being recorded inconsistently, and disputes over the split being addressed by adjusting one side's recognition without adjusting the other. A subtle failure is the stand-alone selling price being inherited from a prior period and no longer reflecting the current market, leading to a misallocation that compounds over time.

Correction requires reconciling the parties' books to the agreed split, investigating the cause of the divergence, and adjusting the recognition in line with the contractual basis. Where the modification treatment was applied incorrectly, restate the affected periods in line with the chosen accounting framework. Where the divergence is recurring, escalate to a financial-control review rather than continuing to adjust individual periods.

## Limitations

This article describes a revenue-split review discipline. It is not accounting advice, does not establish that any particular arrangement complies with ASC 606, IFRS 15, or any other revenue-recognition framework, and does not address tax, transfer pricing, or audit-reporting requirements.
