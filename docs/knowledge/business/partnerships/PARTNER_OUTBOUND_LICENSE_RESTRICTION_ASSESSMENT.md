# Partner Outbound License Restriction Check

Outbound licensing to a partner is where organisations quietly give away more than intended: grant-back clauses that hand improvements back, field-of-use limits that look administrative but fragment markets, and exclusivity terms that bind future strategy. This article sets the review discipline for restrictions in outbound licences — what to check before signing, how to score each restriction's real commercial effect, and how to keep the restrictions operational after signature.

## Scope

Covers licence agreements under which the organisation grants rights to a partner: technology licences, manufacturing licences, distribution licences with embedded IP grants, and licensing within joint ventures. In scope: grant-back and improvement provisions, field-of-use and territory definitions, exclusivity and most-favoured terms, and restrictions surviving termination. Out of scope: inbound licence screening, escrow, and dispute-forum selection. The accountable role is the **outbound licence reviewer**, with commercial sign-off from the business owner of the licensed field.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Extract every restriction into a single restriction map before evaluating any of them individually. A licence that grants a field of use "industrial automation" while separately excluding "motion control" and defining "industrial automation" to include "motion control for packaging lines" contains a contradiction only visible when the three clauses are laid side by side. The map lists each restriction, its clause reference, the definitions it depends on, and the rights it limits.

Score grant-back provisions by direction, scope, and price. Non-exclusive, royalty-free grant-backs of improvements to the licensed technology are the market norm and generally acceptable. Exclusive grant-backs — where the partner owns improvements outright or receives exclusive rights — transfer the value of the partner's future development to the licensed technology and should be resisted or priced. Check three specifics: whether the grant-back covers improvements to the licensed technology only or extends to related technologies; whether it is automatic or requires assignment formalities; and whether it survives termination, which can convert a temporary partnership into a permanent one-way flow.

Check field-of-use limits for definitional workability. A field defined by application, by customer type, and by industry can be combined so that legitimate customers sit in overlaps or gaps. Test the field against the partner's actual pipeline: name five real opportunities and confirm each falls clearly inside or outside the licensed field. Where a field is hard to police — because the same product serves multiple fields — prefer territory, product-line, or channel limits instead, and write a field-verification mechanism into the reporting obligations.

Assess exclusivity by duration, performance conditions, and escape valves. Exclusivity without minimum performance obligations transfers market risk to the licensor. Require minimum volumes, revenue, or activity commitments with defined measurement periods and a contraction or termination right on shortfall. Check whether exclusivity is exclusive against the licensor only, or also against third parties the licensor might authorise, and whether the partner may sublicense within the field, which multiplies the restriction's reach. Identify any most-favoured-licence clause: a commitment that future licensees get better terms can constrain pricing strategy across the whole portfolio.

Evaluate restriction interactions. Field limits plus territory limits plus channel limits can combine to leave the partner no viable market, producing a predictable breach or a renegotiation from weakness. Conversely, broad fields plus exclusivity can bar the licensor from its own adjacent markets. Model both failure directions.

Negotiate survivability explicitly. Restrictions on the licensor's own research, on improvements, and on servicing customers frequently survive termination by default drafting; decide deliberately which do and record the intent in a term sheet so drafting matches the deal.

Record the accepted restriction set in the operating file, with the definitions it depends on and the reporting the partner owes to make field and exclusivity limits verifiable.

## Controls

- Restriction map produced for every outbound licence before signature, listing clause references and definitional dependencies.
- Grant-back scorecard: direction, scope, price, survival, per deal.
- Pipeline test evidence: five named opportunities classified inside or outside the licensed field.
- Exclusivity conditions register: minimum performance metrics, measurement windows, shortfall remedies, and review dates.
- Reporting clause checklist ensuring the partner supplies the data that makes field, territory, and exclusivity limits checkable.
- Definition control: any reused defined term ("Field", "Territory", "Improvements") resolves to one authoritative clause.
- Post-signature compliance calendar tracking minimum-performance milestones and reporting receipts.

## Validation evidence

The check produces the restriction map, the executed licence, the grant-back scorecard, the pipeline classification note, minimum-performance schedules, and the reporting receipts collected after signature. Validate by selecting one active restriction — a field limit, say — and confirming the partner's reported revenue classification can be reproduced from its reporting against the field definition, with any borderline cases escalated and resolved in writing. Reconcile the minimum-performance calendar with actual receipts; missed reporting is itself a controlled event with escalation, not an administrative oversight. Review the restriction map annually against the partner's observed product scope, because products drift into unlicensed fields through ordinary roadmap evolution. Where restrictions were renegotiated, retain both versions with dates, since the applicable restriction at any historical moment determines what was owed then.

## Failure modes and correction

Typical failures: granting exclusivity without performance minimums and waiting two years before acting on shortfall; field definitions built on industry labels that the partner's new product line straddles; automatic exclusive grant-backs accepted in a template and never re-examined; most-favoured clauses triggering unexpectedly when a later, better-priced licence is signed in a different market; and termination clauses that kill the licence but leave improvement grants standing. Correct by exercising contractual remedies on schedule rather than accumulating forbearance that reads as waiver; amending fields with named annexes rather than interpretive emails; and conducting a grant-back audit when a partner's patent filings claim priority from jointly discussed work. Where a restriction has become unenforceable in practice, renegotiate openly rather than relying on selective enforcement.

## Limitations

Restriction enforceability varies with competition law: field-of-use and exclusivity arrangements may be scrutinised where they amount to market division, and grant-backs can raise concerns where the licensor holds dominance. The checks here are commercial-diligence steps, not antitrust clearance. Field classification of real opportunities involves judgement at boundaries. Performance-remedy timing interacts with waiver and estoppel doctrines that only counsel can assess for a specific case.
