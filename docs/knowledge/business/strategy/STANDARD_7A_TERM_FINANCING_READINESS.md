# Standard 7(a) Term Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) **Standard 7(a)** delivery method is a term-loan option within SBA's primary business loan program. Current SBA lender guidance defines Standard 7(a) loans as loans from **$350,001 to $5 million**, excluding specialized delivery methods such as SBA Express, Export Express, CAPLines, Export Working Capital, International Trade, and pilot programs.

Standard 7(a) financing can support a broad range of eligible business purposes, but it is not a revolving line of credit. Readiness depends on a clear use of proceeds, repayment capacity, collateral analysis, ownership/eligibility, and a coherent financing structure.

## Current public terms

SBA's current lender guidance lists:

- loan amount: **$350,001 to $5 million**;
- maximum SBA guaranty: **75%**;
- interest rate: negotiated between borrower and lender subject to SBA maximums;
- revolving credit: not available under Standard 7(a); and
- processing through delegated or non-delegated SBA lender channels.

Verify current SOP 50 10 and lender terms before relying on any program threshold.

## Common eligible purposes

Current SBA public guidance lists 7(a) uses that can include:

- acquiring, refinancing, or improving real estate/buildings;
- short- or long-term working capital;
- refinancing eligible business debt;
- machinery and equipment;
- furniture, fixtures, and supplies;
- complete or partial changes of ownership; and
- multi-purpose financing.

A specific transaction still needs to satisfy current eligibility and use-of-proceeds rules.

## Financing plan

Prepare a sources-and-uses schedule that identifies:

1. each use of proceeds;
2. amount;
3. vendor/seller/payee where known;
4. borrower contribution;
5. other financing sources;
6. timing; and
7. expected economic benefit.

Do not use a broad label such as "expansion" when the lender needs to understand the actual assets, working capital, acquisition, or refinancing components.

## Repayment capacity

A 7(a) guaranty protects the lender against part of the loss; it does not make a weak business able to repay debt.

Model debt service from realistic operating cash flow and include:

- existing debt;
- new loan payment;
- owner compensation/distributions;
- taxes;
- working-capital needs;
- capital expenditures; and
- downside scenarios.

A borrower should be able to explain the assumptions behind projected cash flow rather than presenting unsupported growth percentages.

## Collateral readiness

Current SBA lender guidance describes Standard 7(a) collateral rules based on assets acquired, refinanced, or improved with the loan and available fixed assets of the applicant, subject to current SOP conditions.

Maintain a collateral schedule showing:

- asset description;
- ownership;
- estimated/book value;
- existing liens;
- lender;
- lien priority; and
- whether the asset is being acquired or improved with loan proceeds.

Do not assume the SBA guaranty means collateral is irrelevant.

## Acquisition financing

For a business acquisition or ownership change, prepare:

- purchase agreement;
- allocation of purchase price;
- historical financials of the target;
- buyer financial information;
- management transition plan;
- working-capital requirement;
- valuation evidence where required; and
- post-close debt-service model.

Separate business value, real estate, equipment, inventory, and working capital so the financing structure is understandable.

## Real-estate financing

For real-estate uses, anticipate:

- purchase contract;
- appraisal;
- environmental review;
- title;
- insurance;
- occupancy/use requirements;
- renovation budget; and
- closing conditions.

Compare 7(a) with 504 financing when a project is primarily long-lived fixed assets.

## Working-capital financing

When Standard 7(a) provides permanent working capital, distinguish that need from short-term revolving credit.

Document:

- cash-cycle gap;
- inventory/receivable needs;
- growth assumptions;
- liquidity buffer; and
- why term financing is appropriate.

A term loan can improve liquidity but does not provide ongoing re-borrowing after principal is repaid.

## Debt refinancing

For refinancing, identify:

- debt being refinanced;
- original purpose;
- outstanding balance;
- payment history;
- collateral;
- existing rate/maturity; and
- business benefit from refinancing.

Confirm the debt qualifies under current SBA requirements rather than assuming all business debt can be refinanced.

## Lender-readiness package

A typical package can include:

- business and owner information;
- historical/interim financial statements;
- tax information required by the lender;
- debt schedule;
- projections;
- use-of-proceeds schedule;
- collateral schedule;
- purchase agreements/quotes; and
- management/business-plan information.

Exact requirements vary by lender and transaction.

## Evidence to retain

Retain where appropriate:

- application and SBA/lender forms;
- loan agreement;
- sources-and-uses schedule;
- collateral/lien documents;
- closing statement;
- invoices or acquisition records;
- financial reporting; and
- amendments/waivers.

## Scope note

This article provides general Standard 7(a) readiness guidance based on current SBA public information. It does not guarantee eligibility, approval, a $5 million loan, a particular maturity, collateral result, or interest rate. Current SBA rules and lender underwriting control the actual financing.
