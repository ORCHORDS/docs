# Strategic Venture Gravity Definition Vint Brown

## Scope

This article describes how to use the "gravity" framing attributed to Stanford d.school professor and IDEO founder David Kelley and popularized in writing by Michael Eisner (former Disney CEO) referencing Tom Kelley / David Kelley, sometimes associated with Pixar's Ed Catmull and Pixar's John Lasseter ecosystem, and by Tim Brown (IDEO chair) in his 2008 Harvard Business Review article *Design Thinking*. The framing here is adapted to strategic planning: a venture has "gravitational pull" when its value proposition, its customers, and its capabilities align so strongly that the venture attracts partners, talent, and capital without the venture having to spend disproportionate effort recruiting them. The article treats the framing as a planning aid; it does not validate that any particular venture has gravity and does not substitute for primary research on customers, partners, and capital markets.

The gravity definition is a structured field that records the venture's mass in three axes: customer gravity (how strongly the customer is drawn to the venture), partner gravity (how strongly partner organizations are drawn to the venture), and capital gravity (how strongly capital providers are drawn to the venture). Each axis is scored on the basis of observable evidence and labeled by source and date. The aggregate gravity score is a planning signal, not a forecast.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's Stevenson heuristics article (which assesses the fit between the venture and the team) and the leaf's capability heatmap article (which assesses the organization's capability to deliver the venture). The three lenses (gravity, Stevenson fit, capability heatmap) are complementary, not redundant.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Building the gravity definition proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why the venture's gravity is being defined, who will use the definition, and what decision it informs. A gravity definition used to support a capital raise is different from one used in an internal review.
2. **Restate the venture in operational terms.** Replace the venture name with a specific description: the customer being served, the problem being solved, the approach being taken, and the geography and time horizon. The gravity definition depends on this description.
3. **Define customer gravity.** Customer gravity is the degree to which the customer is drawn to the venture on the basis of the value proposition, the credibility of the team, the credibility of the evidence, and the credibility of the brand. Score on a documented rubric.
4. **Define partner gravity.** Partner gravity is the degree to which potential partners (suppliers, distributors, channel partners, technology partners, regulators) are drawn to the venture on the basis of the venture's strategic fit with their goals. Score on the same rubric.
5. **Define capital gravity.** Capital gravity is the degree to which capital providers are drawn to the venture on the basis of the venture's market, the venture's economics, and the venture's team. Score on the same rubric.
6. **Document the evidence behind each score.** Each score should be supported by observable evidence: signed letters of intent, named customer development conversations, partner term sheets, capital interest indications, third-party recognition. A score without evidence is provisional.
7. **Aggregate and reconcile.** The gravity definition should reconcile to the Stevenson fit assessment, the capability heatmap, and the broader plan. A high gravity score with low Stevenson fit or low capability is a tension to address.

The appendix should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the venture description, the three gravity axes, the aggregate, and the principal risks of misjudgment. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying tables.

## Controls

- **Venture description frozen before scoring.** The venture description is written and approved before any axis is scored. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Evidence required for each score.** Each score is supported by named sources, dates, and reviewers. A score without evidence is provisional and surfaced in the review.
- **Consistent rater set.** The same set of raters scores each axis. Ad hoc scoring by different people produces cells that are not comparable.
- **Aggregate rule stated.** The rule for combining the three axis scores into the aggregate is stated up front. Mid-cycle change in the rule is an amendment.
- **Cadenced review.** The gravity definition is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. Material change in the venture, the market, or the team's composition triggers off-cycle review.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the venture description, the scores, and the aggregate. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the gravity definition from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the venture description, and the approving authority;
- the customer gravity score with source, date, and reviewer;
- the partner gravity score with source, date, and reviewer;
- the capital gravity score with source, date, and reviewer;
- the aggregate rule and the resulting score;
- the reconciliation to the Stevenson fit assessment and the capability heatmap;
- the variance between planned and actual gravity at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the minutes of the gravity review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes comparison against external checks. Where the gravity definition claims strong partner gravity but the venture has no signed partner terms, the divergence is a finding to address.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Venture description drift.** The venture described at the start of the cycle widens or narrows mid-cycle to make the gravity look better. Correct by freezing the description and treating redefinition as an amendment.
- **Score without evidence.** A gravity axis is scored at a level the rater cannot defend. Correct by surfacing provisional scores at the review and requiring evidence within a stated horizon.
- **Customer gravity asserted.** The customer is described as "drawn" without signed interest. Correct by sourcing customer gravity from documented customer development conversations, pilots, or signed terms.
- **Partner gravity asserted.** Partners are described as "aligned" without term sheets. Correct by sourcing partner gravity from documented partner engagement.
- **Capital gravity asserted.** Capital providers are described as "interested" without evidence of diligence. Correct by sourcing capital gravity from documented investor engagement (for example, term sheets, completed diligence, signed allocations).
- **Tension ignored.** The gravity score is high but the Stevenson fit or the capability heatmap is low. Correct by surfacing the tension at the review and deciding which lens governs before the venture proceeds.
- **No plan reconciliation.** The gravity definition does not reconcile to the Stevenson fit, the capability heatmap, or the broader plan. Correct by reconciling before approval.

When any of these condition is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the gravity definition.

## Limitations

The gravity framing is a planning aid. It does not validate that any particular venture has gravity and does not substitute for primary research on customers, partners, and capital markets. The framing here draws on design-thinking and innovation literature published in *Harvard Business Review* and other venues; the application of that literature to strategic planning is by adaptation rather than direct prescription.

The framing does not address the question of fit between the venture and the team (that is the subject of the Stevenson heuristics article) or the question of whether the organization has the capabilities to deliver the venture (that is the subject of the capability heatmap article). The three lenses (gravity, Stevenson fit, capability heatmap) are complementary, not redundant.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
