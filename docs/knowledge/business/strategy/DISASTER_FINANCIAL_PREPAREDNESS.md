# Disaster Financial Preparedness

## Purpose

Disaster financial preparedness estimates how long a business can continue meeting critical obligations when revenue, facilities, staff, suppliers, systems, or customer access are disrupted. The objective is to make financial recovery assumptions explicit before an emergency occurs.

Preparedness is broader than applying for post-disaster assistance. A business should understand its own liquidity, insurance, records, dependencies, and decision thresholds before relying on external recovery funding.

## SBA preparedness basis

U.S. Small Business Administration emergency guidance recommends a three-step pattern: assess realistic risks, create a response plan, and practice the plan. SBA notes that disasters can create substantial financial costs and that preparation improves the ability to recover and continue operations.

SBA also administers disaster-loan programs after qualifying declared disasters. Those programs have specific eligibility, loss, use-of-proceeds, credit, declaration, and application requirements; they should not be treated as guaranteed emergency funding.

## Financial-impact assessment

For realistic hazards, estimate where practical:

- expected duration of service interruption;
- revenue or collection delays;
- payroll and benefit obligations;
- rent, utilities, debt service, taxes, subscriptions, and other fixed commitments;
- supplier prepayments or replacement costs;
- temporary facilities, logistics, connectivity, or equipment costs;
- customer refunds, credits, remediation, or contractual exposure;
- insurance deductibles, exclusions, waiting periods, and expected claim timing;
- data or record restoration costs;
- critical staff or contractor costs; and
- additional recovery or mitigation spending.

Model timing, not only total loss. A reimbursable cost can still create a liquidity problem if payment arrives after obligations are due.

## Liquidity planning

Define the liquidity needed to sustain critical operations through plausible disruption periods. Consider:

- unrestricted cash;
- committed credit facilities and their actual draw conditions;
- insurance proceeds and realistic timing;
- owner or investor funding only where genuinely available;
- receivable collections likely to continue during disruption;
- spending that can be deferred without causing greater harm; and
- minimum cash thresholds that trigger escalation or protective action.

Do not count an unapproved loan, uncertain insurance claim, or hypothetical investor contribution as committed liquidity.

## Insurance review

Financial preparedness should record the role insurance is expected to play without assuming every disaster loss is covered.

Review where relevant:

- property and equipment coverage;
- business-interruption or income coverage;
- cyber or technology-related coverage;
- deductibles and limits;
- waiting periods;
- exclusions;
- documentation needed for a claim;
- dependency or contingent-business-interruption coverage; and
- contact and notification procedures.

Use qualified insurance or legal advice for policy interpretation. Internal planning summaries should not be presented as binding interpretations of coverage.

## Financial records

Protect the records needed to operate, substantiate losses, obtain assistance, make insurance claims, pay staff and suppliers, and meet legal or tax obligations.

Relevant records can include:

- financial statements and ledgers;
- bank and financing records;
- accounts-receivable and payable records;
- payroll records;
- insurance policies and claim contacts;
- asset inventories and purchase evidence;
- leases and critical contracts;
- tax records;
- supplier and customer obligations; and
- ownership and authorization records.

Backups should be accessible when the normal office, device, identity system, or network is unavailable, while still being protected against unauthorized access.

## Recovery funding

SBA's current disaster programs distinguish different purposes. Business physical disaster loans can support repair or replacement of eligible disaster-damaged business property after qualifying declared disasters. Economic Injury Disaster Loans can provide working capital to eligible small businesses and certain other organizations that cannot meet ordinary and necessary financial obligations because of the disaster.

Eligibility depends on current program rules and the specific declared disaster. Do not build a continuity plan that assumes SBA assistance will be available, sufficient, or approved.

## Decision thresholds

Define financial triggers before an event, such as:

- projected cash falling below a minimum buffer;
- inability to fund the next payroll cycle;
- interruption exceeding the insurance waiting period;
- loss of a critical supplier or facility;
- receivables aging beyond a defined limit;
- recovery cost exceeding delegated authority; or
- a scenario requiring external financing.

For each threshold, identify the accountable decision owner and available actions.

## Exercises

Financial elements should be included in continuity and disaster exercises, not left as a paper appendix.

Test questions such as:

- Can critical financial records be accessed from an alternate location?
- Who can authorize emergency spending or financing?
- How long can essential obligations be met without normal revenue?
- Can payroll and supplier payments continue if primary systems are unavailable?
- Are insurance contacts and evidence requirements known?
- Can the business identify losses separately from ordinary performance decline?
- Are customer refunds or credits financially and operationally executable?

Update assumptions after exercises, incidents, major growth, financing changes, facility moves, insurance changes, or significant supplier changes.

## Evidence to retain

For a material preparedness plan, retain where appropriate:

- hazard and interruption assumptions;
- liquidity calculations;
- critical-expense inventory;
- insurance summary and review date;
- protected financial-record inventory;
- emergency authorities and contacts;
- recovery-funding assumptions and their confidence level;
- exercise results; and
- remediation owners and completion evidence.

## Scope note

This article describes reusable financial-preparedness practices. It does not provide insurance, accounting, tax, legal, lending, or investment advice and does not assert eligibility for SBA disaster assistance or any other recovery program.
