# Strategic Decision Records

A strategic decision record preserves why an organization made a consequential choice, what evidence was available, who had authority, and how the result will be reviewed. It supports accountability without pretending that uncertainty can be removed. The record is most useful for choices that are costly to reverse, create significant obligations, consume scarce capacity, or materially alter objectives.

This practice complements a business plan and meeting minutes. The plan describes direction; minutes establish formal action; the decision record connects a specific choice to alternatives, assumptions, controls, and follow-up. It should not become a diary of routine operational approvals.

## Scope and decision threshold

Define in advance which choices require a record. Examples include entering or leaving a market, approving a major capital commitment, changing a core delivery model, accepting material regulatory exposure, acquiring another business, or signing an unusually long or concentrated contract. Use financial, duration, reversibility, safety, legal, and reputational thresholds rather than relying only on purchase value.

The decision sponsor owns the case. A designated authority—owner, executive, committee, or board—approves it under the organization’s delegation rules. Finance, legal, risk, technical, or customer leaders provide challenge according to the issue; their participation does not transfer accountability from the decision-maker.

## Workflow

1. **Frame the decision.** State the question, deadline, decision owner, approving authority, affected objectives, constraints, and what happens if no action is taken.
2. **Establish criteria before comparing options.** Define the measures that matter, their relative importance, minimum requirements, and risk limits. This reduces the temptation to rewrite criteria around a favored option.
3. **Develop genuine alternatives.** Include the status quo or defer option where feasible. Describe each option consistently: benefit, cost, timing, dependencies, reversibility, compliance implications, and principal risks.
4. **Document evidence and uncertainty.** Link forecasts, research, customer evidence, expert advice, and source dates. Label estimates, ranges, conflicts, missing information, and assumptions that would change the choice.
5. **Record challenge and conflicts.** Capture material dissent, responses, declared interests, recusals, and conditions requested by reviewers. Do not erase disagreement after approval.
6. **Make and communicate the decision.** State the selected option, rationale, approval date, accountable implementer, budget or authority granted, conditions, and stakeholders who must be informed.
7. **Schedule validation.** Define expected outcomes, leading and lagging measures, review dates, and thresholds for correction, pause, or exit.

## Controls

Give every record a unique identifier, version, owner, decision date, and status. Keep the evidence package with the approved version, apply access controls for confidential material, and retain records according to legal and organizational requirements. Changes after approval should be amendments with dates and authorities, not silent overwrites.

Use a delegation-of-authority check before approval. For governing-body decisions, minutes should show the action taken and any abstentions or recusals. The record should reference—not replace—the formal resolution. Conflict handling should follow applicable law and the entity’s governing documents; Internal Revenue Service guidance for tax-exempt organizations is also a useful primary example of documenting disclosures, recusals, and contemporaneous minutes.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the decision using the record and linked material. Evidence includes dated option analysis, source data, approval or resolution, conflict declarations, implementation milestones, and outcome reviews. Validate a sample periodically: confirm the named authority had power to decide, required specialists reviewed the case, assumptions were current, and follow-up occurred on schedule.

Assess decision quality separately from outcome. A sound process can encounter an adverse external event, while a poor process can be temporarily lucky. Compare actual results with the stated ranges and identify which assumptions, execution choices, or environmental changes explain variance.

## Failure handling

If material evidence is missing before the deadline, the approver can defer, commission targeted work, choose a reversible pilot, or explicitly accept the information gap within their authority. If an undisclosed conflict or authority defect is discovered, pause implementation where practical and send the matter to the proper governing or legal channel for review; do not retroactively edit the original record.

When a trigger is reached after approval, the accountable owner initiates the specified pause, correction, or exit review. Record the new facts and issue a superseding decision rather than rewriting history. Repeated missed reviews or records created only after outcomes are known indicate a governance failure and warrant a process-level corrective action.
