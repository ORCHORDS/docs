# Capital Expenditure Budget Review

## Purpose

A capital-expenditure budget turns major asset purchases and long-lived investments into a time-phased plan that can be tested against business need, capacity, cash flow, financing, and implementation readiness before funds are committed.

Capital expenditure decisions should not be approved solely because an asset is desirable or because financing is available. The decision should show why the asset is needed, when it is needed, what alternatives exist, and how the investment affects the broader financial plan.

## SBA planning basis

Current U.S. Small Business Administration business-plan guidance recommends prospective financial projections that include capital-expenditure budgets together with forecast income statements, balance sheets, and cash-flow statements. SBA asset and equipment guidance also recommends first identifying the assets the business actually needs and then deciding how they should be acquired.

These sources support integrating capital expenditure with operating and funding assumptions rather than managing major purchases as isolated procurement events.

## Investment request

For each material proposed capital expenditure, record where appropriate:

- business purpose and problem being solved;
- asset or project description;
- required capacity, performance, location, or technical characteristics;
- estimated purchase, installation, implementation, and commissioning cost;
- expected timing of commitments and cash payments;
- expected useful operating period;
- maintenance, support, energy, insurance, training, licensing, or other lifecycle costs;
- dependencies such as facilities, staff, permits, integrations, or supplier readiness;
- expected operational or financial benefit;
- alternatives considered; and
- accountable business owner.

Avoid presenting a vendor quote as the complete investment cost when implementation or operating costs are material.

## Review workflow

1. **Confirm need.** Tie the proposal to a current operating, capacity, resilience, compliance, safety, or growth requirement.
2. **Validate demand.** Check whether the sales, utilization, workload, or strategic assumption actually requires the planned capacity.
3. **Compare alternatives.** Consider delaying, repairing, leasing, sharing, outsourcing, buying used equipment, changing process, or selecting a smaller or staged investment where relevant.
4. **Estimate lifecycle cost.** Include acquisition and the material costs of operating, maintaining, supporting, financing, and eventually replacing or disposing of the asset.
5. **Check timing.** Confirm the asset will be available when needed and that prerequisite work is included in the schedule.
6. **Reconcile funding.** Show how the purchase affects cash, working capital, debt capacity, financing, and other planned investment.
7. **Stress test.** Evaluate the decision under lower demand, implementation delay, cost overrun, higher financing cost, or shorter useful life where those risks are material.
8. **Approve or defer.** Record decision authority, conditions, and any spending or schedule limits.
9. **Track implementation.** Compare committed and actual cost with the approved budget.
10. **Review outcome.** After the asset is operating, compare actual utilization, cost, and benefit with the assumptions used to approve it.

## Capacity and utilization

A capacity investment should identify the demand level that makes the asset necessary. Useful questions include:

- What constraint exists today?
- When will existing capacity be exhausted?
- What utilization is expected after the purchase?
- Can demand be shifted, scheduled, outsourced, or otherwise managed?
- Does the investment create excess fixed cost if growth is slower than forecast?
- Can the investment be staged rather than committed all at once?

Do not use optimistic demand projections without reconciling them to the current sales or operating forecast.

## Buy, lease, or alternative acquisition

SBA guidance distinguishes buying and leasing as different acquisition approaches. A capital review should compare the economically relevant alternatives where both are realistic.

Consider:

- upfront cash or credit required;
- total expected payments;
- financing cost;
- maintenance responsibility;
- flexibility and early-termination terms;
- expected useful life;
- obsolescence risk;
- residual or resale value;
- upgrade or replacement needs; and
- operational control requirements.

Tax and accounting treatment require qualified advice and should not be inferred from a simplified internal comparison.

## Funding and liquidity

A capital expenditure can be economically attractive and still create unacceptable liquidity risk. Reconcile the timing of deposits, milestone payments, installation costs, and final payment with:

- operating cash needs;
- working-capital buffer;
- existing debt service;
- other approved capital projects;
- financing availability; and
- realistic revenue and collection timing.

If the investment depends on external financing, distinguish approved or committed funding from financing that is only planned or under application.

## Change control

Return a capital request for review when material assumptions change, such as:

- price increases;
- scope growth;
- implementation delay;
- supplier change;
- demand reduction;
- financing change;
- facility or regulatory constraint;
- major specification change; or
- expected useful life materially changing.

Define thresholds for reapproval instead of allowing cumulative small changes to bypass the original decision authority.

## Post-investment review

After a material asset is placed into service, review whether:

- final cost matched the approved budget;
- commissioning occurred on schedule;
- expected capacity became usable;
- utilization supports the original need;
- maintenance and operating costs match assumptions;
- expected benefits are observable; and
- any lessons should change future capital reviews.

A post-investment review should improve forecasting and approval discipline rather than be used to retroactively justify a completed purchase.

## Evidence to retain

For a material capital decision, retain where appropriate:

- approved investment request;
- quotations or cost basis;
- alternative analysis;
- demand or capacity assumptions;
- lifecycle-cost model;
- funding and cash-flow impact;
- approval and conditions;
- change approvals;
- final cost and implementation result; and
- post-investment review.

## Scope note

This article describes reusable capital-budget governance. It does not determine accounting capitalization, depreciation, tax treatment, procurement law, financing eligibility, or the appropriate investment decision for a specific business.
