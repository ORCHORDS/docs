# Contract CAPLine Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) CAPLines program includes a **Contract CAPLine** designed to finance costs associated with one or more specific contracts. SBA's current lender guidance states that the facility can finance contract-allocable costs, including applicable overhead or general and administrative expenses, and can be structured as revolving or non-revolving credit.

Contract CAPLine readiness is therefore different from general working-capital planning. The borrower needs to show how contract performance creates a temporary funding need and how contract proceeds are expected to repay the facility.

## Program boundary

CAPLines is an umbrella within SBA's 7(a) lending framework for short-term and cyclical working-capital needs. Current SBA guidance describes four principal CAPLine types:

- Seasonal CAPLine;
- Contract CAPLine;
- Builders CAPLine; and
- Working CAPLine.

This article focuses only on the Contract CAPLine.

Do not assume that a business needing cash for a contract automatically qualifies. Eligibility, underwriting, collateral, loan structure, and permitted uses are determined under the current SBA rules and the lender's credit decision.

## What a Contract CAPLine finances

SBA's current public lender guidance states that a Contract CAPLine finances costs of one or more specific contracts, including overhead or general and administrative expenses allocable to the contract or contracts.

A practical cost model can include, where eligible and properly allocable:

- direct labor;
- materials;
- contract-specific subcontractors;
- mobilization costs;
- approved equipment or operating costs associated with performance;
- contract-related overhead; and
- allocable general and administrative expenses.

The exact eligibility of a cost should be verified with the lender and current SBA rules rather than assumed from accounting treatment alone.

## Contract-level cash model

Before seeking financing, build a cash model around the actual contract.

At minimum, map:

1. contract value;
2. performance period;
3. major milestones;
4. expected invoicing dates;
5. approval or acceptance periods;
6. customer payment terms;
7. retainage or holdback;
8. payroll timing;
9. supplier and subcontractor payment timing;
10. required bonding or insurance costs;
11. expected change orders; and
12. the maximum cumulative cash deficit before collections.

A headline contract value does not show the amount of financing required. The relevant question is how much cash is tied up between paying performance costs and receiving contract proceeds.

## Allocability discipline

Because Contract CAPLine proceeds are linked to specific contracts, the borrower should be able to distinguish contract-related costs from unrelated business spending.

Useful controls include:

- contract-specific cost codes;
- job-cost accounting;
- separate tracking for direct and indirect costs;
- documented allocation methods for overhead and G&A;
- reconciliation to invoices and purchase orders;
- periodic work-in-progress review; and
- controls preventing loan proceeds from drifting into unrelated projects or owner distributions.

Allocation methods should be consistent, explainable, and appropriate to the business's accounting environment.

## Revolving versus non-revolving structure

SBA permits Contract CAPLines to be structured as revolving or non-revolving facilities under current public guidance.

A revolving structure can be useful when the borrower repeatedly incurs eligible contract costs and receives contract payments during the facility period. A non-revolving structure may be more appropriate when the financing need is concentrated in a defined draw-and-repayment pattern.

The lender determines the structure based on the credit, contract, collateral, monitoring, and SBA requirements. Borrowers should understand:

- draw conditions;
- borrowing-base or advance calculations if applicable;
- documentation needed for each draw;
- repayment sweeps from contract proceeds;
- interest and fees;
- reporting frequency; and
- events that can freeze additional advances.

## Maturity boundary

Current SBA public guidance states that, except for Builders CAPLines, CAPLine maturities can be up to **10 years**.

That maximum does not mean a Contract CAPLine for a short contract will necessarily be structured for 10 years. The lender should align the facility to the contract and repayment source under current program rules.

Verify the current SOP and lender terms before relying on a maturity figure in a financing plan.

## Contract quality review

A financing decision depends on more than the borrower's historical financials. The contract itself is part of the repayment story.

Review:

- customer identity and credit quality;
- signed contract or award status;
- scope clarity;
- payment terms;
- termination rights;
- acceptance criteria;
- change-order process;
- dispute provisions;
- retainage;
- liquidated damages or penalties;
- assignment-of-proceeds restrictions; and
- conditions that could delay billing or collection.

A high-margin contract with uncertain acceptance or payment terms can create greater financing risk than a lower-margin contract with reliable collections.

## Government-contract considerations

When financing a government contract, confirm the specific rules affecting performance and payment.

Potential considerations include:

- required registrations and eligibility;
- bonding;
- limitations on subcontracting for applicable set-asides;
- invoicing systems and acceptance procedures;
- assignment-of-claims requirements;
- wage or labor obligations;
- contract modifications; and
- termination rights.

Do not assume that winning a government award guarantees timely payment or removes performance risk.

## Working-capital sufficiency

A Contract CAPLine can finance eligible contract costs, but the business should still retain sufficient liquidity for costs or delays the facility does not cover.

Model downside cases such as:

- customer payment delayed 30, 60, or 90 days;
- disputed invoice;
- supplier price increase;
- subcontractor replacement;
- delayed mobilization;
- rejected milestone;
- unapproved change work;
- retainage held longer than expected; and
- another major contract drawing on shared resources.

The financing plan should show how the business survives a plausible delay without defaulting on payroll, taxes, suppliers, or other obligations.

## Lender-readiness package

A useful package can include:

- current business financial statements;
- historical financial statements;
- accounts-receivable aging;
- accounts-payable aging;
- debt schedule;
- tax or financial information required by the lender;
- signed contract, purchase order, or award documentation;
- contract budget;
- cash-flow forecast;
- work-in-progress schedule;
- subcontractor/supplier plan;
- bonding information;
- management experience; and
- explanation of the requested line amount and repayment source.

Exact requirements vary by lender and SBA policy.

## Draw governance

For each material draw, preserve enough documentation to show:

- which contract it supports;
- eligible cost category;
- underlying invoice, payroll, or cost record;
- amount;
- approval;
- cumulative contract cost;
- remaining facility availability; and
- expected repayment timing.

This helps the business manage its own project economics and supports lender monitoring.

## Repayment discipline

Contract proceeds are the central repayment source for a Contract CAPLine.

A borrower should understand whether contract receivables or proceeds must be directed to a controlled account, applied to the line, or otherwise handled under the lender's agreement.

Do not divert proceeds intended to reduce contract financing into unrelated growth spending without confirming that the loan agreement permits it.

## Changes during performance

Reforecast financing need when:

- contract value changes;
- scope changes materially;
- completion date moves;
- billing schedule changes;
- a major subcontractor is replaced;
- cost overruns emerge;
- customer payment behavior deteriorates; or
- another contract is added to the facility.

Notify the lender when required by the loan agreement. Waiting until the facility is fully drawn can remove options that were available earlier.

## Contract CAPLine versus surety bonding

Contract financing and surety bonding solve different problems.

- A Contract CAPLine can provide liquidity to pay eligible performance costs.
- A surety bond provides the project owner or other obligee with a guarantee related to bidding, performance, payment, or another bonded obligation.

A contract may require both. Coordinate financing and bonding early because the surety and lender may each evaluate working capital, backlog, contract risk, and management capacity.

## Evidence to retain

For material contract financing, retain where appropriate:

- loan and line agreements;
- financed contract documents;
- contract budget and revisions;
- cost-allocation methodology;
- draw records;
- lender reports;
- work-in-progress schedules;
- contract invoices;
- collections and repayments;
- waivers or amendments; and
- records of material contract changes.
