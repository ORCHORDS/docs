# Working Capital Planning

## Purpose

Working capital planning makes the timing of short-term operating cash needs visible before they become a payment or financing problem. A business can show positive revenue or accounting profit while still experiencing liquidity pressure when customer receipts, inventory purchases, payroll, taxes, and supplier payments occur on different schedules.

## Core working-capital view

A practical review should track at least:

- cash and immediately available liquidity;
- accounts receivable and expected collection dates;
- accounts payable and committed payment dates;
- inventory or other operating assets that absorb cash before sale;
- payroll, tax, rent, debt-service, and other near-term obligations; and
- committed purchases or capital spending that compete for the same liquidity.

Do not treat the headline difference between current assets and current liabilities as sufficient by itself. Timing, collectability, inventory conversion, and restricted or unavailable cash can materially change the operational picture.

## Planning workflow

1. Build a rolling cash forecast using realistic receipt and payment dates.
2. Maintain receivables and payables aging so overdue balances are visible rather than hidden in totals.
3. Identify inventory or work-in-progress that ties up cash and estimate how quickly it can convert into collections.
4. Separate unavoidable obligations from spending that can be delayed or reduced.
5. Define a minimum liquidity buffer appropriate to the volatility and critical obligations of the business.
6. Model downside cases, including slower collections, lower sales, supplier prepayment requirements, inventory buildup, or unexpected expenses.
7. Record the earliest decision point at which management should reduce spending, accelerate collections, renegotiate terms, or obtain financing.
8. Compare actual results with the forecast and refresh assumptions when operating conditions change.

## Receivables and payables discipline

Working-capital planning depends on the quality of underlying records. Current SBA Working Capital Pilot guidance specifically expects qualifying businesses to be able to produce timely and accurate financial statements, accounts-receivable and accounts-payable agings, and inventory reports. Those records are useful even when no SBA financing is contemplated because they expose the operating drivers behind a liquidity gap.

Aging reports should distinguish routine timing differences from persistent collection or payment problems. Material overdue receivables should not be treated as equivalent to cash. Likewise, delaying supplier payments can improve short-term cash while creating service, pricing, or supply-continuity risks that must be considered separately.

## Financing decision boundary

A forecast shortfall does not automatically mean borrowing is appropriate. Before adding debt or drawing a line of credit:

- define the amount and duration of the expected gap;
- identify the operating cause rather than using financing to conceal a persistent structural loss;
- assess the business's ability to service the debt under realistic downside assumptions;
- compare interest, fees, collateral, covenants, and flexibility; and
- match financing duration to the underlying need.

SBA's 7(a) program permits both short- and long-term working-capital uses. Its Working Capital Pilot is a monitored line-of-credit option for qualifying businesses and is not a general entitlement to financing. Eligibility and lender underwriting remain separate from internal liquidity planning.

## Expansion and major purchases

Re-run the working-capital forecast before hiring waves, new locations, large inventory commitments, equipment purchases, or other growth decisions. Growth can increase the cash tied up in receivables, inventory, and payroll before the associated revenue is collected.

The plan should show both the expected steady-state economics and the temporary funding requirement created by the transition.

## Evidence to retain

For a material working-capital decision, retain where appropriate:

- the forecast version and assumptions used;
- receivable and payable aging snapshots;
- inventory or operating-asset reports;
- downside scenarios and liquidity thresholds;
- financing comparisons or lender terms considered;
- management decisions and accountable owners; and
- follow-up comparison of forecast versus actual results.
