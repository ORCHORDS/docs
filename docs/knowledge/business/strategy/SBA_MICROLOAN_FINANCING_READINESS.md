# SBA Microloan Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) **Microloan program** provides smaller loans through approved nonprofit intermediary lenders. Current SBA public guidance states that microloans can be up to **$50,000** and may support eligible startup or expansion needs such as working capital, inventory, supplies, furniture, fixtures, machinery, and equipment.

Microloans are not made directly by SBA to the small-business borrower. SBA provides funds to intermediary organizations, and those intermediaries make credit decisions, set borrower terms within program rules, and often provide management or technical assistance.

## Current program boundary

The current SBA Microloan page states:

- maximum loan amount: **$50,000**;
- average microloan: approximately **$13,000**;
- lending through SBA-approved nonprofit intermediaries;
- maximum repayment term: **7 years**; and
- intermediary-set interest rates, generally shown by SBA in a broad range rather than as a guaranteed borrower rate.

The current direct Microloan program page should be preferred over older comparison pages that may still display prior repayment-term limits.

## Eligible uses

SBA's current public guidance lists examples including:

- working capital;
- inventory;
- supplies;
- furniture;
- fixtures;
- machinery; and
- equipment.

Current SBA guidance also states that Microloan proceeds **cannot be used to pay existing debts or purchase real estate**.

Confirm the actual proposed use with the intermediary before committing funds or signing purchase agreements.

## When a microloan can fit

A microloan can be appropriate when the business:

- needs less capital than a conventional bank/SBA term loan;
- has a specific startup or expansion use;
- benefits from hands-on technical assistance;
- is building a lending track record; or
- needs a local/community-based financing relationship.

Do not assume that a smaller loan requires less planning. A $25,000 debt obligation can still be material to a young business.

## Amount planning

Build the request from actual uses rather than asking for the maximum by default.

A useful schedule includes:

1. inventory or supply purchases;
2. equipment/furniture/fixtures;
3. working-capital reserve;
4. startup/launch expenses eligible under program rules;
5. borrower funds; and
6. requested microloan amount.

Separate real-estate purchases and existing debt from the request because current SBA guidance identifies them as prohibited uses.

## Repayment capacity

Prepare a simple but realistic cash-flow model.

Include:

- monthly sales;
- gross margin;
- payroll;
- rent;
- utilities;
- inventory purchases;
- taxes;
- other debt; and
- proposed microloan payment.

For a startup, explain how long it may take to reach break-even and what cash supports the business during the ramp period.

## Intermediary variation

Microloan intermediaries set many borrower-facing terms and credit requirements.

Expect differences in:

- interest rate;
- collateral;
- personal guarantees;
- required business training;
- application forms;
- minimum credit history;
- geographic service area; and
- technical-assistance expectations.

Compare authorized intermediaries where more than one serves the business's area.

## Technical assistance

SBA's Microloan model pairs lending intermediaries with management and technical assistance capabilities.

Use that support to strengthen areas such as:

- bookkeeping;
- cash-flow forecasting;
- pricing;
- business planning;
- marketing;
- inventory management; and
- loan-readiness documentation.

Required training should be treated as part of the financing process rather than a procedural annoyance.

## Equipment purchase

For equipment, prepare:

- vendor quote;
- total installed cost;
- expected useful life;
- expected revenue/productivity impact;
- maintenance costs; and
- other cash needed to make the equipment usable.

Avoid exhausting all liquidity on the asset purchase if the business still needs working capital to operate it.

## Inventory purchase

For inventory financing, document:

- products/categories;
- unit cost;
- expected selling price;
- turnover period;
- supplier terms;
- seasonality; and
- risk of unsold/obsolete inventory.

Loan repayment should not depend on an unrealistic assumption that every item sells immediately at full price.

## Startup readiness

For startups, provide:

- founder/management experience;
- business plan;
- startup-cost estimate;
- licenses/permits;
- market evidence;
- opening cash-flow forecast;
- owner's contribution; and
- contingency plan.

The intermediary may have its own startup criteria and training requirements.

## Existing-business readiness

For an operating business, keep current:

- financial statements;
- tax information requested by the lender;
- bank statements;
- debt schedule;
- use-of-proceeds schedule; and
- recent operating performance.

Explain material losses, late payments, or changes rather than leaving the intermediary to guess.

## Current repayment-term nuance

SBA's current direct Microloan page states a maximum repayment term of **seven years**. Some older SBA comparison material can still show six years.

When a program term differs across public pages, use the most current program-specific authoritative source and confirm the intermediary's live terms before making a financing decision.

## Evidence to retain

Retain where appropriate:

- application;
- intermediary disclosures;
- loan agreement;
- use-of-proceeds records;
- purchase receipts/invoices;
- training/technical-assistance records where relevant;
- payment history; and
- amendments or hardship arrangements.

## Scope note

This article provides general SBA Microloan readiness guidance based on current public information. It does not guarantee approval, a $50,000 loan, a seven-year term, interest rate, collateral treatment, or availability from a particular intermediary. The authorized intermediary and current SBA rules control the actual loan.
