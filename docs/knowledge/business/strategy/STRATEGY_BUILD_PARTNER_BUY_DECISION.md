# Strategy Build Partner Buy Decision

When a capability gap blocks the strategy, the standing question is whether to build it internally, partner for it, or buy it outright. Each path carries a different mix of control, speed, and cost, and each fails in a characteristic way: builds arrive late, partners leak value and independence, acquisitions dilute and culturally collide. This article sets the decision discipline: how to frame the capability requirement, score the three routes against explicit criteria including exit considerations, and structure the chosen route so its known failure mode is managed rather than discovered.

## Scope

This article covers the structured decision among build, partner, and acquire for capability gaps of strategic significance. It applies to technology, market access, operations, and talent capabilities. It addresses decision framing, comparative scoring, route selection, structuring, and post-decision review. It does not cover deal valuation or acquisition integration mechanics, which follow separate articles on valuation and culture due diligence, nor routine supplier procurement.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

**Specify the capability, not the solution.** Write the requirement as the ability to perform an outcome-defining activity at a stated level: "process claims at defined cost and accuracy," not "acquire a claims software vendor." A capability framing keeps all three routes genuinely comparable; a solution framing pre-commits the answer.

**Assess strategic centrality first.** Ask whether the capability is core to where advantage will live — through the core-capability screen — or context: necessary but not differentiating. Centrality sets the control requirement. Core capabilities tolerate neither dependency nor easy imitation, which biases toward build or full acquisition; context capabilities favor partnership or purchase on best-available terms. Most decision errors come from mislabeling context as core and buying control of things that only needed contracting.

**Score the three routes on fixed criteria.** Use a consistent set: time to capability, honoring urgency; total cost of ownership over the realistic horizon, including integration, governance, and switching; control sufficiency for the centrality finding; capability quality achievable by each route, since building world-class internally is slow and buying average companies is easy; organizational absorption capacity, because concurrent builds and integrations fail; and exit reversibility, meaning what it costs to undo the choice. Score with evidence notes, not adjectives, and record who scored what.

**Weight exit explicitly.** Builds can be stopped with sunk cost and redeployment; partnerships end at contract with re-sourcing obligations; acquisitions end only through divestiture at uncertain multiples. Reversibility is worth real value when the capability's strategic centrality might change — which, over multi-year horizons, it often does. A route that scores marginally better on speed but is effectively irreversible against uncertain centrality deserves heightened scrutiny.

**Run the partnering-specific checks.** Partnership viability depends on alignment of interest, not warmth: does the partner earn more when you succeed; who owns improvements and data created in the work; what happens at renewal when your dependency has deepened; and is the partner also serving competitors, with what separation? Score negotiation leverage honestly — dependency reverses over the life of a partnership as switching costs accumulate on your side.

**Run the acquisition-specific checks.** Verify the capability actually transfers: does it live in people who can leave, in systems that interoperate, or in contracts that assign cleanly — the embeddedness question from the capability screen. Price cultural distance per the culture due-diligence review. Confirm the capability is cheaper to buy than to rent through a partnership on total cost, including retention packages and integration drag.

**Structure against the known failure mode.** For build: staged gates with kill criteria and a named challenger, per the staged-investment discipline. For partner: contract terms covering data ownership, improvement allocation, exit assistance, and renewal caps on price escalation; plus a maintained re-sourcing plan so dependence never becomes absolute. For buy: retention structures for the people who carry the capability, integration sequencing that protects the acquired routine, and a defined synergy tracking baseline.

**Review the decision as a portfolio.** Annually, examine the mix of routes chosen across the last several years. A firm that only ever builds is underusing markets; one that only ever buys is accumulating integration debt and paying control premiums for context capabilities. Rebalance is a legitimate strategic output.

## Controls

- **Capability statement gate.** Decisions papers must present the outcome-framed capability statement before route comparison; papers starting from a named target or vendor are returned.
- **Centrality finding required.** Each decision cites a core-versus-context determination from the capability screen, with its evidence dossier.
- **Scoring audit.** Comparative scores carry evidence notes and named scorers; finance validates cost-of-ownership inputs.
- **Exit costing.** Every route decision includes a written reversal cost estimate, reviewed for honesty by finance.
- **Concurrent-load check.** New builds and integrations are admitted only against measured organizational absorption capacity.
- **Post-decision review.** Eighteen months after execution, actual time, cost, and capability level are compared with the decision paper, and the variance is fed back into scoring calibration.

## Validation evidence

Evidence includes: decision papers with capability statements, centrality citations, and scored comparisons; contract artifacts showing the partner-failure-mode protections; retention and integration plans for acquisitions; and the post-decision review record with variances. The most telling evidence over time is the calibration record: whether the firm's predicted times and costs for each route have converged on actuals, and whether route-mix rebalancing has actually occurred when reviews demanded it.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Answer-first papers.** A target company or friendly vendor identified before framing. Correct with the capability statement gate.
- **Centrality inflation.** Context labeled core to justify control purchases. Correct by requiring the capability dossier and independent challenge.
- **Exit amnesia.** Reversal costs never estimated or ignored in scoring. Correct through the exit costing control.
- **Absorption overload.** Three integrations and two builds launched the same year, all failing slowly. Correct via the concurrent-load check.
- **Partnership drift.** Renewals auto-extended while dependency deepened and re-sourcing plans decayed. Correct with mandatory re-sourcing plan maintenance.

## Limitations

Scoring compresses uncertainty: build times and acquisition synergies are systematically underestimated industry-wide, and honest ranges are wide. Partner behavior changes with ownership or strategy shifts outside your control. The framework assumes the capability can be described precisely; genuinely novel capabilities resist specification, which argues for reversible routes and small commitments until definition improves.
