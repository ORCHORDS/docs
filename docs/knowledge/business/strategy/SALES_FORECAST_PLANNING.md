# Sales Forecast Planning

## Purpose

A sales forecast converts market, pricing, channel, and capacity assumptions into a time-phased estimate of future sales. It should make the logic behind expected revenue visible enough that management can test, revise, and compare it with actual results.

A forecast is an estimate, not a guarantee. It should support decisions without disguising uncertainty.

## Forecast building blocks

For each material product, service, customer segment, or channel, identify where practical:

- expected unit or transaction volume;
- expected selling price or average revenue per sale;
- timing and seasonality;
- channel mix and conversion assumptions;
- customer acquisition, retention, renewal, or repeat-purchase assumptions;
- capacity constraints that could limit fulfillment;
- contractual or pipeline evidence where available; and
- material dependencies such as product launch, staffing, regulatory approval, inventory, or partner readiness.

Separate historical observations from forward-looking assumptions so users can see where the forecast changes from demonstrated performance.

## Forecast methods

Current SBA growth guidance notes that a business forecast can use quantitative analysis, intuitive judgment, or both. The method should fit the available evidence and maturity of the business.

Useful approaches can include:

- historical run-rate or trend analysis for established activity;
- pipeline-weighted estimates where opportunities have defined stages;
- unit-volume multiplied by expected price;
- capacity-based forecasts where production or service delivery is the limiting factor;
- market or channel assumptions for a new offering with little historical data; and
- scenario ranges when uncertainty is material.

Do not mix methods without documenting how overlaps or double counting are prevented.

## Planning workflow

1. Define the forecast period and decision it is intended to support.
2. Segment the forecast enough to expose different drivers without creating unnecessary complexity.
3. Record each material volume, price, timing, and conversion assumption.
4. Check the forecast against operational capacity, inventory, staffing, marketing, and customer-support plans.
5. Translate sales timing into expected cash-collection timing separately when payment is delayed.
6. Prepare a realistic base case and, where useful, downside and upside scenarios.
7. Reconcile the sales forecast with operating expenses, working capital, cash flow, break-even analysis, and funding requirements.
8. Review actual results against forecast and classify material variance by cause.
9. Refresh the forecast when evidence or operating conditions change rather than preserving an outdated target for appearance's sake.

## New or limited-history businesses

A business without reliable sales history should make uncertainty especially visible. Use externally supportable market information, testable conversion assumptions, known capacity, signed commitments where available, and explicit ranges.

Avoid presenting top-down market size as though it were an achievable sales forecast. A large addressable market does not demonstrate the business's ability to acquire, serve, and retain a corresponding share of customers.

## Established businesses

For an established business, compare the forecast with prior periods while accounting for known structural changes such as:

- price changes;
- lost or newly contracted customers;
- channel expansion or contraction;
- product launches or retirements;
- capacity changes;
- economic or seasonal changes; and
- material changes in marketing investment or conversion performance.

SBA business-plan guidance recommends financial projections that explain the underlying assumptions and align with funding requests. Sales forecasts should therefore connect visibly to projected income, cash flow, and capital needs rather than exist as an isolated revenue target.

## Variance review

When actual sales differ materially from forecast, identify whether the variance came from:

- volume;
- price or discounting;
- mix;
- conversion rate;
- timing;
- churn or retention;
- capacity or fulfillment limitations; or
- an assumption that was not supported by evidence.

Use that analysis to improve the next forecast. Do not automatically roll an unmet target into later periods without evidence that the delayed sales remain achievable.

## Evidence to retain

For a material forecast, retain where appropriate:

- forecast version and period;
- source data and assumptions;
- pipeline or contract evidence used;
- market or historical data used;
- scenario definitions;
- capacity constraints;
- forecast-versus-actual variance analysis; and
- date and owner of the latest assumption refresh.

## Scope note

This article describes reusable sales-forecasting practices. It does not provide investment guidance, accounting advice, or assurance that forecast sales or revenue will occur.
