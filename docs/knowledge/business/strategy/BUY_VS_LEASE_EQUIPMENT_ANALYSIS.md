# Buy vs Lease Equipment Analysis

## Purpose

A buy-versus-lease decision should compare the full economic and operational effect of acquiring an asset, not only the monthly payment. The right choice depends on expected useful life, utilization, cash availability, financing cost, maintenance, flexibility, obsolescence risk, tax and accounting treatment, and the terms of the specific lease or loan.

The U.S. Small Business Administration currently advises businesses to first identify the assets they actually need and then compare buying and leasing based on their circumstances. SBA notes that leasing can reduce upfront cash or credit needs and sometimes include maintenance, while purchasing can provide ownership, balance-sheet value, and lower lifetime cost in many cases.

## Decision inputs

Document at least the following before comparing alternatives:

- asset description and business purpose;
- expected acquisition date and required service life;
- expected utilization and capacity requirement;
- purchase price and installation or commissioning costs;
- available cash contribution;
- financing rate, fees, term, collateral, and guarantees for a financed purchase;
- lease term, deposits, periodic payments, escalation clauses, and other fees;
- maintenance, insurance, taxes, consumables, and operating costs;
- residual or resale value assumptions;
- early-termination, return-condition, excess-use, and buyout terms;
- expected technology or market obsolescence;
- downtime and replacement risk; and
- tax and accounting treatment requiring professional confirmation.

## Compare total cash impact

Build a time-based cash-flow comparison rather than comparing only purchase price with lease payment.

For a purchase, include:

1. upfront cash or down payment;
2. loan principal and interest where financed;
3. acquisition, installation, and commissioning costs;
4. maintenance, insurance, and ownership costs;
5. expected replacement or major repair costs; and
6. expected resale or residual value at the end of the analysis period.

For a lease, include:

1. deposits and initiation fees;
2. all scheduled lease payments;
3. payment escalations;
4. maintenance not included in the lease;
5. insurance and other pass-through charges;
6. excess-use, damage, return, or restoration charges;
7. early-termination exposure; and
8. end-of-term purchase or renewal costs if relevant.

Use the same analysis period and operating assumptions for both alternatives.

## When leasing may fit better

Leasing may be attractive when:

- preserving cash or borrowing capacity is important;
- the asset is needed quickly but only for a defined period;
- technology changes rapidly and obsolescence risk is high;
- the organization wants to test equipment before committing to long-term ownership;
- maintenance or replacement services included in the lease have meaningful value; or
- demand is uncertain and flexibility is more valuable than lowest lifetime cost.

SBA cautions that lifetime leasing cost is commonly higher than buying and that replacing equipment after the lease ends can create additional cost.

## When buying may fit better

Buying may be attractive when:

- the asset will be used for a long period;
- utilization is expected to remain high;
- ownership flexibility is important;
- the asset has meaningful residual value;
- maintenance and replacement risk can be managed internally; and
- available cash or financing does not create unacceptable liquidity pressure.

SBA notes that buying generally requires more cash or credit upfront but can reduce lifetime cost compared with leasing.

## Financing context

A purchase does not have to be funded entirely with cash. SBA's current 7(a) program permits eligible proceeds to be used for machinery and equipment, and the 504 program can support qualifying long-term machinery and equipment. Program eligibility, terms, useful-life requirements, lender underwriting, and collateral expectations must be checked against the current program rules rather than assumed.

## Contract review

Lease terminology and accounting treatment can vary. Do not rely on labels such as "operating" or "capital" lease without confirming the actual contractual, accounting, and tax treatment applicable to the transaction.

Before signing a material lease, review:

- ownership and title;
- permitted use and location;
- maintenance responsibility;
- insurance requirements;
- default and cure provisions;
- termination rights and penalties;
- assignment and subleasing restrictions;
- return condition and inspection process;
- renewal terms; and
- purchase options or residual-value commitments.

## Evidence record

A reusable decision record can include:

- business requirement and alternatives considered;
- analysis period;
- major cost assumptions;
- utilization and useful-life assumptions;
- financing or lease quotations;
- total expected cash outflow by alternative;
- residual-value assumptions;
- operational and obsolescence risks;
- tax/accounting review status; and
- selected option with decision rationale.

This article is a decision-analysis framework, not tax, accounting, financing, or legal advice. Tax deductions, capitalization, depreciation, lease accounting, lender terms, and program eligibility depend on current rules and the specific transaction.
