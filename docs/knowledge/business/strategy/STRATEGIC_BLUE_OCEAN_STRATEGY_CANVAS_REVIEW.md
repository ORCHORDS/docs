# Strategic Blue Ocean Strategy Canvas Review

## Scope

This article describes how to use the Blue Ocean Strategy Canvas as a structured review aid for an existing strategy, not as a discovery tool. The framework was introduced by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne in a series of *Harvard Business Review* articles starting in 2004 and elaborated in their 2005 book *Blue Ocean Strategy*. The article treats the canvas as a planning aid that supports review and challenge of a strategy, not as a definitive recommendation that the strategy should be repositioned into a blue ocean.

The canvas records, for the organization and for its principal competitors, the score on a set of competing factors that the industry takes for granted. The review uses the canvas to surface where the organization's profile converges with competitors (a "red ocean" pattern of head-to-head competition) and where it diverges (a candidate "blue ocean" pattern of differentiation and low cost). The canvas does not by itself create differentiation or low cost; it visualizes a profile that the strategy is meant to deliver.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's competitive and capability articles. The canvas is a review aid; the organization's competitive position and capability are separate artifacts that should reconcile to the canvas rather than be derived from it.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Using the canvas as a review aid proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why the canvas is being reviewed, who will use it, and what decision it informs. A canvas used to challenge an existing strategy is different from a canvas used to scope a new business.
2. **Confirm the industry frame.** Identify the industry whose competing factors the canvas will use. The industry frame determines the list of competing factors; a loose frame produces an unfocused canvas.
3. **List the industry's competing factors.** Identify the factors the industry competes on and for which buyers compare offers: price, quality, features, service level, brand, distribution, and so on. The list should be long enough to capture the industry but not so long that it includes factors no buyer cares about.
4. **Score each competing factor for the organization.** For each factor, record the organization's current score on a documented scale (often 0 to 10 or low–high). The score must be supported by evidence (operational metrics, customer research, third-party recognition), not by sponsor preference.
5. **Score each competing factor for the principal competitors.** Use the same scale and the same evidence standard. Where evidence is unavailable, document the limitation.
6. **Plot the profiles.** Plot the organization's profile and the principal competitors' profiles on the same canvas. The visual pattern is the diagnostic: a profile that overlaps the competitors is a candidate for differentiation; a profile that is sparse or scattered is a candidate for refocusing.
7. **Surface the implications.** State where the profile suggests differentiation is real, where it is illusory, where it is missing, and where it overlaps a competitor. The implications should be specific enough to be reflected in the operating plan and the capital plan.

The review should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the industry frame, the competing factors, the organization's profile, the principal competitors' profiles, and the implications. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying canvas.

## Controls

- **Industry frame frozen before listing factors.** The industry frame is written and approved before the competing factor list is finalized. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Evidence required for each score.** Each score is supported by named sources, dates, and reviewers. A score without evidence is provisional and surfaced in the review.
- **Consistent rater set.** The same set of raters scores each factor. Ad hoc scoring by different people produces profiles that are not comparable.
- **Competitor evidence.** Scores for competitors are based on observable evidence (publicly reported metrics, customer research, third-party reports), not on sponsor estimation alone.
- **Cadenced review.** The canvas is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. Material changes in competitor profile trigger off-cycle review.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the industry frame, the factor list, and the scores. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the canvas from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the industry frame, and the approving authority;
- the competing factor list with the rationale for inclusion;
- the organization's score on each factor with source, date, and reviewer;
- the competitors' scores on each factor with source, date, and reviewer;
- the visual canvas or its data table;
- the implications tied to the operating plan and the capital plan;
- the variance between planned and actual at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the minutes of the canvas review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes comparison against other planning artifacts. If the canvas implies differentiation on a factor but the operating metrics show the factor is at parity with competitors, the canvas is detached from the evidence and must be corrected before approval.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Industry frame drift.** The industry defined at the start of the cycle widens or narrows mid-cycle to make the profile look better. Correct by freezing the frame and treating redefinition as an amendment.
- **Score without evidence.** A factor is scored at a level the rater cannot defend. Correct by surfacing provisional scores at the review and requiring evidence within a stated horizon.
- **Competitor caricature.** Competitors are scored lower than the evidence supports in order to flatter the organization's profile. Correct by sourcing competitor scores from observable evidence and by requiring dual reviewers.
- **Factor inflation.** The factor list is long enough that the organization can claim a high score on at least one. Correct by requiring the factor list to be approved by a role separate from the sponsor and by linking factors to customer research.
- **Hidden parity.** The canvas shows differentiation but the customer research shows customers perceive parity. Correct by reconciling the canvas to customer evidence before approval.
- **No plan reconciliation.** The canvas's implications are not reflected in the capital plan, the operating budget, or the headcount plan. Correct by reconciling before approval.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the canvas.

## Limitations

The Blue Ocean Strategy Canvas is a review aid. It does not by itself create differentiation or low cost and does not validate that any particular strategy is correct. The Kim and Mauborgne writing popularized the framework through *Harvard Business Review* in 2004 and a 2005 book; the framework has been widely adopted but its application to specific industries requires primary research on customers and competitors that the canvas does not provide.

The canvas does not address the organization's capabilities (that is the subject of the capability heatmap article) or the venture's strategic fit (that is the subject of the Stevenson heuristics article). The three lenses (canvas, capability heatmap, Stevenson heuristics) are complementary, not redundant.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
