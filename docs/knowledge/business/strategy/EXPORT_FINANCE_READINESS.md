# Export Finance Readiness

## Purpose

Export growth can create financing needs before cash from international customers arrives. Inventory, supplier deposits, production, freight, insurance, documentation, and longer payment terms can all increase working-capital pressure even when an export order is profitable.

This article provides a reusable planning framework for evaluating export-finance readiness and current U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) export-financing paths. It is distinct from export market-entry planning: the focus here is funding structure, evidence, timing, and repayment capacity.

## Start with the financing need

Before selecting a product, define what the financing must cover and for how long. Examples include:

- pre-shipment production and supplier costs;
- inventory required for an export order;
- foreign accounts receivable;
- contract or purchase-order fulfillment;
- equipment or facility investment supporting international sales;
- market-entry expenses tied to export development;
- short-term cash gaps created by international payment terms; and
- refinancing or working-capital needs directly connected to trade activity.

Separate one-time investment from revolving working-capital needs. A term loan and a line of credit solve different timing problems.

## Readiness evidence

A business considering export financing should be able to produce current, internally consistent information such as:

- recent financial statements;
- cash-flow forecast;
- accounts-receivable aging;
- accounts-payable aging;
- inventory reporting where relevant;
- existing debt obligations;
- export pipeline, contracts, purchase orders, or credible sales evidence;
- expected payment terms and collection timing;
- customer and country concentration;
- intended use of funds; and
- repayment assumptions.

SBA's current 7(a) Working Capital Pilot guidance specifically identifies timely and accurate financial statements, receivable and payable agings, and inventory reports as important capabilities for businesses using that working-capital structure.

## Current SBA export-financing paths

### Export Express

SBA describes Export Express as a streamlined export-financing option under which participating lenders make the credit decision. Current SBA information states that Export Express loans can be up to $500,000 and can be structured as term loans or revolving lines of credit, subject to program and lender requirements.

Use this path as a comparison point when the required amount is smaller and speed or a lender-delegated process is important. Do not assume that the published maximum equals an available approval amount for a specific borrower.

### Export Working Capital Program

The Export Working Capital Program (EWCP) is designed for businesses that can generate export sales and need working capital to support those sales. Current SBA material lists a maximum loan amount of $5 million and a maximum SBA guaranty of 90%.

EWCP can be particularly relevant where financing is tied to export inventory and foreign accounts receivable. The actual borrowing base, collateral structure, documentation, and availability depend on the lender and program requirements.

### 7(a) Working Capital Pilot

The Working Capital Pilot (WCP) supports domestic and/or export working-capital needs through monitored lines of credit. SBA states that it can be structured as a transaction-based line for specific contracts or as an asset-based line with advances against inventory and receivables.

Current SBA information lists a maximum loan size of $5 million. A business should compare whether one combined domestic/export facility is operationally preferable to an export-specific structure.

### International Trade loan

The 7(a) International Trade loan program supports eligible small businesses entering or expanding in international markets, and businesses affected by import competition. SBA states that proceeds can support fixed assets, working capital for export transactions, and certain refinancing or competitive-position investments.

Current SBA material lists a maximum loan amount of $5 million and a maximum guaranty of 90%, subject to program requirements. SBA also published program updates effective May 1, 2026 intended to ease participation burdens and expand lender use of the program.

## Comparison criteria

Evaluate financing options using consistent criteria rather than selecting the program with the largest published maximum.

Compare:

- **use of funds** — whether the financing matches inventory, receivables, fixed assets, contract costs, or another need;
- **facility type** — term debt, revolving line, transaction line, or asset-based line;
- **timing** — when funds are needed relative to production, shipment, invoicing, and collection;
- **borrowing base** — what receivables, inventory, or other assets can support advances;
- **currency and payment risk** — whether foreign-customer payment terms change the cash-conversion cycle;
- **guaranty structure** — SBA guaranty percentage is a lender-risk feature, not a promise that the borrower will receive the maximum amount;
- **interest and fees** — lender terms and applicable program limits;
- **collateral and guarantees** — requirements vary by facility and circumstances;
- **reporting burden** — frequency of borrowing-base, aging, inventory, or other lender reports;
- **repayment capacity** — whether downside scenarios still support obligations; and
- **exit or refinance plan** — what happens when the export contract ends or the facility matures.

## Cash-conversion model

For each major export scenario, model the sequence:

1. customer order or contract;
2. supplier deposits and production spending;
3. inventory build;
4. shipment and freight costs;
5. invoice creation;
6. customer payment terms;
7. expected collection date; and
8. loan repayment or revolving-line reduction.

The peak financing requirement is often determined by the maximum cumulative cash gap, not the accounting profit on the sale.

## Payment and customer risk

Export financing does not remove commercial or country risk. Consider where relevant:

- advance payment versus open-account terms;
- letters of credit or documentary collection;
- export credit insurance;
- customer concentration;
- country, sanctions, legal, and currency risk;
- dispute and returns exposure; and
- the effect of delayed collection on borrowing availability.

SBA's current trade-finance material points exporters to additional federal resources, including the Export-Import Bank of the United States (EXIM) and the International Trade Administration's Trade Finance Guide.

## Decision record

For a material export-financing decision, retain:

- financing need and timing;
- programs or lender structures considered;
- financial evidence used;
- export contracts or pipeline assumptions;
- borrowing-base assumptions where applicable;
- expected and downside cash-flow scenarios;
- lender terms available at the time;
- decision rationale; and
- owner and review date.

Refresh the analysis when order volume, customer terms, exchange rates, supplier terms, lender conditions, or program rules materially change.

## Important boundaries

Published SBA maximums and guaranty percentages are program parameters, not approvals or commitments to lend. Eligibility, underwriting, collateral, documentation, pricing, availability, and final terms depend on the borrower, lender, transaction, and current program requirements.

This article does not recommend borrowing merely because an SBA-supported program exists. A business should compare debt service, dilution-free alternatives, internal cash, customer deposits, supplier terms, insurance, and other financing structures before committing.
