# Funding Source Comparison

## Purpose

Funding-source comparison helps a business choose capital that fits the purpose, duration, risk, ownership model, and repayment capacity of the need being funded. The cheapest-looking option is not always the best fit, and capital that is available quickly can create obligations that outlast the benefit it financed.

Start with the amount, timing, and use of funds. Then compare realistic sources on consistent criteria.

## Current SBA funding categories

Current U.S. Small Business Administration guidance identifies common routes such as:

- **self-funding**, where owners use their own resources and retain control while bearing the financial risk;
- **loans**, including conventional and SBA-guaranteed lending, which create repayment obligations;
- **investment capital**, including equity or debt/equity investment through private investors or SBA-licensed Small Business Investment Companies (SBICs); and
- **other opportunities**, such as crowdfunding, surety-bond support, and limited grant programs where the business and activity qualify.

SBA specifically notes that its grant programs are limited and that SBA does not generally provide grants simply to start or expand an ordinary business. Do not build a funding plan around an assumed grant without verifying a current eligible program.

## Comparison criteria

Evaluate realistic funding sources against factors such as:

- amount available;
- timing and certainty of access;
- total cost of capital;
- interest or expected investor return;
- repayment schedule;
- ownership dilution;
- governance or control rights;
- collateral or guarantee requirements;
- covenants and operating restrictions;
- reporting obligations;
- transaction and professional-service costs;
- effect on future borrowing or investment capacity;
- maturity or investment horizon;
- flexibility to prepay, refinance, or raise later capital; and
- consequences if expected growth or cash flow does not occur.

Use the same time horizon when comparing alternatives so a low initial payment does not hide a higher lifecycle cost.

## Match source to use of funds

The duration and risk of the capital should make sense for the asset or activity being funded.

Examples:

- a short seasonal working-capital gap may require a different structure from a long-lived property or equipment purchase;
- uncertain early-stage research may be poorly matched to rigid near-term debt service if the business has no dependable repayment capacity;
- permanent ownership capital can absorb business risk differently from debt but changes ownership economics and potentially decision rights; and
- a grant or program-specific award should be used only for eligible purposes and under its actual conditions.

Avoid funding a long-term requirement with capital that must be repaid before the funded activity can reasonably generate the required cash.

## Self-funding

Self-funding can preserve ownership control and avoid external underwriting, but it concentrates risk on the owner.

Review:

- the amount the owner can actually afford to commit;
- emergency and personal liquidity that should remain separate;
- whether retirement or other protected assets are being placed at risk;
- whether owner funding is debt, equity, or another documented contribution; and
- whether relying on owner capital delays recognition that the business model requires external funding.

SBA cautions business owners not to spend more than they can afford and notes that using retirement funds can involve fees, penalties, and retirement consequences.

## Loans and credit

Debt can preserve ownership while requiring repayment regardless of whether projected growth occurs.

Compare:

- principal amount;
- interest basis and effective cost;
- fees;
- maturity;
- payment frequency;
- collateral;
- guarantees;
- covenants;
- variable-rate exposure;
- prepayment terms; and
- debt-service capacity under downside scenarios.

Current SBA loan guidance notes that program and lender eligibility varies and generally depends on factors including business purpose, ability to repay, and applicable program requirements. An SBA guarantee supports the lender; it is not a grant to the borrower and does not remove the repayment obligation.

## Investor and SBIC capital

Equity or debt/equity investment can provide capital without the same fixed repayment profile as ordinary loans, but it can change ownership, governance, economics, and future decisions.

For investor capital, review:

- ownership percentage or conversion rights;
- valuation assumptions;
- voting or consent rights;
- board or information rights;
- liquidation or preference rights;
- future financing provisions;
- transfer restrictions;
- founder or management obligations; and
- expected investment horizon or exit assumptions.

SBA-licensed SBICs are privately owned investment companies; SBA does not invest directly into the small business through the SBIC program. Individual SBICs have their own investment profiles and eligibility requirements.

## Grants and program-specific funding

Treat grant funding as program-specific rather than as a generic category of free capital.

Verify:

- the current sponsoring authority;
- eligible applicant type;
- eligible use of funds;
- application period;
- matching requirements;
- reporting obligations;
- milestones or deliverables;
- reimbursement timing; and
- conditions that can require repayment or create other consequences.

SBA states that its small-business grant opportunities are limited and concentrated in specific areas such as research and development, entrepreneurship support, manufacturing initiatives, and exporting programs.

Do not rely on third-party advertisements claiming guaranteed government grant money without confirming the program on an authoritative government source.

## Crowdfunding and other sources

Crowdfunding, credit cards, supplier financing, revenue-linked structures, and other alternatives can have materially different legal, platform, disclosure, fee, and repayment characteristics.

Compare them using the same decision framework rather than treating an unfamiliar structure as automatically more flexible.

Where securities, consumer, tax, or other regulated obligations may apply, obtain qualified advice for the specific structure and jurisdiction.

## Scenario analysis

Test each serious funding option against at least the base case and a realistic downside case.

Questions include:

- Can required payments still be made if revenue arrives later than expected?
- What happens if interest rates, costs, or exchange rates rise?
- Is additional capital likely to be needed before maturity?
- Will covenants restrict the actions needed during a downturn?
- Does dilution become materially different if later financing occurs at a lower valuation?
- Could the funding source force an asset sale, ownership change, or operational restriction at the wrong time?

Do not compare sources only under the forecast used to justify raising the money.

## Decision record

For a material funding decision, retain where appropriate:

- amount and intended use;
- required timing;
- sources considered;
- offers or term sheets reviewed;
- cost comparison;
- dilution or ownership impact;
- collateral and covenant impact;
- downside analysis;
- liquidity and debt-service assessment;
- decision owner and approval; and
- reasons alternatives were rejected.
