# Strategic Capability Heatmap Rigor Vs Differentiation

## Scope

This article describes how to build a heatmap that maps organizational capabilities against two dimensions, rigor and differentiation, and how to record the resulting picture in the appendix of a business plan. The conceptual grounding is the core-competence framing introduced by C. K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel in their 1990 Harvard Business Review article. The article treats that framing as a planning aid; it does not validate the organization's specific capabilities nor does it prove that any particular configuration is correct. The heatmap is a tool for surfacing where the organization can compete, where it can merely keep up, and where it is at risk of falling behind.

Rigor refers to the ability to execute a capability consistently, at cost, at speed, and with predictable quality. Differentiation refers to the ability to deliver a capability in a way customers and competitors recognize as distinct. A capability that is rigorous but not differentiated is operating excellence; a capability that is differentiated but not rigorous is a fragile advantage; a capability that is both is a true core competence; a capability that is neither is a candidate for outsourcing, partnering, or pruning.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's `STRATEGIC_AMBIDEXTERITY_EXPLOIT_EXPLORE_BUDGET_SPLIT` article (which assumes the organization can run both sides of the ambidexterity question) and `STRATEGIC_PORTFOLIO_BCG_GROWTH_SHARE_MATRIX_REGISTER` article (which classifies business units, not capabilities). The two lenses (capability heatmap and BCG matrix) are complementary.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Constructing the capability heatmap proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why the heatmap is being built, who will use it, and what decision it informs. Without this preamble the heatmap is decoration rather than a decision input.
2. **Inventory the candidate capabilities.** List the capabilities the strategy depends on: the things the organization must do well to deliver the plan. Resist the temptation to list aspirations; list capabilities actually in use.
3. **Define rigor and differentiation operationally.** Replace the abstract labels with measurable proxies. Rigor might be measured by defect rates, cycle time, cost variance, on-time delivery, employee turnover, or audit findings. Differentiation might be measured by customer preference data, win rate against named competitors, willingness-to-pay evidence, or third-party recognition. Whatever the proxies, freeze them at the start of the cycle.
4. **Score each capability on both dimensions.** Use a consistent rubric. Record the evidence, the date, the source, and the reviewer behind each score. A heatmap cell without evidence is a finding to address, not a settled judgment.
5. **Classify each capability into one of four zones.** Quadrant 1 (high rigor, high differentiation) is the candidate core competence. Quadrant 2 (high rigor, low differentiation) is operational hygiene. Quadrant 3 (low rigor, low differentiation) is a candidate to prune, partner, or outsource. Quadrant 4 (low rigor, high differentiation) is fragile advantage that needs investment in rigor before it can be exploited.
6. **For each zone, state the implication for the plan.** State which capabilities the plan will defend, which it will invest in, which it will partner on, and which it will prune. The implications should be specific enough to be reflected in the operating budget and the capital plan.
7. **Surface capability dependencies.** Note where one capability supports another (for example, an analytical capability that supports multiple product capabilities) and where capabilities compete for the same scarce talent or infrastructure. The heatmap should reconcile to the broader plan rather than exist in isolation.

The appendix should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the zones, the implications, and the principal risks of misclassification. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying tables.

## Controls

- **Rubric frozen before scoring.** The rigor and differentiation proxies are written and approved before any capability is scored. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Evidence required for each cell.** Each heatmap cell is supported by a named source, a date, and a reviewer. A score without evidence is treated as provisional and surfaced in the review.
- **Consistent rater set.** The same set of raters scores each capability. Ad hoc scoring by different people produces cells that are not comparable.
- **Cadenced refresh.** The heatmap is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. The review should record the change since the prior review and the cause.
- **Plan reconciliation.** The heatmap's implications reconcile to the capital plan, the operating budget, and the headcount plan. Material variance is a finding, not a footnote.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the rubric, the scores, and the implications. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the heatmap from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the rubric for rigor and differentiation, and the approving authority;
- the inventory of capabilities with sponsor and use;
- the scoring of each capability on both dimensions with source, date, and reviewer;
- the zone classification for each capability;
- the implications for the plan tied to each zone;
- the dependency map across capabilities;
- the variance between planned and actual scores at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the reconciliation to the capital plan, the operating budget, and the headcount plan;
- the minutes of the capability review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes checking the heatmap against other artifacts. If the heatmap implies investment in a capability but the capital plan and operating budget contain no spend on it, the heatmap is detached from the plan and should be corrected before approval.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Aspiration inventory.** The capability list describes what the organization wishes it could do rather than what it does. Correct by sourcing the list from the operating plan and removing items not actually exercised.
- **Quadrant drift without investment.** Capabilities are placed in Quadrant 1 but neither rigor nor differentiation has improved materially since the prior cycle. Correct by tying Quadrant 1 placement to evidence of recent, sustained investment.
- **Quadrant 4 never repaired.** Quadrant 4 capabilities (differentiated but not rigorous) remain year after year with no investment in rigor. Correct by surfacing Quadrant 4 capability duration at the capability review and committing to either invest or withdraw.
- **Quadrant 3 never pruned.** Quadrant 3 capabilities (neither rigorous nor differentiated) remain funded because they are entrenched. Correct by tying Quadrant 3 placement to a documented decision to partner, outsource, or prune within a stated horizon.
- **Hidden talent dependencies.** Multiple capabilities share the same scarce talent and the heatmap treats them as independent. Correct by mapping talent dependencies explicitly and reconciling with the headcount plan.
- **Proxies chosen to flatter.** Rigor proxies are chosen because they are easy to meet and differentiation proxies are chosen because they are easy to claim. Correct by requiring the rubric to be approved by a role separate from the sponsor and by including external comparators where available.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the heatmap.

## Limitations

The capability heatmap is a planning aid. It does not validate that the organization actually possesses the capabilities it claims. It does not substitute for independent benchmarking, customer research, or competitive analysis. The article draws on the Prahalad and Hamel *core competence of the corporation* framing published in *Harvard Business Review* in 1990; the framing has been widely adopted but its application to specific organizations requires sectoral context that the framework does not provide.

The heatmap does not address the ambidexterity question (whether the organization can run exploit and explore simultaneously) and does not classify individual business units. Those are the subjects of separate leaf articles. The three lenses (capability heatmap, ambidexterity split, BCG matrix) are complementary, not redundant, and should not be merged into a single chart.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
