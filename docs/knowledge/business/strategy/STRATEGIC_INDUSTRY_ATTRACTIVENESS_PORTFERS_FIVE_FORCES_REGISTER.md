# Strategic Industry Attractiveness Porters Five Forces Register

## Scope

This article describes how to use Michael Porter's Five Forces framework as a structured register to document industry attractiveness, rather than as a definitive market analysis. The framework was introduced in Porter's 1979 Harvard Business Review article *How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy* and elaborated in his 1980 book *Competitive Strategy*. The article treats the framework as a planning aid: a way to organize evidence about rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes, and barriers to entry into a register that supports review and challenge. It does not validate that any particular industry is or is not attractive, and it does not substitute for primary research on customers, competitors, suppliers, or substitutes.

The register has five rows, one per force. Each row contains a definition, evidence, a score, and the source of the score. The register also records the implications for strategy and the cadence of review. The framework is widely cited but its application to a specific industry requires judgment; the register makes that judgment visible.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's competitive and segmentation articles, including `COMPETITOR_STRENGTH_WEAKNESS_MATRIX`, `CONCENTRATION_RATIO_HHI_MARKET_STRUCTURE`, and `COMPETITIVE_ADVANTAGE_EVIDENCE`. The Five Forces register captures industry structure; those articles capture the organization-specific competitive position within that structure.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Constructing the register proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why the industry's attractiveness is being assessed, who will use the register, and what decision it informs. Without this preamble the register is decoration.
2. **Define the industry in operational terms.** Replace the abstract label with a specific definition: the set of competitors, products, and geographies considered. The definition determines the scope of every row that follows. A loose definition produces an unfocused register.
3. **Document rivalry.** Record the number of competitors, their relative size, the rate of growth, the fixed-cost intensity, the product differentiation, the exit barriers, and the diversity of competitors. Score the force (low, medium, high) and cite the evidence for the score.
4. **Document supplier power.** Record the number and concentration of suppliers, the importance of the input to the buyer, the differentiation of the input, the switching cost, the threat of forward integration, and the cost relative to the buyer's total cost. Score and cite.
5. **Document buyer power.** Record the number and concentration of buyers, the buyer's switching cost, the buyer's information, the buyer's ability to backward integrate, the price sensitivity, and the importance of the buyer's purchase. Score and cite.
6. **Document substitutes.** Record the existence of substitute products or services, the price-performance relationship, the buyer's propensity to substitute, and the cost of switching to the substitute. Score and cite.
7. **Document barriers to entry.** Record the economies of scale, capital requirements, product differentiation, switching costs, expected retaliation, government policy, and any other barrier. Score and cite.

For each row the register should record the date of the evidence, the source, the access path, and the named reviewer. The implication column should connect the score to a strategic action: invest, harvest, divest, reposition, partner, monitor. The appendix should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the industry definition, the force scores, the implications, and the principal risks of misjudgment.

## Controls

- **Industry definition frozen before scoring.** The industry definition is written and approved before any force is scored. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Evidence required for each force.** Each force row is supported by named sources, dates, and reviewers. A score without evidence is treated as provisional and surfaced in the review.
- **Consistent rater set.** The same set of raters scores each force. Ad hoc scoring produces cells that are not comparable.
- **Implications stated.** Each force score is paired with a stated strategic implication. A high score on rivalry without an implication is a finding.
- **Cadenced review.** The register is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. Major industry events (a major merger, a regulatory change, a substitute's emergence) trigger off-cycle review.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the industry definition, the evidence, and the scoring. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the register from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the industry definition, and the approving authority;
- the rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, substitute, and entry-barrier evidence with source, date, and reviewer;
- the score for each force with the rubric used;
- the implication for each force with the planned action;
- the variance between planned and actual at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the minutes of the register review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes comparison against external checks. Where the public data on industry concentration, growth, or regulatory environment diverges from the register's evidence, the divergence is a finding.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Industry definition drift.** The industry defined at the start of the cycle widens or narrows mid-cycle to make a force look better. Correct by freezing the definition and treating redefinition as an amendment.
- **Score without evidence.** A force is scored at a level the rater cannot defend. Correct by surfacing provisional scores at the review and requiring evidence within a stated horizon.
- **Implication omitted.** A high force score has no strategic implication. Correct by tying every force score to a documented implication.
- **Rater mix.** Different raters score different forces with different rubrics. Correct by enforcing a consistent rater set and rubric.
- **Hidden substitutes.** Substitutes that have not yet emerged at scale are excluded. Correct by including plausible-but-not-yet-realized substitutes with their probability and impact.
- **Entry barriers overstated.** An industry that is in fact contestable is treated as protected. Correct by including recent entrants and their cost-to-enter evidence.
- **No plan reconciliation.** The register's implications are not reflected in the capital plan, the operating budget, or the headcount plan. Correct by reconciling before approval.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the register.

## Limitations

The Five Forces framework is a heuristic for organizing industry analysis. It does not produce a defensible forecast of industry profitability and does not validate that any particular industry is or is not attractive. Porter's 1979 HBR article and his 1980 book *Competitive Strategy* provide the canonical exposition; the framework has been widely adopted and adapted but its application to a specific industry requires primary research the framework does not provide.

The register does not substitute for the organization's competitive analysis; Porter's framework is about industry structure, not about the organization's position within that structure. The register also does not address the organization's capabilities or its capacity to execute on the implications. Those are the subjects of separate articles on competitive advantage evidence and capability heatmap. The two lenses (Five Forces and capability heatmap) are complementary, not redundant.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
