# Operating Expense Forecast

## Purpose

An operating-expense forecast converts expected business activity into a time-phased view of the costs required to run the business. It supports pricing, cash-flow, hiring, financing, and break-even decisions by making cost assumptions explicit before money is committed.

## Cost structure

Separate expenses in a way that helps explain how they behave:

- **fixed or committed costs** — costs that do not immediately change with sales volume, such as contracted rent or core software subscriptions;
- **variable costs** — costs that move with units sold, transactions, usage, or other activity drivers;
- **step costs** — costs that remain stable until capacity crosses a threshold, such as adding another employee, vehicle, or facility;
- **discretionary costs** — spending that can be adjusted without immediately stopping core operations; and
- **one-time or nonrecurring costs** — implementation, relocation, setup, professional-service, or other exceptional expenses.

Use categories consistently enough that forecast-to-actual comparisons remain meaningful from period to period.

## Forecast workflow

1. Start from the operating plan rather than simply extending prior spending.
2. Identify the activity driver for each material variable or step cost.
3. Record recurring contractual commitments using the period in which cash or expense is expected to arise.
4. Link payroll and contractor costs to the staffing plan, including realistic hiring dates and ramp periods.
5. Link marketing, fulfillment, support, infrastructure, and other scalable costs to the assumptions that cause them.
6. Separate capital expenditures from ordinary operating expenses where the financial model requires that distinction.
7. Add known one-time costs and explicitly label uncertain estimates.
8. Build expected and downside scenarios for volatile or concentrated cost drivers.
9. Reconcile the operating-expense forecast with cash-flow, funding, and break-even models.
10. Compare actual results with the forecast and update assumptions rather than mechanically carrying unexplained variances forward.

## Forecast horizon and detail

The useful level of detail depends on the decision being supported. Current SBA business-plan guidance recommends prospective financial projections for multiple years, with greater detail in the first year, including quarterly or monthly projections. A practical internal model can therefore use detailed near-term periods and broader longer-term assumptions, provided material timing differences are not hidden.

## Variance review

Review material forecast-versus-actual differences by cause, not only by amount. Common causes include:

- volume or utilization changed;
- unit price changed;
- hiring or project timing moved;
- a recurring cost was omitted or duplicated;
- foreign-exchange, commodity, usage, or other external inputs changed;
- a supposedly one-time expense became recurring; or
- the underlying operating assumption was wrong.

Record whether a variance is temporary, structural, or timing-only. Structural changes should update later forecast periods and related decisions.

## Cost-benefit decisions

SBA finance guidance describes cost-benefit analysis as a way to compare money-in and money-out and place recurring benefits or cost reductions in context. For a material new operating expense, document the expected benefit, duration, alternatives, dependencies, and conditions under which the spend should be reduced or stopped.

Do not treat an expected benefit as guaranteed revenue. Where the benefit is uncertain, use scenario ranges and decision thresholds.

## Integration with other planning

Operating-expense forecasts should remain consistent with:

- sales and demand forecasts;
- staffing and compensation plans;
- working-capital requirements;
- cash-flow forecasts;
- capital-expenditure plans;
- break-even analysis; and
- funding requests or financing capacity.

A change in one of these models should trigger a review of affected expense assumptions rather than leaving disconnected versions in circulation.

## Evidence to retain

For material forecasts, retain where appropriate:

- forecast version and period;
- source assumptions and responsible owner;
- vendor quotes or contractual amounts for major costs;
- activity-driver calculations;
- scenario assumptions;
- approvals for material discretionary spending;
- forecast-versus-actual variance explanations; and
- the date assumptions were last refreshed.
