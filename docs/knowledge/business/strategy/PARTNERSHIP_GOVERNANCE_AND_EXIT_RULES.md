# Partnership Governance and Exit Rules

A partnership governance agreement converts a shared commercial ambition into rules for authority, contribution, information, deadlock, and exit. It is relevant when independent businesses create a joint venture, alliance, consortium, or other sustained collaboration. It does not replace legal advice or the formation documents required for the chosen entity. Its planning value is to expose incompatible expectations before the parties commit customers, intellectual property, capital, or reputation.

## Scope and design

Begin by defining the collaboration’s objective, term, territory, customer group, products, and exclusions. State what each party remains free to do independently. Identify the legal structure and the documents that control if provisions conflict. Antitrust and competition rules apply to collaboration between actual or potential competitors; governance must not become a channel for improper coordination on prices, customers, output, wages, or competitively sensitive plans.

Each partner should appoint an accountable sponsor and an operational representative. The governing body’s remit should distinguish reserved matters requiring partner approval from delegated operating decisions. Reserved matters commonly include a strategy change, new debt, budget variance above an agreed level, admission of a partner, transfer of ownership, material litigation, use of jointly developed intellectual property, and termination.

## Workflow

1. **Perform partner diligence.** Verify legal identity, authority, financial capacity, sanctions or regulatory concerns, relevant litigation, delivery capability, ownership of promised assets, and conflicts. Record the source and date of each check.
2. **Define contributions and economics.** Describe cash, staff, data, facilities, intellectual property, customer access, and timing. Set valuation methods, cost allocation, invoicing, distributions, tax responsibilities, and treatment of under-delivery.
3. **Allocate decisions.** Create a matrix showing the decision, proposer, reviewers, approver, voting threshold, quorum, and escalation route. Address related-party decisions and recusals.
4. **Protect information and assets.** Classify shared information; limit use and access; define security, privacy, retention, return, and destruction duties. Specify ownership and licenses for background and newly created intellectual property.
5. **Set performance controls.** Agree deliverables, service levels, milestones, quality measures, budget controls, reporting cadence, audit rights, and remediation periods.
6. **Design change and exit before launch.** Cover change requests, deadlock, default, insolvency, change of control, regulatory prohibition, voluntary withdrawal, wind-down, customer transition, records, staff, licenses, and continuing obligations.

## Controls and validation evidence

Use a signed agreement, current authority matrix, approved annual plan, contribution ledger, issue log, meeting minutes, conflict register, and controlled repository. Restrict access to competitively sensitive information to what is necessary for the legitimate collaboration. Counsel should review the arrangement where competitors, regulated data, cross-border activity, or market allocation concerns are present.

Evidence that governance works includes timely approvals by the correct authority, reconciled partner contributions, accepted deliverables, access reviews, tested escalation contacts, and completion of corrective actions. At least annually—and after a material ownership, regulatory, or strategy change—confirm that the objective still benefits each partner, controls match actual operations, and exit obligations remain executable. A tabletop wind-down can reveal missing data-export rights, unsupported customer promises, or licenses that terminate too early.

## Failure handling

For missed milestones, preserve evidence, notify the accountable representative, assess customer and compliance impact, and invoke the agreed cure process. Do not compensate for persistent under-delivery through informal side arrangements. Escalate unresolved issues through the specified operational, executive, and governing levels.

A deadlock process should have deadlines and a defined endpoint: executive negotiation, mediation or another agreed mechanism, then a buyout or orderly termination where appropriate. If suspected illegality, data exposure, sanctions risk, or unauthorized competitive information sharing arises, stop the affected activity, preserve records, restrict further disclosure, and obtain qualified legal advice. During exit, use a controlled checklist for notices, customer continuity, money owed, asset return, access revocation, data disposition, licenses, public communications, and final acceptance. Document any surviving confidentiality, warranty, indemnity, or record-retention duty.
