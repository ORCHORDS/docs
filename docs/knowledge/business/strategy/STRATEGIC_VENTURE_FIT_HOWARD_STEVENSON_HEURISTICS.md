# Strategic Venture Fit Howard Stevenson Heuristics

## Scope

This article describes how to use a set of heuristics from Howard H. Stevenson's writing on entrepreneurship as a structured register for evaluating venture fit. Stevenson, a Harvard Business School professor, distinguished entrepreneurial management from administrative or promotional management in a series of articles and teaching materials beginning in the 1980s. The article treats Stevenson's heuristics as planning aids; they do not validate a particular venture and do not substitute for primary research on customers, costs, and competition.

The register maps each Stevenson heuristic to a plan field and records the current state of that field, the evidence supporting it, the gap to be closed, and the accountable owner. Examples of heuristics include: pursuing opportunity without regard to resources currently controlled; pursuing opportunity that is durable, systemic, and integrated; managing the venture's ability to learn from the pursuit; using resources of others in the pursuit; pursuing fit between the opportunity and the entrepreneur's personal aspirations, abilities, and tolerance for risk, ambiguity, and failure. The register records where each heuristic is met, partially met, or unmet.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's `STRATEGIC_CAPABILITY_HEATMAP_RIGOR_VS_DIFFERENTIATION` article (which evaluates capabilities) and the leaf's `STRATEGIC_VENTURE_GRAVITY_DEFINITION_VINT_BROWN` article (which evaluates the gravitational pull of a venture). The Stevenson register focuses on fit between the opportunity and the entrepreneurial team, while the other two lenses focus on capability and on the venture's mass.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Constructing the Stevenson register proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why venture fit is being assessed, who will use the register, and what decision it informs. Without this preamble the register is decoration.
2. **State the heuristics in operational terms.** Replace Stevenson's phrasing with operational definitions tied to the plan. For example, "pursuing opportunity without regard to resources currently controlled" might be operationalized as "the plan documents how milestones are met using staged commitments and partner resources, not pre-funded capacity."
3. **Map each heuristic to a plan field.** Each heuristic should point to a specific section of the plan or appendix. A heuristic that maps to no field is a finding to address.
4. **Score each heuristic.** Use a three-level rubric: met, partially met, unmet. Each score requires evidence and a named reviewer. A score without evidence is provisional.
5. **Record the gap for each unmet or partially met heuristic.** The gap should be specific, measurable, time-bounded, and assigned to an owner. A gap with no owner is a finding.
6. **Aggregate to a fit summary.** The summary states whether the venture, as currently configured, fits the heuristics. The summary is not the average of the scores; an unmet critical heuristic can outweigh several met heuristics.
7. **Surface the decision.** The register concludes with a documented decision: proceed, revise, defer, or stop. The decision records the authority, the date, the conditions, and the next review.

The appendix should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the heuristics, the scores, the gaps, and the decision. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying tables.

## Controls

- **Heuristic definitions frozen before scoring.** The operational definitions are written and approved before any heuristic is scored. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Evidence required for each score.** Each score is supported by named sources, dates, and reviewers. A score without evidence is provisional and surfaced in the review.
- **Gap ownership.** Every unmet or partially met heuristic has a named owner and a closure horizon. A gap with no owner is a finding.
- **Aggregate rule.** The aggregate fit summary is not a simple average. The rule for combining heuristic scores is documented before scoring and applied consistently.
- **Cadenced review.** The register is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. Material changes in the venture's environment trigger off-cycle review.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the definitions, the scores, and the decision. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the register from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the heuristic definitions, and the approving authority;
- the mapping of each heuristic to a plan field;
- the score for each heuristic with source, date, and reviewer;
- the gap for each unmet or partially met heuristic with owner and closure horizon;
- the aggregate fit summary with the rule used;
- the decision with authority, date, conditions, and next review;
- the variance between planned and actual at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the minutes of the register review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes comparison against other planning artifacts. If the register concludes "proceed" but the capability heatmap shows the venture lacks the capabilities to deliver the plan, the two artifacts disagree and the disagreement is a finding.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Heuristic inflation.** Heuristics are stated in operational terms that the venture can already meet, regardless of underlying reality. Correct by sourcing the operational definitions from primary research rather than from sponsor preference.
- **Score without evidence.** A heuristic is scored as met without supporting evidence. Correct by surfacing provisional scores at the review and requiring evidence within a stated horizon.
- **Gap without owner.** An unmet heuristic has no closure plan. Correct by tying each gap to a named owner and a closure horizon.
- **Average misjudgment.** The aggregate summary averages the scores and concludes "proceed" despite an unmet critical heuristic. Correct by documenting the aggregate rule before scoring and by flagging critical-vs-non-critical heuristics.
- **Heuristic drift.** A heuristic is redefined mid-cycle to make the venture fit. Correct by freezing the definitions and treating redefinition as an amendment.
- **No reconciliation to other plans.** The register's decision does not reconcile to the capital plan, the operating budget, or the headcount plan. Correct by reconciling before approval.
- **Authority defect.** The decision is made by a role without authority to commit the venture. Correct by tying the decision to the organization's delegation-of-authority policy.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the register.

## Limitations

The Stevenson heuristics are a planning aid. They do not validate that a particular venture will succeed and do not substitute for primary research on customers, costs, and competition. Stevenson's writing is a teaching synthesis developed at Harvard Business School; it has been widely adopted in entrepreneurship curricula, but the application of specific heuristics to specific ventures is by adaptation rather than direct prescription.

The register does not address the organization's capabilities (that is the subject of the capability heatmap article) or the venture's gravitational pull (that is the subject of the Vint Brown article in this leaf). The three lenses (Stevenson heuristics, capability heatmap, venture gravity) are complementary, not redundant.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
