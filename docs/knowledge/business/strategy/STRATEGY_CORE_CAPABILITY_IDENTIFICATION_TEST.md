# Strategy Core Capability Identification Test

Every strategy claims strengths, but most lists describe activities the firm performs rather than capabilities that competitors cannot easily match. The difference matters because capability claims drive what a firm builds in-house, what it dares to charge for, and which adjacencies it enters. This article defines a three-test screen for core capabilities and catalogues the common impostors — the adjacency that looks core, the asset that is valuable but imitable, and the competence that belongs to individuals rather than the organization — that fail the screen.

## Scope

This article covers the identification and validation of core capabilities for strategy formulation: the three-test screening procedure, evidence requirements for each test, and the classification of near-misses. It applies when a firm is deciding which capabilities to invest in, protect, or build partnerships around, and when evaluating whether a claimed strength can support differentiation or premium pricing. It does not cover the market-side question of which capabilities customers will pay for, which belongs to value-proposition evidence work, nor the organizational question of capability-building programs.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

**Step one: generate candidate capabilities from activities, not departments.** List what the firm actually does that creates customer-relevant value: a fulfillment process, a qualification method, an integration practice, a data feedback loop. Phrase each candidate as a capability — an ability to perform a class of activity at a level of outcome — not as an asset or a team. "Ability to certify suppliers to a reliability standard in half the industry cycle" is a testable capability; "our procurement team" is not.

**Step two: apply the three tests.**

*Test one — relative advantage.* The capability must produce outcomes measurably better than relevant competitors on a metric customers care about: cycle time, defect rate, cost per unit at equal quality, or yield. Evidence must come from comparable measurement — your number against theirs on the same basis — not from internal belief or customer politeness. If no competitor comparison exists, the test is unproven, not passed.

*Test two — imitability.* Estimate, with evidence, what a well-resourced competitor would need to reproduce the outcome: the accumulated tacit skill, the data volume, the network effects, the regulatory approvals, or the calibrated routines involved. Imitability is about the difficulty and time of reproduction, not the impossibility. A capability a determined rival could license, hire, or copy within one planning cycle fails this test. Patents and contracts buy time; time is the currency, so state how much.

*Test three — organizational embeddedness.* The capability must survive the departure of named individuals and remain executable across the sites and teams that need it. Test by tracing the mechanism: documented and rehearsed routines, retained data, tooling, and more than one practitioner able to perform at the standard. A capability held by two veterans and not written down is key-person risk wearing a strategy costume.

A candidate passes as core only when all three tests pass. Candidates failing exactly one test are classified deliberately: pass relative advantage but fail imitability — a temporary advantage to be exploited fast; pass relative advantage and imitability but fail embeddedness — an urgent institutionalization project; fail relative advantage — a table-stakes activity to be managed for cost.

**Step three: stress the tests with challenges.** For each pass, assign a red-team reviewer to argue the opposite: find the competitor who claims the same advantage, the startup that shortcuts the imitation path, the unit where the capability already failed. Record the challenge and the response in the capability dossier.

**Step four: connect survivors to strategy.** Each validated core capability lists the offerings where it is load-bearing, the investments required to keep test two and test three passing over time, and the adjacencies it could credibly support. Capabilities validated but unused by any current offering are flagged for portfolio review.

**Step five: schedule re-testing.** Imitability erodes as rivals learn; embeddedness erodes as people leave. Re-run the screen on a defined cycle — annually for fast-moving sectors — and after any event that plausibly changes a test, such as a key hire market shift or a competitor's acquisition.

## Controls

- **Dossier requirement.** No capability enters strategy documents without a dossier containing the three test results, evidence citations, challenge notes, and a review date.
- **Comparable-basis rule.** Relative advantage claims must state metric, basis, competitor set, and measurement date; unmatched internal metrics cannot support a pass.
- **Named-rival test.** Imitability assessments must name the specific rival whose reproduction path was estimated, and the estimate's assumptions.
- **Bus-count check.** Embeddedness requires evidence of at least two independent practitioners or a documented routine; single-person capabilities are automatically flagged.
- **Red-team assignment.** Each core capability has a designated challenger independent of the owning function.
- **Expiry discipline.** Test passes carry a validity period; expired dossiers are treated as unverified until re-tested.

## Validation evidence

Evidence that the screen is real rather than ratifying: dossiers with dated competitor data and its provenance; recorded challenges that changed or de-certified at least some candidates; bus-count documentation such as succession rosters or runbooks; and re-test records after personnel changes. Triangulation with market-side evidence strengthens the file — customers who switch and cite the capability, competitors' public attempts to imitate, or benchmarking results from an independent body. Absence of any failed candidates across multiple screening rounds is itself a red flag worth investigating, since it suggests the tests were set to pass.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Asset-capability confusion.** Counting a factory, brand, or license as a capability. Correct by rephrasing as an ability with an outcome metric; assets appear inside the imitation-cost estimate instead.
- **Borrowed halo.** Claiming a capability demonstrated by a partner or acquired unit that never integrated. Correct by testing only what the continuing organization executes.
- **Scale mistaken for skill.** Being biggest, not better, on any customer-relevant metric. Correct with the comparable-basis rule on outcome metrics.
- **Unfalsifiable advantage.** Claims framed so no evidence could disprove them. Correct by requiring a stated observation that would fail the test.
- **Dossier decay.** Passes from years ago still cited in current strategy. Correct with expiry discipline.

## Limitations

The screen is only as honest as competitor data, which is often stale, indirect, or self-reported. Imitability estimates embed assumptions about rival intent and budget that may change without notice. Capabilities new to the firm may pass no tests yet but matter later, so the screen should never be the sole gate on exploration spending. And customer relevance sits outside these tests: a capability can be genuinely distinctive and still fail to command willingness to pay, which is a market question this screen deliberately does not answer.
