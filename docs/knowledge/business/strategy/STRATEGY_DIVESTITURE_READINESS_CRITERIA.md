# Strategy Divestiture Readiness Criteria

Putting a business on the market unprepared transfers value to the buyer. Buyers price what they can verify quickly and discount what they cannot: tangled shared services, undocumented contracts, unclear intellectual property ownership, and people whose departure would gut the asset. Divestiture readiness work closes that gap before the first buyer conversation, so the seller negotiates from evidence rather than explanations. This article defines readiness criteria across financial, legal, operational, and human dimensions, and the go or no-go gates that must be satisfied before a capability is offered for sale or spun out.

## Scope

This article covers readiness assessment and preparation for divestitures, carve-outs, and spin-outs of a business line or capability. It applies to sales to third parties and to separations into standalone entities. It addresses the readiness criteria themselves, evidence preparation, separation planning, and the governance of the decision to proceed. It does not cover valuation method selection, auction process management, or tax structuring, which are governed by separate articles on valuation and financing readiness.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

**Confirm the strategic case before preparing the asset.** Write the rationale — capital reallocation, focus, terminal position in the portfolio — and the minimum acceptable outcome, including the walk-away floor. Preparing a business for sale is itself an investment; without a committed rationale, readiness work drifts into permanent half-preparation that demoralizes the unit.

**Establish standalone financials early.** A carve-out must present an income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow on a standalone basis: direct revenues and costs, allocated corporate costs restated as the service levels a buyer would face, and required working capital. State every allocation basis explicitly. Standalone financials are the foundation buyers test everything against; late adjustments to allocations are read as manipulation and reprice the deal.

**Map every dependency in both directions.** Inventory what the unit consumes from the rest of the company — shared systems, treasury, brand, insurance, procurement terms, and specific people — and what the rest of the company consumes from it. For each dependency, define the post-separation arrangement: transitional service agreement with duration and pricing, contract novation, re-created function, or severed supply. The dependency map converts directly into the separation cost estimate and the transitional services schedule.

**Clean title and compliance.** Verify that intellectual property used by the unit is owned or licensable in a transferable form; that customer and supplier contracts are assignable or contain consents that can be obtained; that permits, registrations, and export or data-transfer authorizations travel with the business; and that compliance history — safety, environmental, privacy, anti-bribery — is documented with known issues disclosed. Undisclosed problems discovered in diligence convert into price chips several times their size.

**Prepare the people dimension.** Identify the people whose departure would impair value and address them before marketing: retention arrangements, role clarity in the future entity, and honest communication timing that balances confidentiality against the shock of learning from outside. Understand which jurisdictional consultation obligations apply to a transfer, because they set the minimum clock.

**Prepare the data room as evidence, not advocacy.** Populate it so a buyer can verify: financials with support, the customer and revenue concentration picture, contract summaries with the underlying documents, the dependency map, compliance files, and organizational detail. Material weaknesses are disclosed with their remediation status; discovered-but-undisclosed issues are the classic deal-killer.

**Gate the go decision on explicit readiness criteria.** The unit goes to market only when: standalone financials are complete and internally consistent; the dependency map has arrangements and costs for every line; title and assignability questions are resolved or priced; retention plans cover named key people; and the board has approved the floor and the separation budget. Missing criteria are either cured or consciously priced into structure — never ignored.

**Plan the separation as a project.** Assign a separation manager distinct from the unit's operating leadership, define day-one readiness for the future entity, and rehearse the first month of standalone operation — payroll, bank accounts, customer invoicing — before close.

## Controls

- **Readiness scorecard.** A standing scorecard across financial, legal, operational, people, and compliance dimensions, reviewed monthly by the governing committee; entry to market requires all dimensions at green or board-accepted amber.
- **Allocation basis lock.** Corporate cost allocations in carve-out financials are fixed in writing before marketing; changes require deal-committee approval and restatement notes.
- **Dependency completeness check.** A reviewer outside the unit confirms the dependency map against actual system, treasury, and procurement usage records.
- **Key-person retention gate.** Named critical people have executed arrangements before marketing begins.
- **Disclosure review.** Counsel and compliance review the data-room disclosures against known issues; the gap between known issues and disclosed issues must be zero at launch.
- **Floor discipline.** The approved minimum outcome is documented; bids below it trigger the pre-agreed alternatives, not improvised renegotiation.

## Validation evidence

Evidence of readiness: the standalone financial package with allocation documentation; the dependency map with signed transitional-service termsheets or internal pricing; IP and contract assignability opinions; retention confirmations for named individuals; the populated data room with a disclosure log; and the board-approved readiness decision. Post-close, compare separation costs and transitional service actuals against the readiness estimates as a calibration record for the next transaction.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Eternal grooming.** Perpetual near-readiness without a decision. Correct with a dated board gate: cure the criteria or release the unit from sale preparation.
- **Allocation games.** Restating allocations to flatter standalone performance. Correct through the allocation lock and buyer-side restatement risk being priced honestly.
- **Hidden dependency.** A shared service discovered during buyer diligence. Correct via the independent completeness check against usage records.
- **Key-person surprise.** Value carriers resigning at announcement. Correct by concluding retention arrangements pre-marketing and rehearsing communication.
- **Data-room advocacy.** Promotional summaries unsupported by documents. Correct through disclosure review against the underlying files.

## Limitations

Readiness cannot eliminate buyer caution on genuinely entangled assets; some dependencies have no clean separation at any price. Confidentiality constrains how much preparation and communication can occur before announcement, and premature leaks damage customers and staff. Market windows move; a unit ready in a soft market may still fail its floor. Readiness improves price and speed but does not create a buyer.
