# Working CAPLine Asset-Based Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) CAPLines program includes a **Working CAPLine**, an asset-based revolving line of credit for eligible small businesses with recurring, cyclical, or short-term working-capital needs. Current SBA lender guidance explains that borrowing availability is tied to short-term assets, with repayment generated as those assets convert to cash.

This structure can provide useful liquidity for businesses that sell on credit, but it also requires disciplined receivables, inventory, collateral, and reporting practices. Asset-based borrowing should follow the real cash-conversion cycle rather than mask permanent losses.

## Current SBA boundary

SBA's current public CAPLines summary describes the Working CAPLine as:

- an asset-based revolving line of credit;
- intended for businesses that may not meet the credit standards associated with long-term credit;
- usable for cyclical growth, recurring, and/or short-term needs;
- repaid through conversion of short-term assets to cash; and
- commonly useful to businesses that provide credit to other businesses.

Because the facility requires continual servicing and collateral monitoring, SBA notes that lenders may charge additional fees.

Exact structure, eligible collateral, advance rates, reserves, reporting, and fees are governed by current SBA policy and the lender's agreement.

## Asset-based borrowing model

A Working CAPLine is not simply a fixed dollar amount available regardless of collateral.

A practical borrowing model typically starts from eligible short-term assets and applies lender-defined adjustments such as:

- ineligible receivables;
- concentration reserves;
- aged receivable exclusions;
- inventory eligibility;
- inventory valuation adjustments;
- dilution reserves;
- outstanding line balance; and
- other risk reserves.

The actual borrowing-base formula belongs to the loan agreement. Businesses should understand each component rather than treating lender availability as a black box.

## Receivables readiness

Receivables often provide an important source of borrowing availability.

Maintain accurate information on:

- invoice date;
- due date;
- customer;
- amount;
- credits and returns;
- disputes;
- aging;
- customer concentration;
- collections; and
- write-offs.

High headline receivables do not necessarily mean high borrowing availability. Old, disputed, related-party, foreign, concentrated, or otherwise ineligible receivables may receive reduced or no credit under lender policy.

## Inventory readiness

Where inventory supports the facility, maintain reliable records for:

- quantity;
- cost or other approved valuation basis;
- location;
- ownership;
- purchase commitments;
- aging;
- obsolescence;
- slow-moving stock;
- damaged goods; and
- reconciliation to the general ledger.

Inventory that is difficult to verify or liquidate can have limited collateral value even when accounting records show a substantial balance.

## Cash-conversion cycle

The line should reflect how assets turn into cash.

Map:

1. cash used to buy inventory or fund operations;
2. inventory conversion into sale;
3. sale conversion into receivable;
4. receivable collection; and
5. collected cash applied according to the lender's repayment requirements.

Monitor days inventory outstanding, days sales outstanding, and other practical cycle measures suitable for the business.

A lengthening cash-conversion cycle can increase borrowing while weakening repayment capacity.

## Revolving discipline

Because Working CAPLines revolve, the business can draw, repay, and re-borrow within the approved structure.

Governance should address:

- authorized borrowers;
- draw approvals;
- borrowing-base verification;
- reporting deadlines;
- proceeds handling;
- customer-remittance controls where applicable;
- line reductions from collections;
- exception approvals; and
- conditions that suspend further borrowing.

Do not treat a revolving line as permanent equity capital.

## Continual monitoring

SBA's public guidance emphasizes ongoing collateral monitoring for Working CAPLines.

A business should be prepared to provide periodic information such as:

- current financial statements;
- accounts-receivable aging;
- accounts-payable aging;
- inventory reports;
- borrowing-base certificates;
- customer concentration information;
- collateral reconciliations; and
- covenant or compliance reporting.

The exact frequency and forms come from the lender's agreement and current SBA rules.

## Data quality

Asset-based credit depends heavily on operational data.

Controls should prevent:

- duplicate invoices;
- fictitious receivables;
- invoices recorded before performance;
- unreconciled credits;
- stale inventory;
- double-counting pledged assets;
- unrecorded liens; and
- delayed posting of collections.

Material reporting errors can cause over-advances and damage lender confidence even if they began as weak accounting rather than intentional misconduct.

## Customer concentration

A line dependent on one or two large customers can be vulnerable to delayed payment, dispute, insolvency, or contract termination.

Track concentration by:

- total receivables;
- eligible receivables;
- expected collections; and
- sector or common risk factors.

Stress-test the borrowing base after excluding the largest customer or delaying its payment.

## Accounts-payable pressure

Borrowing availability should not be assessed without considering supplier obligations.

Review:

- overdue payables;
- critical supplier exposure;
- tax obligations;
- payroll timing;
- liens or collection actions;
- supplier credit limits; and
- early-payment or COD requirements.

A borrower can appear liquid from receivables while facing immediate supplier or statutory obligations that consume collections.

## Maturity boundary

Current SBA public guidance states that CAPLines other than Builders CAPLines can have maturities of up to **10 years**.

That maximum does not eliminate annual or periodic credit review, collateral monitoring, renewal requirements, or lender authority to reduce availability when credit quality deteriorates.

Verify the current SBA SOP and loan agreement for a live facility.

## Downside scenarios

Model the facility under conditions such as:

- 30- or 60-day collection slowdown;
- loss of a major customer;
- receivable dispute;
- inventory write-down;
- sales decline;
- supplier tightening terms;
- margin compression;
- unexpected tax or legal obligation; and
- lender reducing eligibility or advance rates.

The borrower should understand how quickly borrowing availability can contract when collateral quality worsens.

## Lender-readiness package

A useful package can include:

- historical and current financial statements;
- accounts-receivable aging;
- accounts-payable aging;
- inventory reports;
- customer concentration analysis;
- debt schedule;
- cash-flow forecast;
- collateral information;
- existing liens;
- management information; and
- requested line amount with borrowing-cycle explanation.

Exact requirements vary by lender.

## Working CAPLine versus WCP

SBA also operates a separate **7(a) Working Capital Pilot (WCP)** with monitored lines of credit and its own eligibility, fee, and monitoring framework.

Do not assume that the permanent Working CAPLine and the WCP are interchangeable. Compare the current program requirements with an SBA lender based on the business's transaction, asset, export, and project-financing needs.

## Evidence to retain

For a material asset-based facility, retain where appropriate:

- loan agreement;
- borrowing-base methodology;
- borrowing-base certificates;
- receivables and inventory reports;
- draw and repayment records;
- collateral audits or inspections;
- lender reporting;
- covenant calculations;
- amendments or waivers; and
- records of material collateral-quality changes.
