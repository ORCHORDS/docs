# Startup Cost Estimation

## Purpose

Startup-cost estimation turns a business idea into a time-phased view of the cash and resources required before and shortly after launch. The goal is not false precision; it is to expose the major cost categories, timing, and uncertainty early enough to support funding and viability decisions.

## Build the cost inventory

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends identifying expenses that apply to the specific business model. Common categories can include:

- office or operating space;
- equipment and supplies;
- communications and utilities;
- licenses and permits;
- insurance;
- legal and accounting services;
- inventory;
- salaries;
- advertising and marketing;
- market research;
- printed materials; and
- website or other launch technology.

The relevant list varies for online, service, and physical-location businesses, so use the categories as a prompt rather than a fixed checklist.

## Separate timing and recurrence

Classify estimated expenses into at least:

- **one-time costs** required to establish or launch the business; and
- **recurring costs** such as rent, salaries, subscriptions, insurance, utilities, and other ongoing commitments.

The SBA recommends looking beyond the launch month when estimating recurring costs. A time-phased view makes it easier to see when capital is required rather than only the total amount.

## Estimation discipline

1. List the required resources and obligations before pricing them.
2. Use published prices, quotations, vendor information, or comparable-market evidence where available.
3. Record the date and source of important estimates.
4. Separate known contractual amounts from assumptions.
5. Add location-specific costs where geography materially changes wages, rent, tax, insurance, licensing, or utilities.
6. Revisit estimates when scope, timing, suppliers, or launch assumptions change.

## Use in planning

Startup-cost estimates feed business plans, funding requirements, cash-flow planning, and break-even analysis. Investors and lenders may compare expected costs with projected revenue, so assumptions should be explainable and internally consistent.
