# Strategic Okr Objective Key Result Definition Register

## Scope

This article describes how to maintain an Objectives and Key Results (OKR) register as a structured set of fields that carries the operating cadence of the strategy. The OKR concept was popularized by John Doerr in his 2018 book *Measure What Matters* and traces its origins to Andy Grove's articulation of OKRs at Intel in the 1970s and the subsequent adoption at Google. The article treats OKRs as a planning and review discipline; it does not validate that any particular objective or key result is correct and does not substitute for the strategic intent set elsewhere in the plan.

The register records, for each objective, the objective statement, the owner, the time horizon, the key results, the measure definitions, the targets, the source of evidence, and the cadence of review. The register also records the relationship between OKRs and the parent strategy, the parent Balanced Scorecard, and the parent budget envelope. An OKR without a parent is a floating commitment; a strategy without OKRs is unmeasurable intent.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's Balanced Scorecard register, the horizon allocation article, the ambidexterity article, and the budget envelope article. The OKR register is the operational layer that the other appendices ultimately reconcile to.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Building the OKR register proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Set the strategic question.** State why the OKR register is being built, who will use it, and what decision it informs. Without this preamble the register is a tracking spreadsheet rather than a decision input.
2. **Restate the parent strategy.** Briefly restate the strategy that the OKRs must serve. Each OKR should be traceable to a parent strategic objective (typically from the Balanced Scorecard register or the strategic intent section of the plan).
3. **Define each objective.** State the objective as a directional commitment. The objective should be ambitious, qualitative, and short. A common test is whether the objective states what the organization wants to achieve, not how it will achieve it.
4. **Define each key result.** For each objective, define two to five key results that are observable and measurable. Each key result should specify the measure, the baseline, the target, and the time horizon. A key result that does not include all four is provisional.
5. **Source the evidence.** Each key result should be supported by a named data source, a steward, a refresh cadence, and a method. A key result without a source is a finding to address before the next review.
6. **Assign owners.** Each objective and each key result should have a named owner. The owner is accountable for the measure and the outcome, not for the activity.
7. **Reconcile to the parent strategy.** The OKR register should reconcile to the Balanced Scorecard register, the horizon allocation, the ambidexterity split, and the budget envelope. An OKR that cannot be traced to a parent is a finding.

The appendix should conclude with a summary that names the strategic question, the objectives, the parent strategy links, and the principal risks of mis-measurement. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying tables.

## Controls

- **Objective definitions frozen before key results are set.** The objectives are written and approved before any key result is defined. Mid-cycle redefinition is treated as an amendment.
- **Key result methodology documented.** Each key result has a documented measure, baseline, target, time horizon, source, and steward. Mid-cycle change in any of these is an amendment.
- **Cadenced review.** The register is reviewed at intervals aligned with the planning cycle. Material change in the parent strategy or in the market triggers off-cycle review.
- **Owner discipline.** Every objective and every key result has a named owner. A floating owner is a finding.
- **Score transparency.** The scoring rule for OKRs (typically 0.0 to 1.0) is documented before the cycle begins. Mid-cycle change in the scoring rule is an amendment.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the objectives, the key results, and the parent links. The challenge is logged with names, dates, and dispositions.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the register from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic question, the parent strategy restatement, and the approving authority;
- the objective statement, owner, and time horizon;
- the key result definition with measure, baseline, target, and time horizon;
- the data source, steward, refresh cadence, and method for each key result;
- the parent link from each OKR to a parent strategic objective;
- the scoring rule for the cycle;
- the score log showing progress against each key result with date and reviewer;
- the variance between planned and actual scores at the prior review with cause;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the reconciliation to the Balanced Scorecard register, the horizon allocation, the ambidexterity split, and the budget envelope;
- the minutes of the OKR review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also includes comparison against external checks. Where the OKR is meant to deliver a customer outcome but the customer evidence contradicts the trajectory, the divergence is a finding to address before the next review.

## Failure modes and correction

- **Output dressed as outcome.** A key result measures an output (deliverables shipped, programs launched) rather than an outcome (customer retention improved, revenue per account increased). Correct by requiring each key result to be traceable to an outcome.
- **Target without baseline.** A target is set without a baseline. Correct by requiring every key result to have a baseline and a target, and a stated change.
- **OKR without parent.** An OKR exists in the register but is not traceable to a parent strategic objective. Correct by reconciling the OKR register to the parent strategy and removing or rebasing floating OKRs.
- **Source substitution without record.** The data source for a key result is changed mid-cycle without documenting the substitution. Correct by routing changes through the amendment process.
- **Cadence slip.** The OKR register is updated inconsistently or not at all. Correct by enforcing the review cadence and surfacing missed reviews.
- **Score inflation.** Key result scores are routinely 0.7 or higher despite missing the target. Correct by surfacing score distribution at the review and tightening targets where the score is too loose.
- **No reconciliation to plan.** The OKR register does not reconcile to the Balanced Scorecard register, the horizon allocation, or the budget envelope. Correct by reconciling before approval.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the register.

## Limitations

The OKR framework is a planning and review discipline. It does not validate that any particular strategy is correct and does not substitute for the strategic intent set elsewhere in the plan. The framework was popularized by John Doerr in his 2018 book *Measure What Matters* and traces its lineage to Andy Grove at Intel and Google's subsequent adoption; the framework has been widely adopted but its application to specific organizations requires primary research on customers and operations that the framework does not provide.

The register does not address the question of how the strategy is set (that is the subject of the leaf's horizon and ambidexterity articles) or how the strategy is measured at the perspective level (that is the subject of the Balanced Scorecard register article). The three lenses (OKR, BSC, strategic intent) are complementary, not redundant.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
