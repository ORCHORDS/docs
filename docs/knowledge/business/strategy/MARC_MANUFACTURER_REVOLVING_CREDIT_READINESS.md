# MARC Manufacturer Revolving-Credit Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) **Manufacturers' Access to Revolving Credit (MARC)** delivery method is a 7(a) financing option dedicated to eligible small manufacturers. SBA introduced MARC in 2025 to expand manufacturer access to working capital and revolving credit.

Current SBA lender guidance states that MARC is available only to eligible small businesses engaged in manufacturing in NAICS sectors **31-33**. MARC may be structured as term or revolving credit and combines features of Standard 7(a) underwriting with monitoring practices similar to SBA Express.

## Current public terms

SBA's current lender summary lists:

- maximum loan amount: **$5 million**;
- maximum SBA guaranty: **85% for loans up to $150,000 and 75% for loans above $150,000**;
- term or revolving structures; and
- for revolving loans, maturity of up to **20 years**, described as up to 10 years revolving followed by up to 10 years term.

Verify the current SOP and Appendix 13 before relying on these terms in a live application.

## Manufacturing eligibility

MARC is not a general-purpose 7(a) delivery method for every small business.

Current SBA guidance ties eligibility to manufacturing businesses whose six-digit NAICS code begins with 31, 32, or 33.

Before planning around MARC:

1. confirm the business's primary NAICS classification;
2. verify SBA size eligibility;
3. confirm the business meets general 7(a) eligibility requirements;
4. verify current MARC-specific rules; and
5. use an SBA lender that offers or understands the MARC delivery method.

Do not select a manufacturing NAICS code solely to access financing if it does not reflect the business's actual primary activity.

## Term versus revolving structure

MARC can be structured as a term loan or revolving line.

A term structure may fit a defined working-capital or eligible financing need with a scheduled amortization path. A revolving structure can fit recurring working-capital cycles where the business draws and repays based on operating needs.

For revolving credit, understand:

- borrowing procedures;
- line limits;
- annual financial review;
- conditions for continued advances;
- monitoring and reporting;
- conversion triggers; and
- the term-out structure that applies after the revolving period.

## Annual review and conversion risk

Current SBA public lender guidance states that a lender must conduct an annual review of borrower financials for a revolving MARC loan.

If the annual review is unsatisfactory, the revolving line must be converted to a fully amortizing term loan with a zero balance at maturity. Once converted, further disbursements are not permitted.

This makes reporting and financial discipline operationally important. A manufacturer should not build its business plan around indefinite re-borrowing without considering the possibility that the revolving feature could end.

## Manufacturing working-capital model

Manufacturers often finance a longer operating cycle than service businesses.

Map:

1. raw-material purchases;
2. work in process;
3. finished-goods inventory;
4. customer shipment;
5. invoicing;
6. customer acceptance; and
7. collection.

Track how much cash is tied up at each stage and how the line supports that cycle.

## Inventory readiness

Manufacturing inventory can include raw materials, work in process, and finished goods with different liquidity and valuation risks.

Maintain reliable records for:

- quantity;
- cost;
- location;
- production stage;
- obsolete or slow-moving inventory;
- scrap;
- customer-specific inventory;
- consigned inventory; and
- reconciliation to financial statements.

If inventory supports lender credit decisions, ensure physical and accounting records are consistent.

## Receivables readiness

Manufacturers selling to distributors, OEMs, retailers, government customers, or other businesses can accumulate substantial receivables.

Monitor:

- customer concentration;
- payment terms;
- invoice aging;
- returns;
- warranty deductions;
- disputes;
- chargebacks;
- customer credit quality; and
- collections.

A large receivable from one customer can create both liquidity and concentration risk.

## Equipment and fixed-asset boundary

MARC working-capital needs should be separated from major fixed-asset financing decisions.

If the manufacturer also needs substantial equipment or real estate, compare other SBA structures such as Standard 7(a) or 504 financing. The July 2026 cumulative 7(a)/504 coordination change can be relevant to capital-intensive expansion.

Do not overload a revolving working-capital facility with long-lived assets if a term structure better matches the useful life and cash-generation profile.

## Supply-chain risk

Manufacturing credit needs can change quickly after supplier disruption.

Review:

- supplier concentration;
- foreign-source exposure;
- lead times;
- minimum order quantities;
- tariffs/duties where relevant;
- commodity-price exposure;
- single-source components; and
- alternative supplier qualification.

A working-capital line can help finance higher inventory, but financing alone does not solve supplier dependency.

## Demand and backlog

Link the requested line to credible demand evidence.

Useful inputs include:

- firm orders;
- historical customer demand;
- forecast accuracy;
- backlog;
- cancellations;
- customer concentration; and
- production capacity.

Avoid building borrowing needs on an upside sales forecast that the production system cannot support.

## Capacity expansion

If the purpose is to support growth, confirm that working capital is not the only constraint.

Evaluate:

- machine capacity;
- labor availability;
- quality systems;
- factory space;
- maintenance;
- engineering capacity;
- supplier capacity; and
- logistics.

A larger line can increase production problems if operational bottlenecks remain unresolved.

## Cash-flow downside scenarios

Model cases such as:

- major customer pays 45 days late;
- order cancellation after inventory purchase;
- raw-material price increase;
- quality hold;
- production downtime;
- warranty/return spike;
- supplier switches to advance payment; or
- revolving MARC converts to a term loan after an unsatisfactory annual review.

The business should understand how debt service and liquidity behave in each case.

## Lender-readiness package

A MARC financing package can include:

- historical/interim financial statements;
- receivables/payables aging;
- inventory reports;
- production and backlog information;
- debt schedule;
- cash-flow forecast;
- NAICS and manufacturing-activity information;
- major customer/supplier concentration;
- requested structure; and
- explanation of how the line supports the manufacturing cash cycle.

Exact requirements vary by lender and current SBA policy.

## Evidence to retain

For material MARC financing, retain where appropriate:

- application and loan documents;
- NAICS/eligibility analysis;
- annual lender review materials;
- financial statements;
- aging/inventory reports;
- draw and repayment records;
- conversion notices if applicable;
- amendments/waivers; and
- the current MARC Appendix/SOP reference used for the transaction.

## Scope note

This article provides general MARC readiness guidance from current SBA public information. It does not guarantee manufacturing eligibility, loan approval, a revolving structure, a 20-year maturity, or continued line availability after annual review. Current SBA policy and lender underwriting control the actual loan.
