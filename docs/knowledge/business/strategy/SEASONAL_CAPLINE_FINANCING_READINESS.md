# Seasonal CAPLine Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) CAPLines program includes a **Seasonal CAPLine** for predictable seasonal increases in working-capital needs. Current SBA lender guidance states that Seasonal CAPLines can finance seasonal increases in accounts receivable and inventory and, in some cases, associated increased labor costs. The facility may be revolving or non-revolving.

This is different from financing chronic losses or a permanently undercapitalized business. A seasonal facility should be tied to a recurring, supportable operating cycle in which temporary funding needs rise and then convert back to cash.

## Seasonal pattern

Before seeking financing, demonstrate the operating pattern that creates the temporary need.

Useful evidence includes:

- monthly or weekly historical sales;
- accounts-receivable balances by period;
- inventory levels by period;
- payroll or labor changes during peak months;
- supplier-order timing;
- customer-payment timing;
- prior seasonal cash deficits; and
- the point in the cycle when cash normally returns.

A business with only one unexplained busy month does not necessarily have a reliable seasonal cycle. Multiple periods of evidence make the financing request easier to understand.

## Eligible-use discipline

Current SBA public guidance describes Seasonal CAPLines as financing seasonal increases in receivables and inventory and, in some circumstances, the associated increase in labor costs.

A borrower should therefore separate:

- ordinary baseline working capital;
- incremental seasonal inventory;
- incremental seasonal receivables;
- qualifying incremental labor; and
- unrelated expansion or fixed-asset spending.

Confirm permitted uses with the lender and the current SBA SOP before drawing funds. Do not assume a cost is eligible merely because it occurs during the busy season.

## Build a seasonal cash model

A practical forecast can use the following sequence:

1. Establish normal off-season sales and costs.
2. Forecast the build-up of inventory before the season.
3. Forecast additional labor and fulfillment costs.
4. Estimate sales conversion during the season.
5. Model when receivables are invoiced.
6. Model realistic collection dates rather than invoice dates alone.
7. Identify the peak cumulative borrowing requirement.
8. Estimate when collections allow the line to reduce.
9. Stress-test a slower or weaker season.

The peak borrowing need is often earlier than the peak sales period because inventory and labor must be funded before customers pay.

## Revolving versus non-revolving

SBA currently permits Seasonal CAPLines to be revolving or non-revolving.

A revolving structure can support repeated draws and repayments through the seasonal cycle. A non-revolving structure can fit a defined one-way funding build followed by repayment.

Understand the lender's rules for:

- draw documentation;
- borrowing availability;
- interest calculation;
- cleanup or zero-balance expectations if applicable;
- reporting frequency;
- collateral monitoring; and
- renewal or re-approval.

Do not infer exact loan terms from the word "revolving"; the loan agreement controls the facility.

## Inventory readiness

When inventory is the main seasonal driver, retain enough information to explain what is being financed.

Useful controls include:

- item or category inventory reports;
- purchase orders;
- supplier lead times;
- aging or obsolescence review;
- expected gross margin;
- storage and insurance costs;
- markdown or return exposure; and
- reconciliation between inventory records and financial statements.

Buying excess inventory to maximize line usage can turn a temporary financing need into a permanent cash problem.

## Receivables readiness

When receivables drive the seasonal need, review:

- customer concentration;
- invoice terms;
- aging;
- disputes and credits;
- collection history;
- season-end return or cancellation rights; and
- bad-debt assumptions.

A receivable is not equivalent to cash. The forecast should reflect realistic collection behavior.

## Labor-cost readiness

Where seasonal labor costs are part of the financing need, link the requested amount to a documented staffing plan.

Track:

- expected headcount;
- start/end dates;
- wages and payroll burden;
- overtime assumptions;
- contractor versus employee costs; and
- timing between payroll and customer collections.

Confirm with the lender which associated labor costs are eligible under the current program rules.

## Downside scenarios

Model at least a few plausible deviations from plan:

- season starts late;
- demand is below forecast;
- collections slow;
- inventory arrives late;
- supplier prices rise;
- weather or another external event disrupts demand;
- returned or unsold inventory is higher than expected; or
- a major customer delays payment.

The business should know how it will repay the line if the season underperforms.

## Lender-readiness package

A useful package can include:

- historical monthly financial statements;
- current interim financial statements;
- accounts-receivable aging;
- accounts-payable aging;
- inventory reports;
- seasonal sales history;
- cash-flow forecast;
- supplier and customer concentration information;
- debt schedule; and
- explanation of the requested seasonal line amount.

Exact requirements vary by lender and current SBA rules.

## Maturity boundary

Current SBA public guidance states that CAPLines other than Builders CAPLines can have maturities of up to **10 years**.

That is a program maximum, not a promise that a seasonal facility will be approved for that duration. The lender determines the appropriate structure and renewal/monitoring conditions under current policy.

## Evidence to retain

For a material Seasonal CAPLine, retain where appropriate:

- loan agreement;
- seasonal forecast;
- historical seasonality analysis;
- inventory and receivable reports;
- draw requests;
- lender reporting;
- collections and repayments;
- forecast-to-actual analysis; and
- material amendments or waivers.
