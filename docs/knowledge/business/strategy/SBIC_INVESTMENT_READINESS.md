# SBIC Investment Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) licenses and regulates **Small Business Investment Companies (SBICs)** that invest private capital, together with SBA-supported leverage where applicable, into eligible U.S. small businesses. SBA does **not** invest directly in the operating company through the SBIC program; the individual SBIC makes the investment decision.

SBIC capital can take the form of debt, equity, or a combination of both. Investment readiness therefore differs from ordinary bank-loan readiness: the business needs to match its growth stage, sector, geography, cash-flow profile, ownership expectations, and capital use to the mandate of a specific SBIC.

## Current SBA overview

SBA's current investment-capital page states that:

- more than **300 SBICs** are licensed by SBA;
- SBICs invest through debt, equity, or a combination;
- SBA itself does not make the operating-company investment;
- a typical SBIC investment is made over about a **three-year period**; and
- each SBIC has its own industry, geography, maturity, and investment-size profile.

The figures shown by SBA for typical investment sizes and interest rates are descriptive ranges, not guaranteed terms.

## Basic eligibility boundary

Current SBA public guidance identifies universal SBIC eligibility themes including:

- the company must be a U.S. business;
- at least **51% of employees and assets** must be within the United States;
- the company must qualify as a small business under SBA size standards; and
- the company must not operate in an excluded industry.

SBA specifically lists areas such as farmland, real estate, and financing among industries that do not qualify under the general public summary.

Verify current rules with the specific SBIC and SBA guidance before relying on eligibility for a transaction.

## Find mandate fit before pitching

SBICs are not interchangeable pools of money.

Before contacting one, review:

1. industries targeted;
2. geography;
3. company size and maturity;
4. debt versus equity preference;
5. typical check size;
6. control/minority preference;
7. profitability or cash-flow expectations;
8. holding period;
9. follow-on capital capacity; and
10. recent portfolio companies.

A strong company can still be a poor fit for an SBIC whose mandate points elsewhere.

## Debt readiness

SBA's current page describes typical SBIC debt investments ranging from **$250,000 to $10 million**, with illustrative interest rates between **9% and 16%**.

Treat these as descriptive public ranges rather than a rate quote.

For debt-oriented SBIC financing, prepare:

- historical and current financial statements;
- cash-flow forecast;
- debt-service model;
- current debt schedule;
- collateral information where relevant;
- growth-investment plan;
- downside scenarios; and
- clear repayment sources.

A business that cannot support scheduled interest or principal may be better suited to equity or another capital structure.

## Equity readiness

SBA's current page describes typical SBIC equity investments from **$100,000 to $5 million**.

Equity funding requires preparation for ownership and governance consequences.

Document:

- current capitalization table;
- authorized and outstanding shares/interests;
- options or other rights;
- founder/employee ownership;
- prior investor rights;
- valuation rationale;
- amount being raised;
- intended ownership offered; and
- expected use of proceeds.

Do not evaluate an equity offer only by cash received. Review dilution, voting rights, board rights, liquidation economics, information rights, transfer restrictions, and future financing implications.

## Debt-plus-equity structures

SBA also describes mixed debt/equity SBIC investments, with typical public ranges from **$250,000 to $10 million** and illustrative loan rates generally between **10% and 14%**.

Mixed structures can include economics that behave differently from ordinary loans, such as warrants or other equity participation.

Model:

- cash interest;
- principal repayment;
- ownership dilution;
- contingent equity value;
- exit proceeds; and
- downside/default consequences.

Professional legal and tax review is appropriate for material hybrid financing.

## Investment case

An SBIC pitch should explain why the investment can create a return while remaining realistic about risk.

A useful investment case covers:

- customer problem and market;
- revenue model;
- competitive position;
- management team;
- historical performance;
- unit economics or margin structure;
- growth opportunity;
- requested capital;
- milestones funded; and
- plausible investor return or repayment path.

Separate evidence from projections. Market-size claims and forecast growth should have identifiable assumptions.

## Use-of-proceeds discipline

Prepare a time-phased use-of-proceeds schedule.

Examples can include:

- capacity expansion;
- hiring;
- product development;
- sales/marketing;
- acquisitions;
- working capital;
- equipment; and
- other eligible growth investments.

Link each major use to an operating milestone rather than presenting a generic cash runway number.

## Data room readiness

A material SBIC process may require due diligence across:

- corporate formation/governance;
- capitalization;
- financial statements;
- tax records;
- material contracts;
- intellectual property;
- litigation;
- regulatory matters;
- employment;
- customers/suppliers;
- insurance; and
- cybersecurity or technology where relevant.

Organize documents before diligence begins and track which version was provided.

## Management readiness

SBICs often evaluate management quality as heavily as the financial model.

Be prepared to explain:

- leadership responsibilities;
- succession or key-person risk;
- hiring gaps;
- governance cadence;
- financial controls;
- major operating risks; and
- how the team handled previous setbacks.

Inconsistent explanations between executives can undermine confidence even when underlying performance is good.

## Valuation and terms

For equity or mixed structures, treat valuation as one part of the economic package.

Review:

- pre-money/post-money valuation;
- security type;
- liquidation preference;
- dividends/interest;
- anti-dilution provisions;
- voting/board rights;
- information rights;
- redemption rights;
- covenants;
- transfer rights; and
- exit provisions.

Use qualified legal and financial advisors for material transactions.

## Multiple investors and existing lenders

New SBIC financing can affect existing credit or investor arrangements.

Check:

- negative pledges;
- debt-incurrence covenants;
- lien restrictions;
- change-of-control clauses;
- preemptive rights;
- investor consent requirements; and
- intercreditor needs.

Do not sign a term sheet that conflicts with existing obligations without resolving the conflict.

## Downside scenarios

Model what happens if:

- revenue growth is delayed;
- margins decline;
- follow-on funding is unavailable;
- debt covenants tighten;
- the company misses milestones;
- a major customer leaves; or
- exit timing extends materially.

A financing structure should remain survivable under a reasonable downside case.

## Outreach process

SBA's current guidance recommends:

1. researching the SBIC directory for mandate fit;
2. preparing the business plan and investment case; and
3. approaching the SBIC, ideally through a credible network introduction where available.

Keep an investor pipeline rather than relying on one SBIC conversation.

## Evidence to retain

For a material SBIC financing process, retain where appropriate:

- investor-target list;
- pitch materials;
- financial model;
- diligence index;
- term sheets;
- board/member approvals;
- final investment documents;
- capitalization records; and
- records of material representations made during diligence.

## Scope note

This article provides general SBIC investment-readiness guidance based on current SBA public information. It does not guarantee eligibility, investment, valuation, interest rate, ownership terms, or a specific investment size. Each licensed SBIC makes its own investment decisions within applicable SBA rules and its investment mandate.
