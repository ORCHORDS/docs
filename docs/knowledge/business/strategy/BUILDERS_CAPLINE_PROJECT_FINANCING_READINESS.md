# Builders CAPLine Project Financing Readiness

## Purpose

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) CAPLines program includes a **Builders CAPLine** for eligible small general contractors constructing or rehabilitating residential or commercial property for resale. Current SBA lender guidance describes this as a specific exception, under program conditions, to the general 7(a) restriction on financing investment property.

Builders CAPLine readiness requires project-level cost, schedule, market, and repayment discipline. A construction budget that only lists total costs is not enough; the borrower and lender need to understand how funds convert into completed property and ultimately into sale proceeds.

## Current SBA boundary

SBA's public CAPLines guidance states that a Builders CAPLine:

- supports small general contractors;
- finances construction or rehabilitation of residential or commercial property for resale; and
- treats "construct" and "rehabilitate" as work performed on-site to the structure, utility connections, and landscaping.

Program eligibility and permitted uses depend on the current SBA SOP and lender underwriting. Verify the live rules before assuming a land, investment-property, off-site, or development cost is eligible.

## Project versus general working capital

A Builders CAPLine is project-based financing. Keep the project economics distinct from ordinary company overhead and unrelated jobs.

Create a project file that identifies:

- property and project description;
- ownership/control structure;
- scope of construction or rehabilitation;
- construction budget;
- sources and uses of funds;
- contractor and subcontractor roles;
- project schedule;
- contingency;
- expected sale strategy; and
- repayment source.

If several projects are financed, track each project separately enough to understand cost, progress, advances, and repayment.

## Construction budget

Break the project budget into meaningful cost categories, such as:

- site and structural work eligible under the current program;
- materials;
- direct labor;
- subcontractors;
- utilities and connections;
- landscaping;
- permits and inspections;
- professional services where permitted;
- insurance;
- financing costs;
- contingency; and
- other eligible project costs.

Confirm cost eligibility with the lender. Accounting classification alone does not establish SBA eligibility.

## Draw schedule

Construction financing should connect advances to real project progress.

A reusable draw-control process can include:

1. approve a baseline project budget;
2. establish milestone or cost-based draw rules;
3. collect invoices, payroll, or other support for completed work;
4. verify work progress where required;
5. compare cumulative spending with percentage completion;
6. account for retainage or unresolved claims;
7. update cost-to-complete after each material draw; and
8. stop or escalate advances when the remaining facility is insufficient to complete the project.

Do not rely on the original budget after evidence shows that the project has materially changed.

## Cost-to-complete discipline

One of the most important project-financing questions is whether available resources are enough to finish the property.

At each review point, calculate:

- original total budget;
- approved changes;
- costs incurred;
- committed but unpaid costs;
- remaining expected costs;
- contingency remaining;
- undrawn financing; and
- any additional borrower contribution needed.

A line that is fully drawn before the project can be completed creates serious lender, contractor, and sale risk.

## Schedule risk

Construction delays can increase interest, labor, insurance, storage, and market risk.

Track:

- permit timing;
- material lead times;
- subcontractor availability;
- inspections;
- utility connections;
- weather-sensitive work;
- critical-path tasks;
- expected completion date; and
- sale or marketing timeline.

Reforecast financing need when schedule slippage affects costs or expected sale timing.

## Market and sale assumptions

Because the project is intended for resale, repayment depends materially on the completed property's marketability and sale proceeds.

Document:

- target buyer or market segment;
- comparable sales or other market evidence;
- expected listing or sale price;
- expected selling costs;
- likely marketing period;
- downside sale-price assumptions; and
- carrying costs if sale takes longer than expected.

Avoid treating an appraisal or target price as guaranteed proceeds.

## Contractor capacity

The business must be capable of completing the financed project alongside its existing workload.

Review:

- management experience;
- prior comparable projects;
- current backlog;
- staffing;
- subcontractor relationships;
- supplier capacity;
- equipment;
- liquidity;
- bonding if relevant; and
- unresolved claims or defaults.

A project that is individually feasible can still be too much for a contractor already stretched across other jobs.

## Revolving and project controls

The exact facility structure is determined under current SBA policy and lender terms. Borrowers should understand:

- how project advances are calculated;
- required inspections or certifications;
- whether proceeds from a sale must immediately reduce the line;
- conditions for adding another project;
- reporting requirements;
- collateral requirements; and
- events that stop further draws.

Do not assume that availability on a line means the funds can be moved freely between projects.

## Maturity boundary

Current SBA public guidance distinguishes Builders CAPLines from the other CAPLine types. Builders CAPLine loans must not exceed **60 months plus the estimated time to complete construction or rehabilitation** under the current public summary.

Verify the current SOP and lender documentation before using that limit in a live financing model.

## Downside scenarios

Model adverse cases such as:

- 10% or 20% cost overrun;
- completion delay;
- subcontractor failure;
- material-price increase;
- inspection or permit delay;
- damage requiring rework;
- sale-price decline;
- longer marketing period; or
- buyer financing falling through.

The financing plan should show which risks can be absorbed by contingency and which would require new capital or lender approval.

## Lender-readiness package

A project-financing package can include:

- business financial statements;
- contractor experience and project history;
- property/project information;
- construction budget;
- plans/specifications as relevant;
- project schedule;
- permits or approval status;
- contractor/subcontractor information;
- cost-to-complete analysis;
- market and resale evidence;
- sources-and-uses schedule; and
- explanation of requested line amount and repayment source.

Exact lender requirements vary.

## Change governance

Escalate material changes such as:

- scope increase;
- budget overrun;
- revised completion date;
- key subcontractor failure;
- material design change;
- title or ownership issue;
- property damage;
- market deterioration; or
- unexpected legal or permitting problem.

Determine whether lender consent is required before committing to the change.

## Evidence to retain

For a material Builders CAPLine project, retain where appropriate:

- loan agreement;
- project budget and revisions;
- draw records;
- invoices and payment evidence;
- inspections or progress reports;
- cost-to-complete calculations;
- change orders;
- permits and approvals;
- lender communications;
- sale/closing records; and
- repayment evidence.

This article provides general Builders CAPLine readiness guidance based on current SBA public information. It does not guarantee eligibility, financing, property value, construction completion, or a particular use of proceeds. Verify current SBA SOP and lender requirements for each project.
