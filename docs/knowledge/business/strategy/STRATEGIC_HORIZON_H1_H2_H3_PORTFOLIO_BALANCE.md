# Strategic Horizon H1 H2 H3 Portfolio Balance

## Scope

This article describes how to organize a growth portfolio using a three-horizon framing and how to document the resulting balance in the appendix of a business plan. The three-horizon scheme was popularized in McKinsey & Company strategy writing in the late 1990s and early 2000s; it is used here as a planning aid, not a forecast. Horizon 1 covers the existing core business, including extensions and renewals. Horizon 2 covers emerging opportunities that have a credible path to scale. Horizon 3 covers longer-dated options, pilots, and experiments whose economics are not yet proven. The article assumes the planner wants explicit allocation rules across the three horizons rather than implicit drift.

The framing is widely cited but it is a heuristic. It does not guarantee future returns and should not be treated as a market-tested allocation algorithm. The article treats it as scaffolding for an internal conversation about how much of the organization's effort, capital, and talent is being committed to defending the core, scaling the next business, and planting seeds for what might come after.

The article is intended to be read alongside the leaf's `STRATEGIC_AMBIDEXTERITY_EXPLOIT_EXPLORE_BUDGET_SPLIT`, `STRATEGIC_PORTFOLIO_BCG_GROWTH_SHARE_MATRIX_REGISTER`, and `STRATEGIC_OKR_OBJECTIVE_KEY_RESULT_DEFINITION_REGISTER` articles. Each treats a complementary lens: ambidexterity governs the behavior needed to run the portfolio; BCG classifies business units within the portfolio; OKRs carry the operating cadence. None of them substitute for the others.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Constructing the plan appendix proceeds in seven steps.

1. **Restate the strategic intent.** Write down the question the appendix is meant to inform: how is the portfolio positioned to deliver near-term results while building future growth? Without this preamble the horizon allocation becomes a chart rather than a decision.
2. **Define each horizon in operational terms.** Replace the abstract labels with concrete definitions tied to the organization's planning cycle. For example, Horizon 1 might mean mature business units with stable margins; Horizon 2 might mean adjacencies where the model has been validated but is still being scaled; Horizon 3 might mean early-stage bets whose economics are not yet stable.
3. **Classify every business unit, product, and major initiative.** Assign each one to a horizon using the operational definition. Be conservative: misclassifying a true Horizon 1 unit as Horizon 2 inflates the appearance of growth investment.
4. **Capture capital, expense, and headcount allocation by horizon.** For each horizon record committed capital, operating expense, full-time-equivalent headcount, and senior leadership time. Include both approved amounts and currently planned amounts to show the gap.
5. **Capture the evidence behind each allocation.** For each horizon list the sources of evidence used: financial performance, market research, customer development, prior experimentation, or expert opinion. Tag evidence by source, date, and quality. The same allocation number can be defensible or weak depending on the underlying evidence.
6. **Document the rebalancing rules.** State the conditions under which an initiative moves between horizons, the authority that decides, and the cadence of review. Rebalancing rules keep the appendix a living document rather than a one-time chart.
7. **Surface the dependencies across horizons.** Note where Horizon 2 builds on Horizon 1 capabilities, where Horizon 3 experiments rely on shared infrastructure, and where headcount and capital compete across horizons.

The appendix should conclude with a one-page summary that names the strategic intent, the allocation, the principal risks, and the next review. The summary should be readable without recourse to the underlying appendix tables.

## Controls

- **Allocation rules stated up front.** State the target share of capital, expense, headcount, and senior-leadership time across the three horizons before classifying individual items. Apply the rules consistently.
- **Evidence required for each horizon.** Horizon 2 and Horizon 3 classifications should be supported by documented evidence of traction, learning, or credible customer interest. A claim without evidence should not push an item out of Horizon 1.
- **Cadenced portfolio review.** Review the horizon balance at intervals aligned with the planning cycle (often quarterly for the operating business and annually for the broader portfolio). The review should record the actual allocation, the planned allocation, and the variance.
- **Move criteria explicit.** State the criteria for moving an item between horizons: what observation, by whom, on what evidence, on what authority. Mid-cycle reclassification should be an amendment.
- **Independent challenge.** A role separate from the sponsor challenges the classification, the allocation, and the evidence. The challenge is recorded with names, dates, and dispositions.
- **Conservation of leadership time.** Senior-leadership attention is itself a scarce resource. Track it alongside capital so that growth investment is not only funded but also led.

## Validation evidence

A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the appendix from the evidence. Useful artifacts include:

- the strategic intent statement with author and date;
- the operational definition of each horizon and the source of the definition;
- the classification of every business unit, product, and major initiative with horizon tag and supporting evidence;
- the allocation tables for capital, expense, headcount, and leadership time by horizon;
- the variance between planned and actual allocation at the prior review;
- the move criteria and the move log showing items that have shifted horizons;
- the challenge log with names, dates, comments, and dispositions;
- the minutes of the portfolio review with attendees and decisions.

Validation also involves comparison to other planning artifacts. The horizon allocation should reconcile to the capital plan, the operating budget, and the headcount plan within the appendix. Material variance is a finding, not a footnote.

## Failure modes and correction

- **H1 only by default.** The organization reports it is investing in all three horizons but its actual capital and time sit overwhelmingly in Horizon 1. Correct by tracking both the planned and the actual allocation and surfacing variance at the portfolio review.
- **H2 inflation.** Items are placed in Horizon 2 to signal growth ambition without supporting evidence. Correct by requiring documented evidence of customer, market, or model validation before an item is classified as Horizon 2.
- **H3 theater.** Horizon 3 contains many small experiments but no disciplined pipeline of options. Correct by enforcing a portfolio-level rule for the number and scale of Horizon 3 experiments relative to the rest of the portfolio.
- **Hidden Horizon 1 erosion.** The core business is losing share or margin but no Horizon 1 renewal initiative is recorded. Correct by reviewing Horizon 1 health at every portfolio review.
- **Reclassification without authority.** An item is reclassified between horizons by the sponsor without the documented move criteria being met. Correct by tying reclassification to the challenge role and the move criteria.
- **Growth investments cannibalize the core.** Horizon 2 and 3 investments starve Horizon 1 of leadership, capital, or talent. Correct by tracking leadership time and surfacing cannibalization at the portfolio review.

When any of these conditions is detected, document the variance, name the corrective action, and route it to the appropriate authority. Do not retroactively rewrite the appendix.

## Limitations

The three-horizon framing is a heuristic and should be adapted to the organization's scale, sector, and cycle. It does not by itself produce a defensible forecast of returns. It is not a substitute for industry analysis, scenario planning, or financial modeling. The framing also presumes the organization has sufficient management capacity to oversee three horizons simultaneously; in smaller organizations, the same discipline may need to be carried by fewer people and the boundaries may need to be looser.

The framing does not by itself guarantee the behavioral ambidexterity required to defend the core while exploring adjacencies. That is the subject of a separate article in this leaf. The framing also does not classify individual business units within the portfolio; that is the subject of the BCG matrix register article. The three lenses (horizons, ambidexterity, BCG) are complementary, not redundant, and should not be merged into a single chart.

This article is general planning guidance and not a guarantee of outcomes. It is not a substitute for qualified financial, accounting, or legal advice on a specific transaction or capital decision.
