# Export Foreign-Exchange Risk Management

## Purpose

Foreign-exchange (FX) risk arises when an exporter prices, invoices, receives, or pays amounts in a currency different from its functional/home currency. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) notes that accepting a foreign buyer's local currency can improve competitiveness, but unfavorable currency movement can reduce or eliminate the profit from an otherwise successful export sale.

The primary objective of FX risk management is to reduce unpredictable financial loss—not to speculate on currencies.

## Identify exposure before choosing a hedge

For each material foreign-currency transaction, identify:

- currency;
- amount;
- forecast or firm commitment;
- invoice date;
- expected payment date;
- foreign-currency costs that offset the receipt;
- home-currency margin; and
- tolerance for exchange-rate movement.

A hedge should match the economic exposure rather than a convenient round number.

## Transaction exposure

A common exporter exposure begins when a foreign-currency sales price becomes commercially fixed and ends when the currency is received and converted or otherwise used.

Example timeline:

1. quote in foreign currency;
2. customer order accepted;
3. goods produced;
4. shipment made;
5. invoice issued;
6. 30/60/90-day payment term runs; and
7. foreign currency received.

The longer the period, the more time exchange rates have to move.

## Pricing in home currency

Insisting that the buyer pay in the exporter's home currency can shift FX risk to the buyer.

ITA cautions that this may reduce competitiveness and can even increase buyer nonpayment risk if the buyer's local currency depreciates sharply and its home-currency cost of the invoice rises.

Use home-currency-only pricing when commercially justified, not as a substitute for considering the buyer's market reality.

## Foreign-currency pricing

When accepting buyer currency, include FX management in the pricing decision.

Model:

- expected spot/forward rate;
- gross margin;
- bank/FX spread;
- hedging cost;
- payment timing; and
- downside movement.

A transaction should not rely on favorable currency movement to meet its target margin.

## Natural hedges

ITA describes a **natural hedge** as using foreign-currency receipts to pay obligations in the same currency, reducing the amount that must be converted.

Examples can include:

- local sales commission;
- local supplier invoices;
- freight/logistics costs;
- local service providers; or
- other recurring costs in the buyer's currency.

A natural hedge is strongest when amounts and timing are reasonably matched.

Do not create unnecessary foreign-currency spending solely to avoid conversion.

## Forward contracts

ITA identifies forward contracts as a common way to manage short-term FX exposure.

A forward contract fixes an exchange rate today for delivery of a defined currency amount on a future date.

A control process should confirm:

1. underlying commercial exposure exists;
2. currency and amount match;
3. forward settlement date matches expected collection;
4. exporter understands margin/collateral requirements if any;
5. authorized personnel approve hedge; and
6. hedge is reconciled when customer payment is received.

A forward reduces exchange-rate uncertainty but creates its own settlement obligation.

## Timing mismatch risk

ITA specifically warns that a forward contract can become problematic if the buyer does not pay on the expected date: the exporter may still be obligated to deliver the foreign currency to the FX provider.

Mitigate timing mismatch by:

- conservative delivery dates;
- window forwards where available;
- partial hedging;
- active receivable monitoring; and
- prompt communication with the FX provider if collection timing changes.

Do not forget the hedge when the commercial invoice becomes overdue.

## Buyer nonpayment risk

FX hedging and credit-risk mitigation solve different problems.

A forward contract can protect the exchange rate if the buyer pays, but it does not make the buyer pay.

Combine FX controls where appropriate with:

- buyer credit limits;
- export credit insurance;
- letter of credit;
- standby LC; or
- another payment-risk mechanism.

## Currency inconvertibility

ITA states that a forward contract does **not** protect against currency inconvertibility.

If local law, government action, banking disruption, or transfer controls prevent the buyer's currency from being exchanged or transferred, an ordinary FX hedge may not solve the collection problem.

Assess political/transfer risk separately.

## FX options

ITA's Trade Finance Guide also describes FX options as a risk-management technique that can protect against unfavorable exchange movement while preserving some benefit from favorable movement, in exchange for option cost/premium.

Before using options, understand:

- strike price;
- expiry;
- option premium;
- amount;
- exercise mechanics; and
- accounting/tax implications.

Options can be more flexible but more expensive or complex than a simple forward.

## Partial hedging

Not every forecast sale should necessarily be hedged 100%.

A risk-based policy can distinguish:

- firm receivables;
- firm orders;
- highly probable forecast transactions; and
- speculative pipeline.

Hedging sales that never materialize can create a standalone currency position.

## Layered hedging

For recurring foreign-currency sales, consider spreading hedges across time rather than fixing an entire year's expected volume at one rate.

A layered approach can reduce concentration in a single market-timing decision while providing progressively stronger coverage as forecast revenue becomes more certain.

The appropriate approach depends on treasury capacity and transaction volume.

## Currency accounts

A foreign-currency bank account can help an exporter:

- hold receipts temporarily;
- pay same-currency expenses;
- manage conversion timing; or
- support natural hedges.

It does not eliminate exchange risk if the ultimate economic need is in another currency.

Also consider bank fees, account jurisdiction, interest, and controls over foreign-currency balances.

## Governance policy

A written FX policy can define:

- currencies covered;
- exposure thresholds;
- permitted instruments;
- hedge ratios;
- maximum tenor;
- authorized counterparties;
- approval authority;
- prohibited speculative activity;
- accounting treatment; and
- reporting cadence.

Separate trade-operating decisions from treasury speculation.

## Counterparty risk

Evaluate the bank or FX provider used for hedging.

Review:

- legal entity;
- regulatory status;
- credit quality;
- collateral/margin terms;
- settlement instructions;
- fraud controls; and
- operational support.

A hedge creates a financial contract with the provider and therefore counterparty/settlement risk.

## Settlement-instruction security

FX payments are vulnerable to business-email compromise.

Controls should include:

- approved beneficiary/provider accounts;
- dual approval for material transfers;
- independent verification of bank-detail changes;
- protected treasury systems; and
- reconciliation of settlements to actual contracts/invoices.

## Forecast-to-actual review

Compare:

- forecast foreign-currency revenue;
- actual invoiced revenue;
- actual collection;
- hedged amount;
- realized exchange rate;
- unhedged gain/loss; and
- hedge cost/benefit.

Repeated large forecast errors should change hedge policy rather than being treated as random noise.

## Accounting and tax

FX derivatives can create accounting, disclosure, and tax consequences.

Obtain qualified advice regarding:

- derivative recognition;
- hedge accounting;
- mark-to-market changes;
- realized/unrealized FX gains/losses; and
- tax treatment.

Do not describe a transaction as an accounting hedge merely because it economically offsets currency risk.

## Evidence to retain

For material FX management, retain where appropriate:

- underlying sales contracts/invoices;
- exposure register;
- hedge approvals;
- forward/option confirmations;
- settlement records;
- counterparty terms;
- forecast-to-actual reconciliation; and
- policy exceptions.

## Scope note

This article provides general export FX-risk-management guidance from current U.S. government trade-finance information. It does not recommend a derivative, guarantee a hedge result, or replace treasury, banking, accounting, tax, legal, sanctions, or trade-compliance advice. The exporter remains responsible for its commercial exposure and financial contracts.
