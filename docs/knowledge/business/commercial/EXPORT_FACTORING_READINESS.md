# Export Factoring Readiness

## Purpose

**Export factoring** converts short-term foreign accounts receivable into cash by selling or assigning those receivables to a factor. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes export factoring as a financial package that can combine working-capital financing, credit protection, foreign receivables bookkeeping, and collection services.

Factoring can support exporters selling on open-account terms, particularly where a business wants faster cash conversion and outsourced foreign credit/collection functions. It can also be more expensive than export credit insurance or ordinary bank financing, so readiness requires a margin and portfolio analysis—not only a desire for faster cash.

## Current ITA profile

ITA's current Trade Finance Guide states that export factoring is generally best suited to:

- established exporters;
- continuous short-term export sales;
- open-account terms;
- consumer goods and other repeat receivables; and
- companies seeking credit protection and collection support.

ITA also notes that export factoring is generally less suitable for one-time transactions and can be unavailable or difficult in some developing/emerging markets.

## Basic transaction model

A common two-factor structure involves:

- exporter/client;
- export factor;
- import factor in the buyer's country; and
- foreign buyer.

A reusable flow is:

1. exporter signs a factoring agreement;
2. factor evaluates buyer/country eligibility;
3. buyer receives approved open-account terms;
4. exporter ships and invoices;
5. receivable is assigned to factor;
6. factor advances cash or pays under the agreed structure;
7. import factor/local collection process collects from buyer; and
8. final balance is settled after fees/charges.

## Discount factoring

In discount factoring, the factor advances funds against eligible receivables before the buyer pays.

ITA's current guide states that advance rates are generally limited to about **80% of factored invoices** as a descriptive market practice, not a guaranteed rate.

Model:

- advance rate;
- discount/finance cost;
- reserve;
- collection fee;
- currency conversion; and
- final settlement.

The face value of a receivable is not the cash received on day one.

## Collection factoring

In collection factoring, the factor pays the exporter under the agreed maturity/collection arrangement rather than advancing cash immediately.

ITA describes collection factoring fees as commonly fixed and potentially ranging roughly **1%-4%** depending on country, volume, and administrative burden.

Treat public ranges as context only. Obtain current factor pricing for the actual portfolio.

## Credit-risk transfer

ITA states that export factoring can provide very high or effectively full credit-risk protection against the foreign buyer's inability to pay under the factoring arrangement.

Review the contract carefully for:

- recourse versus non-recourse terms;
- excluded disputes;
- dilution/credit-note risk;
- fraud/misrepresentation;
- buyer-limit breaches;
- late notification; and
- ineligible invoices.

"Non-recourse" does not necessarily mean the factor accepts every risk created by exporter performance or document defects.

## Portfolio suitability

Before approaching a factor, analyze:

- annual export receivable volume;
- average invoice size;
- number of buyers;
- countries;
- payment terms;
- buyer concentration;
- return/credit-note frequency;
- disputes; and
- historical bad debt.

A factor may prefer a recurring portfolio rather than one isolated large invoice.

## Receivables quality

Maintain clean receivables data showing:

- buyer;
- invoice;
- shipment/service evidence;
- due date;
- currency;
- credits;
- disputes;
- aging; and
- payment history.

Factoring works poorly when the exporter cannot demonstrate which receivables are genuine, undisputed, and collectible.

## Assignment and notice

Confirm how receivables are legally assigned and whether buyers must be notified to pay the factor/import factor.

Coordinate:

- invoice remittance instructions;
- assignment notices;
- contract anti-assignment clauses;
- existing lender liens; and
- UCC/other security filings where applicable.

Do not factor receivables already pledged elsewhere without resolving priority and consent.

## Existing lender coordination

A bank with a blanket lien on receivables may need to consent to factoring.

Prepare a lien/credit-facility schedule and determine:

- which receivables are pledged;
- whether intercreditor arrangements are needed;
- whether factoring reduces or replaces a borrowing base; and
- how collections flow between lender and factor.

## Customer experience

The import factor may handle collections directly with foreign buyers.

Set expectations for:

- collection tone;
- dispute handling;
- language/local presence;
- customer-service escalation; and
- communication of remittance changes.

Outsourcing collection does not eliminate the exporter's customer-relationship responsibilities.

## Disputes and dilution

Factors may not assume risk arising from exporter performance problems.

Track:

- returns;
- rebates;
- quality claims;
- shortages;
- pricing adjustments;
- warranty claims; and
- credit notes.

High dilution can reduce advance rates or make receivables ineligible.

## Country availability

ITA notes that factoring depends on legal environments that support purchase/assignment of receivables and is not uniformly available worldwide.

Before offering factored open-account terms in a new market, confirm that the factor will approve both country and buyer.

## Term boundary

ITA states that factoring generally does not work well with foreign receivables having terms longer than about **180 days**.

Longer-term receivables may fit forfaiting or other medium-term finance structures better.

## Margin analysis

Calculate the all-in cost as part of gross-margin decisions.

Include:

- factoring commission;
- finance/discount charge;
- reserve impact;
- currency cost;
- buyer/country fees;
- integration/admin cost; and
- value of outsourced credit/collection services.

A structure that eliminates credit losses can still be uneconomic for a low-margin product.

## Operational workflow

A reusable control process is:

1. buyer approved by factor;
2. sale contracted within approved terms;
3. shipment/service completed;
4. invoice issued with correct remittance details;
5. invoice/evidence submitted to factor;
6. advance/payment reconciled;
7. disputes/credits reported promptly;
8. buyer payment tracked; and
9. final reserve/fees reconciled.

## Evidence to retain

Retain where appropriate:

- factoring agreement;
- buyer approvals/limits;
- assignment notices;
- invoices and delivery evidence;
- advance statements;
- reserve statements;
- collection records;
- disputes/credits; and
- final settlements/fees.

## Scope note

This article provides general export-factoring readiness guidance from current U.S. government trade-finance information. It does not guarantee non-recourse treatment, an 80% advance, a particular fee, buyer approval, or legal enforceability of receivable assignment. The factoring agreement, buyer/country approval, existing lender rights, and applicable law control the transaction.
