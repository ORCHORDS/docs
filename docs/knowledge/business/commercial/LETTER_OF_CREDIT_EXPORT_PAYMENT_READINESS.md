# Letter-of-Credit Export Payment Readiness

## Purpose

A **letter of credit (LC)** is a bank commitment to pay an exporter when the exporter presents documents that comply with the terms and conditions of the credit. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes LCs as one of the most secure instruments available to international traders and as particularly useful where the exporter needs stronger payment security than open-account terms.

LCs reduce certain buyer-payment risks, but they replace them with strict documentary, bank, country, timing, and operational requirements. The exporter is paid against compliant documents—not because the underlying goods are commercially satisfactory.

## Core transaction model

A typical LC involves:

- **applicant** — importer/buyer requesting the LC;
- **issuing bank** — buyer's bank issuing the payment undertaking;
- **beneficiary** — exporter/seller;
- **advising bank** — bank that authenticates/advises the LC to the exporter; and
- **confirming bank** — where used, a bank adding its own payment undertaking in addition to the issuing bank.

Other banks can participate depending on negotiation, reimbursement, transfer, or financing structure.

## Documentary independence

ITA emphasizes a fundamental LC principle: the LC is separate from the underlying sales contract, and banks deal in **documents, not goods**.

This means:

- the bank does not ordinarily inspect commercial quality of the goods;
- payment depends on documentary compliance;
- an exporter can perform the shipment correctly but still face delayed/refused payment if documents are discrepant; and
- a buyer dispute about the goods is not automatically the same issue as an LC documentary discrepancy.

Operational teams must therefore coordinate contract, shipment, and document requirements precisely.

## When an LC can fit

ITA identifies LCs as useful when:

- reliable credit information on the importer is difficult to obtain;
- the exporter trusts the issuing bank or can obtain acceptable confirmation;
- the buyer will not accept cash-in-advance terms; and
- the transaction value/risk justifies the additional bank cost and documentation.

Do not use an LC automatically for every export; lower-risk established relationships may support cheaper payment methods.

## Review before acceptance

Review the LC immediately upon receipt rather than after shipment.

Check:

1. applicant and beneficiary names;
2. issuing/advising bank;
3. amount and currency;
4. latest shipment date;
5. expiry date/place;
6. presentation period;
7. required transport document;
8. invoice wording;
9. packing/inspection/certificate requirements;
10. partial shipment/transshipment terms;
11. Incoterms and ports/locations;
12. payment tenor;
13. confirmation requirements; and
14. special conditions.

If the LC conflicts with the sales contract or cannot realistically be complied with, request an amendment before shipping.

## Document matrix

Create one transaction matrix that maps each LC condition to the party responsible for producing the required evidence.

Typical documents can include:

- commercial invoice;
- bill of lading or air waybill;
- packing list;
- certificate of origin;
- inspection certificate;
- insurance document; and
- other certificates required by the transaction.

Avoid demanding unnecessary documents in the sales negotiation because every additional document creates another opportunity for discrepancy.

## Exact wording and data consistency

LC processing can be highly sensitive to documentary inconsistencies.

Cross-check:

- names;
- addresses;
- quantities;
- values;
- currency;
- goods description;
- shipping dates;
- ports;
- document dates; and
- reference numbers.

Use one controlled source of transaction data where practical so different teams do not independently retype critical values.

## Issuing-bank risk

An LC shifts substantial payment risk from the buyer to the issuing bank, but the bank itself creates credit/country risk.

Evaluate:

- issuing-bank credit quality;
- country risk;
- currency-transfer restrictions;
- sanctions;
- correspondent-bank access; and
- whether confirmation is appropriate.

A weak or inaccessible issuing bank can undermine the expected security of the LC.

## Confirmation

A confirmed LC adds a second bank's payment undertaking under the terms of the confirmation.

Confirmation can be useful where the exporter is concerned about:

- issuing-bank risk;
- country/political risk;
- transfer restrictions; or
- practical ability to enforce payment abroad.

Confirmation adds cost and is not always available. Obtain a bank quote before pricing the transaction.

## Irrevocability

ITA's current Trade Finance Guide explains that unless conditions state otherwise, an LC is normally irrevocable, meaning it cannot simply be changed or canceled unilaterally.

Treat amendments as formal transaction events. Confirm that the exporter accepts any amendment before relying on it operationally.

## Shipment control

Do not release goods before confirming that:

- the LC is authentic/advised;
- required amendments are effective;
- shipment can meet all material conditions;
- transport arrangements generate the required documents; and
- document-production responsibilities are assigned.

A rushed shipment under a defective LC can turn a secure payment method into an unsecured negotiation with the buyer.

## Presentation workflow

A reusable presentation process is:

1. collect documents immediately after shipment;
2. compare every document with the LC and amendments;
3. conduct a second-person review for material transactions;
4. correct discrepancies that can still be corrected;
5. present within the required period;
6. record bank receipt;
7. track examination/status; and
8. resolve discrepancies promptly.

Do not wait until the final presentation day to assemble documents.

## Discrepancy handling

If a bank identifies a discrepancy, determine:

- whether it can be corrected;
- whether re-presentation is possible before expiry;
- whether the buyer is willing to waive it;
- whether the bank will accept the waiver; and
- whether shipment/title control has already been lost.

Record the decision and any buyer/bank waiver.

Repeated discrepancy categories should feed back into templates and training.

## Payment timing

LCs can be payable at sight or at a future date/tenor depending on the terms.

Model cash flow using the actual payment structure, including:

- shipment date;
- document-preparation time;
- presentation;
- bank examination;
- acceptance/deferred-payment period; and
- settlement date.

A secure payment due in 90 days still creates a working-capital need.

## Financing the LC receivable

Banks may offer negotiation, discounting, or other trade-finance arrangements for acceptable LC obligations.

Compare:

- discount rate;
- recourse;
- confirmation;
- bank charges;
- currency; and
- timing.

Do not assume that the existence of an LC guarantees immediate financing.

## Standby and transferable credits

The Trade Finance Guide notes specialized LC forms including transferable, revolving, and standby LCs.

These instruments have different commercial purposes. A standby LC often supports default/performance obligations rather than serving as the ordinary primary method of payment.

Use specialized legal/banking review for complex structures.

## Compliance boundary

An LC does not override:

- export controls;
- sanctions;
- anti-boycott rules;
- customs requirements;
- fraud laws; or
- contractual obligations.

Banks can refuse or block transactions for legal/compliance reasons even where documents otherwise appear compliant.

## Fraud controls

Confirm:

- LC authenticity through a bank;
- issuing-bank identity;
- amendment authenticity;
- beneficiary bank instructions; and
- payment-message integrity.

Do not rely on an emailed PDF or buyer-supplied bank contact as proof that a valid LC exists.

## Evidence to retain

For material LC transactions, retain where appropriate:

- sales contract/order;
- original LC/advice;
- amendments;
- confirmation;
- document checklist;
- presented document set;
- discrepancy notices;
- waivers;
- bank communications;
- settlement record; and
- fees/charges.
