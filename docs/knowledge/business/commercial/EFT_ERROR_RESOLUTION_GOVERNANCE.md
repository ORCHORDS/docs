# Electronic Fund Transfer Error-Resolution Governance

## Purpose

This article describes a project-neutral operating model for handling consumer notices of possible electronic fund transfer (EFT) errors under Regulation E, principally 12 CFR § 1005.11. It is intended for teams designing intake, investigation, communications, quality assurance, and evidence-retention controls.

## Current status

As verified on August 28, 2026, Regulation E remains in force, and 12 CFR § 1005.11 continues to prescribe EFT error-resolution procedures.

The rule applies to specified alleged errors involving consumer accounts, including unauthorized or incorrect EFTs, omitted EFTs, certain computational or bookkeeping errors, incorrect amounts received from electronic terminals, inadequately identified EFTs, and qualifying requests for information or clarification.

Not every inquiry is an error notice. Routine balance inquiries, requests made only for tax or recordkeeping purposes, and requests for duplicate documentation generally fall outside § 1005.11. Classification should depend on the substance of the consumer's communication rather than the label used by either party.

## Intake and classification controls

An institution should provide channels capable of receiving both oral and written notices. Intake controls should:

1. Record when and how the notice was received.
2. Identify the consumer and account, even if no account number was supplied.
3. Capture the transaction type, date, amount, and reason for the allegation when available.
4. Identify the statement or passbook documentation that first reflected the alleged error.
5. Classify the allegation against the regulatory error categories.
6. Start the applicable investigation clock without waiting for a signed statement.
7. Preserve the original communication and any later clarification.

The institution ordinarily must receive notice no later than 60 days after sending the periodic statement—or providing applicable passbook documentation—that first shows the alleged error. For an error involving information previously requested by the consumer, the 60-day period runs from transmission of that information.

A late notice should not be silently discarded. The institution should document its timeliness analysis and assess whether another legal, contractual, fraud, complaint, or voluntary-review process applies.

## Oral and written notice handling

Oral notice can trigger § 1005.11. An institution may require the consumer to provide written confirmation within 10 business days after an oral notice. If it does so, it must tell the consumer about that requirement and provide the address to which confirmation must be sent when the oral notice is made.

Investigation must begin promptly and must not be postponed until written confirmation arrives.

Failure to receive a timely requested written confirmation can remove the obligation to provide provisional credit. It does not, by itself, eliminate the institution's other applicable investigation and response duties. Procedures should therefore keep the investigation workflow separate from the written-confirmation and provisional-credit eligibility controls.

## Investigation clocks and provisional credit

The ordinary deadline is to investigate, determine whether an error occurred, and report the result within 10 business days after receiving the notice.

For an EFT occurring within 30 days after the first deposit to the account, the initial investigation period is 20 business days rather than 10.

If the institution cannot complete its investigation during the applicable 10- or 20-business-day period, it may use an extended period only when the governing conditions are met. The general maximum is 45 calendar days after receipt of the notice. The maximum is 90 calendar days for:

- an EFT not initiated within a state;
- a point-of-sale debit-card transaction; or
- an EFT occurring within 30 days after the first deposit to the account.

The point-of-sale category has regulatory detail and should not be treated as synonymous with every card transaction. For example, ATM transactions are not point-of-sale transactions for this provision.

Use of an extended investigation period generally requires the institution, within the applicable initial 10- or 20-business-day period, to:

- provisionally credit the account for the alleged error amount, including applicable interest;
- provide the consumer full use of the provisionally credited funds during the investigation; and
- notify the consumer of the amount and date of the credit within two business days after crediting.

These requirements do **not** mean that every alleged error receives provisional credit. Provisional credit may not be required when a requested written confirmation was not received within 10 business days or when the account is subject to Regulation T. The regulation also permits withholding up to $50 in a specified unauthorized-EFT circumstance when its conditions are satisfied. Any exception should be supported by a documented legal and factual determination rather than a generic fraud flag.

Controls should distinguish business-day initial clocks from the 45- and 90-calendar-day outer periods.

## Results, correction, and explanation

If the institution determines that an error occurred, it must correct the error within one business day after the determination. Correction includes applicable interest and fees imposed because of the error. Any provisional credit may be made final as part of this process.

The institution must report the investigation results to the consumer within three business days after completing the investigation.

If no error occurred, or the error differs from the consumer's allegation, the institution must provide a written explanation of its findings and state that the consumer may request the documents relied upon. Requested documents must be provided promptly and in an understandable form.

When provisional credit is debited following a no-error or different-error determination, additional notice and account-protection requirements apply. Operations should control the date and amount of the debit and the five-business-day treatment of qualifying checks, drafts, and preauthorized transfers so that prohibited overdraft charges are not imposed.

## Reasserted errors

After an institution has fully complied with § 1005.11 for an alleged error, it generally has no further duties under that section when the consumer merely reasserts the same error.

That rule should not be applied mechanically. A renewed allegation following information supplied in response to a request for information or documentation may require further treatment. A previously withdrawn allegation may also be reasserted within the original 60-day period unless all required error-resolution steps had already been completed.

The case record should distinguish a duplicate assertion from a materially new allegation, new transaction, or response to newly supplied information.

## Evidence and review

A defensible file should preserve:

- the original notice and receipt timestamp;
- the statement or documentation relevant to the 60-day calculation;
- intake classification and account-identification records;
- oral-notice disclosures and any written-confirmation request;
- confirmation receipt records;
- the selected 10-, 20-, 45-, or 90-day clock and its basis;
- investigative sources, transaction data, and decision rationale;
- provisional-credit eligibility, calculation, notice, and availability evidence;
- corrections, interest, and fee adjustments;
- consumer result letters and relied-upon documents;
- records of any provisional-credit reversal; and
- quality review, exception approval, and remediation history.

Periodic review should test both individual accuracy and systemic performance: missed clocks, improper written-confirmation dependencies, unsupported provisional-credit exceptions, incorrect day-count conventions, incomplete explanations, and recurring transaction or product defects.

## Operational failure modes

Common failures include:

- treating oral notice as nonbinding;
- starting the clock only after receipt of a form or affidavit;
- applying the 60-day rule from the transaction date instead of the relevant disclosure;
- using calendar days for a business-day deadline, or vice versa;
- treating every account as eligible for the 20-business-day initial period;
- extending an investigation without satisfying provisional-credit conditions;
- assuming every reported error automatically requires provisional credit;
- classifying all debit-card activity as point-of-sale activity;
- omitting interest or error-caused fees from correction;
- issuing a conclusion without an understandable explanation;
- failing to disclose the right to request relied-upon documents; and
- closing a reasserted error without checking for new information.

## Scope note

This article provides general operational information, not legal advice. It does not address every Regulation E provision, official interpretation, account type, liability rule, state-law requirement, network rule, or factual exception. Institutions should have qualified counsel and compliance personnel validate their procedures, forms, day-count rules, and case-specific decisions.
