# Standby Letter of Credit for Open-Account Risk Mitigation

## Purpose

A **standby letter of credit (SBLC)** is a bank undertaking that can provide a secondary payment source if the buyer fails to pay or perform as agreed. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes an SBLC as an instrument that can make open-account export terms safer while allowing the importer to preserve the cash-flow benefits of paying after shipment.

Unlike a commercial letter of credit used as the primary payment mechanism, a standby is generally intended to be drawn only after the underlying buyer obligation is not performed.

## When an SBLC can fit

ITA identifies SBLCs as useful where:

- exporter/importer trade on open account;
- a regular trade relationship exists;
- exporter wants stronger protection against nonpayment;
- buyer can obtain an acceptable standby from a reputable bank; and
- both sides want to avoid using a commercial LC for every shipment.

ITA also notes that an SBLC from a reputable bank can improve comfort when the importer is less familiar, subject to bank/country risk review.

## Core structure

A typical payment standby involves:

- applicant/importer;
- issuing bank;
- beneficiary/exporter; and
- possibly an advising or confirming bank.

The underlying sales contract establishes the buyer's primary payment obligation. The SBLC defines a separate documentary bank obligation that may be drawn if its conditions are met.

## Secondary-payment principle

Operational teams should not treat an SBLC as automatic cash.

To draw, the exporter must present the documents specified in the standby before expiry and at the place/method required by the instrument.

Common drawing requirements may include:

- demand for payment;
- beneficiary statement that the buyer failed to pay;
- copy of unpaid invoice;
- shipment/performance evidence; and
- other specified certificates.

Keep requirements simple enough that the exporter can realistically comply after default.

## Review before accepting

Review the standby at issuance, not after a buyer defaults.

Check:

1. beneficiary legal name;
2. issuing bank;
3. amount;
4. currency;
5. expiry date;
6. expiry place/presentation method;
7. drawing documents;
8. partial/multiple drawings;
9. automatic extension or renewal provisions;
10. governing rules;
11. bank charges; and
12. amendment/cancellation conditions.

Request amendments before shipping if the standby does not match the intended risk protection.

## Amount sizing

Determine what exposure the standby should cover.

Options can include:

- one invoice;
- rolling receivable balance;
- peak approved credit limit;
- defined percentage of exposure; or
- multiple shipments during a period.

Include expected interest/fees only if the standby explicitly covers them.

A $100,000 standby does not protect $250,000 of outstanding open-account exposure.

## Expiry management

Expiry is one of the most important controls.

Maintain a register showing:

- standby number;
- bank;
- amount;
- expiry;
- last shipment date supported;
- latest invoice due date;
- renewal requirement; and
- notice period for non-renewal.

The standby should remain valid long enough to cover the supported payment term plus a practical period to identify default and present a draw.

Do not ship new credit exposure close to expiry without confirming adequate extension.

## Evergreen clauses

Some standbys automatically extend unless the issuing bank provides non-renewal notice.

If using an evergreen structure:

- understand notice timing;
- route notices to monitored contacts;
- stop new exposure when non-renewal is received;
- reduce receivables before expiry; and
- prepare a drawing strategy if existing obligations will remain unpaid beyond expiration.

Automatic extension reduces administration but does not remove monitoring.

## Issuing-bank risk

The value of an SBLC depends heavily on the bank's ability and legal ability to honor it.

Evaluate:

- bank credit quality;
- country risk;
- sanctions;
- currency-transfer restrictions;
- correspondent access; and
- operational ability to present documents.

A standby from a weak bank in a high-risk country may provide less protection than expected.

## Confirmation

Where issuing-bank/country risk is unacceptable, an exporter may explore confirmation or another structure with its bank.

Compare:

- confirmation availability;
- confirming-bank commitment;
- fees;
- documentary conditions;
- expiry; and
- country exposure.

Do not describe an unconfirmed standby as if a U.S. bank has guaranteed it.

## Underlying sales contract

The contract should align with the SBLC.

Cross-check:

- buyer identity;
- invoice currency;
- payment due dates;
- maximum credit exposure;
- default definition;
- dispute handling; and
- termination.

A standby that requires a statement inconsistent with the contract can be difficult to draw.

## Open-account credit limit

An SBLC should be integrated into the buyer's credit limit.

Track:

- gross receivables;
- standby amount;
- insured amount if any;
- unsecured residual exposure;
- overdue amount; and
- future orders.

Avoid using one standby to justify unlimited shipments.

## Drawing readiness

Maintain a pre-approved draw template before default.

A practical process is:

1. identify unpaid obligation;
2. verify it falls within standby coverage;
3. confirm expiry/presentation location;
4. prepare required documents exactly;
5. obtain legal/bank review where appropriate;
6. present through the permitted channel;
7. record bank acknowledgment; and
8. track payment/discrepancy response.

Waiting until the final day creates avoidable operational risk.

## Buyer dispute

A documentary standby can sometimes be drawable even where the buyer disputes the underlying obligation, depending on the exact terms and applicable law/rules.

However, unjustified or bad-faith drawing can create legal and relationship risk.

Escalate material disputed draws to qualified banking/legal counsel.

## Fraud controls

Protect against:

- fake standby documents;
- lookalike bank contacts;
- forged amendments;
- fraudulent beneficiary bank-detail changes; and
- false payment messages.

Authenticate the standby through established bank channels.

## Charges

Identify who pays:

- issuance fee;
- advising fee;
- confirmation fee;
- amendment fee;
- drawing/handling charges; and
- renewal fee.

Include expected banking costs in transaction pricing.

## SBLC versus export credit insurance

Both can mitigate open-account nonpayment risk, but they work differently.

### SBLC

Bank-specific instrument supporting a particular obligation/exposure and drawn under documentary conditions.

### Export credit insurance

Insurance policy covering eligible receivables/buyers subject to policy terms and claims procedures.

Compare bank risk, cost, buyer willingness, coverage, administration, country limits, and working-capital impact.

## SBLC versus commercial LC

A commercial LC is the primary bank-supported payment mechanism for the shipment. A standby generally remains unused unless the buyer fails to pay under the underlying arrangement.

Open-account sales with an SBLC can be operationally simpler for repeat shipments, but the exporter must manage expiry and aggregate exposure carefully.

## Evidence to retain

For material SBLC-backed trade, retain where appropriate:

- sales contract;
- original authenticated standby/advice;
- amendments;
- bank verification;
- expiry/credit-limit register;
- invoices/shipment evidence;
- renewal/non-renewal notices;
- draw documents; and
- bank payment/refusal records.
