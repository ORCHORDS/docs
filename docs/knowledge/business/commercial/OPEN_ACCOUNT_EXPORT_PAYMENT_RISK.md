# Open-Account Export Payment Risk

## Purpose

An **open-account** export sale ships and delivers goods or services before payment is due. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) states that international open-account terms are commonly **30, 60, or 90 days** and are attractive to importers because they improve buyer cash flow, but they expose the exporter to substantial nonpayment and financing risk.

Open-account terms can help an exporter compete, especially in markets where buyers expect seller credit. The commercial decision should be based on buyer credit, country risk, margin, financing capacity, insurance, concentration, and enforceable contract terms—not only on pressure to match competitors.

## Risk transfer

Open account shifts much of the payment-timing risk to the exporter.

The exporter typically:

- produces or procures the goods;
- pays labor and suppliers;
- ships/delivers;
- invoices the buyer; and
- waits for payment after delivery.

During that period, the exporter is effectively financing the buyer.

## Buyer credit decision

Before offering terms, establish a credit limit and payment period appropriate to the buyer.

Review:

- legal identity;
- ownership;
- financial statements where available;
- trade references;
- bank/credit reports where appropriate;
- prior payment performance;
- disputed invoices;
- country risk;
- requested terms; and
- total exposure across all open invoices/orders.

A large order is not a reason to skip credit review.

## Country and transfer risk

Even a solvent buyer can fail to pay because of conditions outside its control.

Consider:

- currency controls;
- transfer restrictions;
- sanctions;
- war/civil disruption;
- banking instability;
- legal enforceability; and
- local insolvency procedures.

Country conditions should influence credit limits and risk mitigants.

## Contract terms

Open-account risk begins with the sales contract.

Define clearly:

- price/currency;
- payment due date;
- delivery/acceptance;
- Incoterms where applicable;
- warranty/claims process;
- late-payment consequences where lawful;
- dispute-resolution forum;
- governing law; and
- collection costs/rights where enforceable.

Ambiguous acceptance terms can let a payment dispute continue long after delivery.

## Payment-term selection

Do not grant 90 days when the commercial justification only supports 30.

Set terms using:

- buyer quality;
- market practice;
- order size;
- product margin;
- shipment frequency;
- exporter liquidity;
- insurance/financing availability; and
- customer strategic value.

Longer terms increase receivable balance and financing cost even when the buyer pays on time.

## Working-capital cost

Model the full cash cycle.

For each customer or market, estimate:

1. supplier/material payment date;
2. production period;
3. shipment date;
4. invoice date;
5. agreed payment term;
6. actual historical days-to-pay;
7. financing rate; and
8. expected collection cost/loss.

Include financing cost in pricing/margin decisions.

## Export credit insurance

ITA notes that exporters can mitigate open-account nonpayment risk using export credit insurance.

Insurance can support longer buyer terms and lender financing, but the exporter still must comply with:

- buyer credit limits;
- policy eligibility;
- shipment reporting;
- overdue-notification requirements; and
- claim procedures.

Do not assume all receivables are insured merely because a policy exists.

## Export working-capital financing

Open-account growth can create a funding gap even when sales are profitable.

Potential tools include:

- SBA Export Working Capital Program;
- SBA Working Capital Pilot;
- private receivables financing;
- factoring; and
- other lender facilities.

Match the facility to the cash cycle and receivable eligibility.

## Standby letters of credit

ITA's Trade Finance Guide identifies standby letters of credit as one technique that can mitigate open-account risk.

A standby LC can provide a bank-backed secondary payment source if the buyer fails to meet an underlying obligation, subject to the instrument's terms.

Review cost, bank quality, expiry, drawing conditions, and documentary requirements before relying on a standby.

## Credit limits

Maintain a current customer exposure register.

Include:

- approved credit limit;
- open orders;
- shipped/unbilled amount;
- current receivables;
- overdue receivables;
- insured amount; and
- remaining available exposure.

Credit exposure begins before invoicing if goods are being produced for a buyer that can cancel or refuse them.

## Aging and collection triggers

Define actions by aging bucket rather than improvising after delinquency.

Example control points can include:

- pre-due reminder;
- due-date confirmation;
- first overdue notice;
- credit hold;
- sales escalation;
- insurer notification;
- collections/legal escalation; and
- bad-debt review.

Exact timing should match contract, policy, market, and legal requirements.

## Stop-ship governance

A customer that is already overdue should not automatically receive more goods.

Define who can approve continued shipments and what evidence is required, such as:

- credible payment confirmation;
- temporary dispute resolution;
- insured available limit;
- partial payment; or
- executive-approved strategic exception.

Record exceptions and cumulative exposure.

## Disputes

Separate genuine commercial disputes from inability or unwillingness to pay.

Track:

- dispute amount;
- reason;
- evidence;
- owner;
- expected resolution date; and
- undisputed amount that remains payable.

Do not let a minor dispute freeze collection of an entire account without review.

## Concentration risk

A customer can be creditworthy yet still create dangerous concentration.

Monitor exposure by:

- buyer;
- corporate group;
- country;
- sector;
- distributor/channel; and
- currency.

Stress-test loss or 90-day delay of the largest buyer.

## Currency risk

If the buyer pays in its local currency, exchange-rate changes can affect the exporter's realized value.

Consider:

- invoicing currency;
- forward contracts or other hedging;
- pricing adjustment clauses; and
- timing between order, shipment, and payment.

Buyer-credit risk and foreign-exchange risk should be assessed separately.

## Transitioning payment methods

Payment terms can evolve with relationship evidence.

A new buyer may begin with cash-in-advance or an LC, then move toward documentary collection or open account after a reliable payment history develops.

Likewise, a deteriorating buyer may need shorter terms or a more secure method.

Credit policy should allow controlled movement in both directions.

## Nonpayment response

If a foreign buyer does not pay:

1. confirm delivery/invoice accuracy;
2. identify disputes;
3. stop additional exposure where appropriate;
4. follow insurer notice requirements;
5. escalate collections;
6. evaluate local legal options;
7. preserve transaction evidence; and
8. update buyer/country risk assumptions.

Do not wait until the receivable is severely aged before checking insurance or legal deadlines.

## Evidence to retain

For material open-account exports, retain where appropriate:

- credit approval;
- buyer financial/credit evidence;
- sales contract/order;
- invoice;
- delivery/shipping evidence;
- insurance/credit-limit records;
- aging reports;
- collection communications;
- disputes; and
- final payment/claim/write-off records.

## Scope note

This article provides general open-account export-payment risk guidance from current U.S. government trade-finance information. It does not guarantee buyer payment, insurance coverage, financing, collection, or enforceability in any jurisdiction. Transaction terms, applicable law, insurer/lender requirements, and buyer/country facts control the actual risk.
