# Incoterms Rule Selection Governance

## Purpose

Incoterms rules provide standardized trade terms for allocating specified delivery tasks, costs, and risks between seller and buyer. Selection governance prevents a three-letter rule from being copied into a quotation without confirming that the named place, transport arrangement, and operational capabilities fit the transaction. The control also prevents teams from treating an Incoterms rule as a complete sales contract.

The International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) states that Incoterms rules clarify responsibilities concerning delivery, costs, and risks. They do not themselves address every contractual matter, such as price, payment, transfer of title, product conformity, or remedies for breach. Those subjects require separate terms.

## Scope and decision ownership

Apply the workflow to cross-border and domestic sales that use an Incoterms rule. Commercial operations may coordinate selection, but logistics, tax, customs, finance, insurance, and legal owners should review the dimensions within their remit. Maintain an approved rule matrix by transport mode, business model, destination capability, and entity.

The matrix is a starting control, not an automatic legal conclusion. Exceptions need named approval and a recorded operational rationale.

## Selection workflow

### 1. Map the physical transaction

Document the origin, destination, transport modes, handoff points, loading and unloading responsibilities, customs borders, and intended carrier contracting party. Identify whether goods move solely by sea or inland waterway or use container, road, rail, air, courier, or multimodal transport. Do not select a rule from habit before this map exists.

### 2. Define the intended delivery point

Choose the exact place or port where the rule’s delivery obligation and risk allocation operate. A city or country alone may be ambiguous. Use a named terminal, facility, address, port, or other precise location as appropriate, and confirm that systems and documents can carry it consistently.

### 3. Check operational capability

Verify that each party can perform the assigned actions. This includes arranging carriage, obtaining cargo insurance when the selected rule calls for it, completing export or import formalities as allocated, providing transport information, and handling delivery at the named place. A seller should not accept an import-facing obligation merely because a buyer requests a delivered price if the seller lacks the required registration, broker arrangement, or local capability.

### 4. Price the allocation

Translate the proposed rule into a cost build: origin handling, main carriage, security charges, insurance, destination handling, duties and taxes where allocated outside or alongside the rule, brokerage, unloading, and contingency. Reconcile that build to the quotation and margin approval. This catches terms that are operationally possible but commercially mispriced.

### 5. Write the term completely

The contract expression should contain the selected three-letter rule, a precise named place or port, and the version reference, for example “Incoterms® 2020.” Ensure the same expression appears in the quotation, order, commercial instructions, and relevant logistics data. Separately state payment, title, acceptance, warranty, governing law, and other contract matters that the rule does not settle.

### 6. Confirm execution

At order release, compare the contracted rule and named place with the booking, shipping instructions, insurance arrangement, broker instructions, and invoice assumptions. Changes require impact review and customer agreement where they alter the bargain.

## Controls

Restrict free-text rule entry to trained roles or validate entries against an approved list. Require a named place field and version field. Block combinations inconsistent with the documented mode or approved matrix until reviewed. Maintain a controlled glossary so internal phrases such as “free delivery” are not substituted for the contracted rule.

Review carrier and broker templates because they can reintroduce a different place or responsibility. Monitor rule overrides, missing named places, post-order changes, unplanned destination charges, customs clearance failures, and margin leakage by rule.

## Validation evidence

Retain the transaction map, selection checklist, approved exception if any, cost build, quotation, accepted contract wording, customer correspondence, booking, insurance evidence where relevant, broker instructions, and final transport documents. A validation sample should trace one transaction from contract through physical handoff and compare actual invoices and events with the chosen allocation.

Control testing should include transactions using each permitted rule, unusual destinations, mixed transport, and rule changes after acceptance. Evidence is stronger when it shows both document consistency and actual operational performance.

## Failure handling

If a conflict is found before dispatch, pause booking or release and obtain a documented correction. Reprice or amend the contract where the correction changes cost or responsibility. Do not “fix” a mismatch only in shipping instructions while leaving the accepted commercial term unchanged.

If discovered in transit, identify the party currently controlling carriage, the actual risk handoff, customs responsibility, insurance position, and charges accruing. Escalate immediately to logistics, commercial, insurance, customs, and legal owners as relevant. Preserve communications and do not concede contractual responsibility through an improvised operational message.

After resolution, classify whether the failure arose from poor selection, ambiguous place wording, system truncation, template drift, or execution variance. Correct the source control and test other open orders sharing the same term or template.

The ICC publication is the authoritative text for the rules. Operational teams should use licensed official text and qualified advice for disputed interpretation rather than summaries alone.
