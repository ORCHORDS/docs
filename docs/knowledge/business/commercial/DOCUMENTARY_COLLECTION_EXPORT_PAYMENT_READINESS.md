# Documentary-Collection Export Payment Readiness

## Purpose

A **documentary collection (D/C)** is an international payment method in which the exporter entrusts shipping and collection documents to its bank, which forwards them to the importer's bank with instructions for releasing documents against payment or acceptance of a draft. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes documentary collections as generally simpler and less expensive than letters of credit, but also materially riskier for the exporter because banks do not guarantee payment.

Documentary collections fit established trade relationships where document control provides practical leverage and where country and buyer risk are acceptable.

## Core parties

A typical documentary collection involves:

- **principal/exporter** — ships the goods and instructs collection;
- **remitting bank** — exporter's bank forwarding documents/instructions;
- **collecting/presenting bank** — importer's bank presenting documents/draft; and
- **drawee/importer** — buyer expected to pay or accept the draft.

Banks act as collection facilitators. They generally do not take the same payment undertaking that an issuing bank takes under a letter of credit.

## Two common structures

### Documents against payment (D/P)

The importer receives the controlled documents after making payment.

D/P gives the exporter more control than releasing documents before payment, but the exporter still faces risk if the importer refuses to pay after the goods arrive.

### Documents against acceptance (D/A)

The importer receives the documents after accepting a time draft promising payment at a future date.

D/A extends credit to the buyer. Once the documents are released, the exporter generally has less control over the goods while still waiting for payment.

The stronger commercial relationship needed for D/A should reflect that higher credit exposure.

## When documentary collection can fit

ITA recommends documentary collections primarily when:

- exporter and importer have an established relationship;
- the export market is politically and economically stable;
- an open-account sale is considered too risky;
- an LC is unacceptable or too costly to the importer; and
- transport documents give meaningful control over delivery of the goods.

Documentary collections are generally not suitable for transactions where goods are delivered without negotiable/controlled documents, such as many air shipments or straight-consigned ocean shipments.

## Buyer credit review

Because banks do not guarantee payment, assess the importer before agreeing to D/C terms.

Review:

- legal identity;
- financial condition;
- trade references;
- prior payment history;
- credit limit;
- ownership;
- sector risk;
- country risk; and
- relationship history.

A collection process cannot compensate for an importer that was never creditworthy.

## Country risk

Documentary collections can leave goods stranded at destination if the importer refuses payment or acceptance.

Before shipping, consider:

- political stability;
- currency controls;
- customs/storage rules;
- port/storage costs;
- ability to redirect or re-export goods;
- local legal remedies; and
- sanctions/trade restrictions.

A refusal can become expensive even if the exporter retains documentary control.

## Collection instruction

Prepare clear instructions to the remitting bank.

Include:

- importer/drawee details;
- collecting bank where known;
- draft amount/currency;
- sight or future payment term;
- documents enclosed;
- D/P or D/A release condition;
- charges allocation;
- protest/nonpayment instructions where relevant;
- communication instructions; and
- disposition instructions if the importer does not pay/accept.

Ambiguous instructions can delay the transaction or cause document release under terms the exporter did not intend.

## Document set

A documentary collection can include:

- commercial invoice;
- transport document;
- packing list;
- certificate of origin;
- insurance document;
- inspection certificate; and
- other transaction documents.

The document set should match the sales contract, customs needs, and collection instructions.

Unlike an LC, the collecting bank is not generally verifying documentary compliance to a bank payment undertaking. The exporter still needs accurate documents so the buyer can clear and receive the goods.

## Transport-document control

Documentary collections work best where the exporter controls a document needed to obtain the goods.

Before using D/C terms, confirm with the freight forwarder/carrier:

- which transport document will be issued;
- who is consignee;
- whether the importer can obtain the goods without the original/controlled document;
- electronic-release practices; and
- whether local customs/carrier rules undermine documentary leverage.

Do not assume the word "bill of lading" automatically means the exporter controls delivery.

## Shipment workflow

A reusable D/C process is:

1. agree payment terms in the sales contract;
2. credit-approve the buyer;
3. confirm country/transport-document suitability;
4. ship goods;
5. collect transport and commercial documents;
6. prepare draft/collection instruction;
7. submit documents to remitting bank;
8. track presenting-bank receipt;
9. monitor payment/acceptance; and
10. reconcile proceeds and charges.

## D/P refusal risk

If the importer refuses to pay under D/P, the exporter may still control documents but may face:

- storage/demurrage;
- deterioration/perishability;
- return freight;
- customs costs;
- local agent costs;
- price decline; and
- difficulty finding an alternate buyer.

Pre-plan who will manage the goods if the buyer refuses them.

## D/A maturity risk

For D/A, record:

- accepted draft;
- maturity date;
- importer obligation;
- document-release date; and
- collection follow-up.

Acceptance does not create a bank guarantee. The importer may still fail to pay at maturity.

Consider export credit insurance or another risk mitigant for material D/A exposure.

## Bank charges

Documentary collections are generally less costly than LCs, but charges can include:

- remitting-bank fee;
- collecting-bank fee;
- courier/messaging;
- protest charges; and
- amendment/handling charges.

State in the sales agreement/collection instruction which party bears charges and what happens if the importer refuses its assigned charges.

## Nonpayment instructions

Define in advance:

- whether to protest the draft where relevant;
- whether goods should be warehoused;
- whether an alternate buyer can be sought;
- whether goods should be returned;
- who can authorize a concession; and
- when legal/insurance escalation begins.

Waiting until goods are accruing port charges reduces options.

## Documentary collection versus LC

Choose based on risk and relationship.

A documentary collection is normally cheaper and operationally simpler than an LC, but banks do not guarantee payment. An LC provides stronger bank payment support when compliant documents are presented, at higher cost and documentary complexity.

Do not select D/C solely because the bank fee is lower.

## Documentary collection versus open account

D/C can retain some document-based control until payment or acceptance, while open account normally ships and delivers goods before payment without that collection mechanism.

If transport documents do not actually control delivery, the practical difference may be smaller than expected.

## Fraud controls

Confirm bank instructions independently and protect against:

- altered bank details;
- fraudulent buyer requests to change collection bank;
- unauthorized release instructions;
- forged acceptance/payment messages; and
- business-email-compromise changes to settlement details.

Use established bank channels for material instruction changes.

## Evidence to retain

For material D/C transactions, retain where appropriate:

- sales contract/order;
- buyer credit approval;
- collection instruction;
- draft;
- complete document set;
- bank receipts/messages;
- acceptance/payment record;
- nonpayment notices; and
- records of goods disposition if collection fails.
