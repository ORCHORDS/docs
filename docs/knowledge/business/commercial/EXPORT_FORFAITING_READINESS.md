# Export Forfaiting Readiness

## Purpose

**Forfaiting** is a trade-finance technique that lets an exporter sell medium- or long-term foreign receivables at a discount on a **without-recourse** basis. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes a forfaiter as a specialized finance firm or bank department that purchases eligible export receivables and assumes the importer's nonpayment risk under the agreed structure.

Forfaiting can convert a deferred-payment export sale into near-term cash for the exporter, making it particularly relevant to capital goods, equipment, and other larger transactions where the foreign buyer needs time to pay.

## Current ITA profile

ITA's current Trade Finance Guide distinguishes forfaiting from short-term factoring.

Forfaiting is generally associated with:

- medium- and long-term export receivables;
- larger transactions;
- capital goods or project-related exports;
- bank-supported payment obligations; and
- non-recourse purchase of receivables.

The actual transaction can vary widely by country, obligor, bank, currency, tenor, and underlying instrument.

## Non-recourse boundary

"Without recourse" generally means the forfaiter assumes the agreed risk that the importer/obligor does not pay.

It does not necessarily protect the exporter from every problem.

The agreement can preserve exporter responsibility for matters such as:

- fraud or misrepresentation;
- invalid or unenforceable documents;
- breach of underlying sales obligations;
- sanctions/compliance violations;
- undisclosed disputes; or
- failure to satisfy conditions of the forfait purchase.

Review the actual recourse carve-outs carefully.

## Basic transaction model

A reusable structure can involve:

1. exporter negotiates a deferred-payment sale;
2. importer agrees to future payment obligations;
3. payment obligation is supported by acceptable instruments and, often, a bank guarantee/aval or other credit support;
4. exporter completes delivery/performance;
5. eligible receivable/instrument is assigned or sold to the forfaiter;
6. forfaiter pays the exporter the discounted value; and
7. forfaiter collects future payments from the obligor or supporting bank.

The exporter exchanges part of the receivable's face value for earlier cash and transferred credit risk.

## Transaction suitability

Before considering forfaiting, evaluate:

- contract value;
- buyer requested tenor;
- country risk;
- buyer/obligor quality;
- bank quality where payment is bank-supported;
- currency;
- delivery/performance milestones;
- financing cost; and
- alternative payment/finance methods.

Small, short-term recurring invoices are generally better suited to factoring, insurance, or ordinary receivables finance.

## Payment instruments

Forfaiting transactions can use instruments such as:

- bills of exchange;
- promissory notes;
- deferred-payment obligations; or
- other acceptable receivable documentation.

The forfaiter may require a bank guarantee or aval depending on the buyer/country risk.

Engage the forfaiter before finalizing the sales contract so payment documentation can be structured in an acceptable form.

## Bank support

Where a foreign bank supports the payment obligation, evaluate:

- bank credit quality;
- country of the bank;
- sanctions/restrictions;
- form of guarantee/aval;
- governing law; and
- practical enforceability.

A weak supporting bank may not materially improve the transaction.

## Pricing

Forfait pricing reflects the time value of money and risk transferred.

Model:

- discount rate;
- commitment fee if any;
- documentation/legal costs;
- bank guarantee/aval fees;
- currency cost;
- days between delivery and purchase; and
- any residual exporter obligations.

Compare the discounted cash received with the margin gained by offering the buyer longer payment terms.

## Quote before commercial commitment

Forfait pricing can change with interest rates, country conditions, and bank risk.

A good process is:

1. develop preliminary transaction terms;
2. request indicative forfait quote;
3. confirm required payment documentation;
4. incorporate financing cost into export price;
5. negotiate buyer terms;
6. obtain firm commitment when appropriate; and
7. finalize documentation before shipment/performance.

Do not promise multi-year buyer credit first and only later discover that the receivable cannot be forfaited economically.

## Tenor and repayment schedule

Document:

- first payment date;
- number of installments;
- installment frequency;
- final maturity;
- grace period;
- interest component; and
- prepayment/default terms.

The receivable sold to the forfaiter must match the commercial agreement.

## Delivery and performance evidence

Because the exporter generally needs a valid receivable before sale, preserve evidence that contractual obligations were satisfied.

Depending on the transaction, this can include:

- shipping documents;
- installation/commissioning certificates;
- acceptance certificates;
- invoices;
- milestone approvals; and
- inspection documents.

Unresolved performance disputes can undermine receivable purchase or non-recourse treatment.

## Political and transfer risk

Forfait pricing/eligibility can reflect:

- sovereign risk;
- war/civil disruption;
- currency transfer restrictions;
- sanctions;
- banking-system risk; and
- enforceability.

Country risk can affect whether a forfaiter will purchase the receivable at all.

## Currency risk

Determine which currency the receivable and forfait purchase use.

If the export contract is denominated in foreign currency, model FX risk between:

- commercial contract date;
- shipment/performance;
- forfait purchase; and
- any residual exporter cash flows.

Selling the receivable can shorten FX exposure but may not eliminate all currency risk.

## Accounting and tax review

A non-recourse receivable sale can have accounting, tax, and balance-sheet effects.

Obtain qualified advice for material transactions regarding:

- receivable derecognition;
- gain/loss on sale;
- fees;
- foreign-currency effects; and
- disclosure requirements.

Do not label the transaction off-balance-sheet without appropriate accounting analysis.

## Forfaiting versus factoring

### Factoring

Generally used for short-term recurring receivables and can bundle collections/credit administration.

### Forfaiting

Generally used for medium-/long-term, larger, transaction-specific export receivables and commonly purchased without recourse.

Select based on transaction tenor and structure rather than terminology alone.

## Forfaiting versus letter of credit

A deferred-payment LC can itself create a bank-supported payment obligation and may be financeable. Forfaiting can also purchase other bank-supported or negotiable obligations.

Compare:

- bank risk;
- documentary requirements;
- tenor;
- cost;
- confirmation; and
- desired transfer of credit risk.

## Evidence to retain

For material forfait transactions, retain where appropriate:

- sales contract;
- payment instruments;
- bank guarantee/aval;
- forfait quote/commitment;
- assignment/purchase agreement;
- delivery/performance evidence;
- discount calculation;
- settlement record; and
- legal/accounting analyses.

## Scope note

This article provides general forfaiting readiness guidance from current U.S. government trade-finance information. It does not guarantee non-recourse treatment, receivable eligibility, bank support, pricing, accounting treatment, or enforceability. The sales contract, payment instruments, forfait agreement, bank/country risk, and applicable law control the transaction.
