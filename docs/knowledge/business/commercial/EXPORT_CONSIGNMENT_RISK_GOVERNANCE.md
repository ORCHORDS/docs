# Export Consignment Risk Governance

## Purpose

**Consignment** in international trade is a variation of open-account selling in which the foreign distributor receives and sells goods while the exporter retains title until the goods are sold to the end customer. Payment is generally due only for items actually sold. The U.S. International Trade Administration (ITA) describes consignment as highly competitive for the importer/distributor but very risky for the exporter because payment is not guaranteed and the goods are held in a foreign country by another party.

Consignment can improve product availability and reduce the distributor's inventory-financing burden, but it requires strong distributor due diligence, inventory controls, insurance, title/enforceability analysis, cash-flow capacity, and exit procedures.

## Core commercial model

A typical consignment relationship works as follows:

1. exporter and foreign distributor sign a consignment agreement;
2. exporter ships inventory to the distributor;
3. exporter retains title under the agreed legal structure;
4. distributor stores, markets, and sells inventory;
5. distributor reports sales;
6. distributor remits payment for sold items; and
7. unsold goods remain exporter-owned or are returned/disposed of according to contract.

The commercial and legal meaning of retained title depends on local law and should be reviewed for the destination country.

## When consignment can fit

ITA notes that consignment is commonly used where local inventory improves sales, including examples such as heavy machinery/equipment floor models and fresh agricultural products.

A consignment structure can fit when:

- local availability is important;
- distributor/customer needs rapid delivery;
- product can be tracked reliably;
- distributor is trusted and financially capable;
- exporter can fund the inventory during the sales period; and
- local law provides workable protection for ownership/collection rights.

Do not use consignment simply because the distributor refuses to buy inventory.

## Distributor due diligence

The exporter is placing its assets under the distributor's control.

Review:

- legal entity;
- ownership;
- financial statements;
- banking/credit references;
- facilities;
- inventory controls;
- sales organization;
- insurance;
- litigation/insolvency history;
- reputation; and
- ability to comply with reporting obligations.

A distributor that is a strong seller but weak custodian of inventory can still be a poor consignment partner.

## Title and local law

The contract should state that title remains with the exporter until sale, but contractual language alone may not fully protect the exporter against local creditors, insolvency proceedings, liens, taxes, or possession rules.

Obtain local legal advice for material programs addressing:

- recognition of retained title;
- registration/perfection requirements;
- warehouseman's or landlord liens;
- customs/tax ownership effects;
- insolvency treatment;
- repossession rights; and
- dispute enforcement.

Do not assume U.S. title concepts automatically apply abroad.

## Inventory register

Maintain a consigned inventory ledger that records:

- item/serial number;
- shipment date;
- location;
- unit cost;
- agreed selling price;
- status;
- sale date;
- customer where appropriate;
- remittance due date;
- returned/damaged status; and
- final disposition.

For high-value equipment, serial-level tracking is particularly important.

## Physical verification

The exporter needs independent confidence that unsold inventory still exists and remains saleable.

Controls can include:

- periodic distributor inventory reports;
- photographs or serial verification;
- third-party inspection;
- physical count rights;
- reconciliation to sales reports; and
- surprise audits where contractually permitted.

Investigate unexplained shrinkage promptly.

## Sales reporting

Define the distributor's reporting cadence and required detail.

At minimum, reconcile:

- opening consigned inventory;
- additions;
- sales;
- returns;
- damaged/lost goods;
- transfers; and
- closing inventory.

Sales reports should tie to remittances and remaining inventory.

## Remittance terms

Specify exactly when payment becomes due after a distributor sells an item.

Define:

- payment period;
- currency;
- bank instructions;
- deductions/commissions;
- taxes/fees;
- chargebacks/returns; and
- late-payment handling.

Consignment should not silently turn into extended open-account credit after the distributor has already sold the goods and received customer cash.

## Distributor commission and pricing

Clarify whether the distributor:

- earns a fixed commission;
- purchases title upon end-customer sale at a transfer price;
- retains a defined margin; or
- uses another compensation model.

Document authority to discount prices and who absorbs promotional discounts.

## Inventory aging

Track time on consignment.

Set review thresholds for:

- slow-moving stock;
- obsolete models;
- seasonal goods;
- perishable products;
- expensive floor models; and
- inventory requiring maintenance or calibration.

Define when goods must be returned, discounted, transferred, or otherwise resolved.

## Loss and damage

Allocate responsibility for:

- theft;
- fire;
- flood;
- handling damage;
- deterioration;
- demonstration damage;
- transit between distributor sites; and
- force majeure.

Require appropriate physical-loss insurance and confirm who is named as insured/loss payee.

Export credit insurance generally addresses payment risk rather than physical damage.

## Export credit insurance

ITA notes that consignment coverage under export credit insurance often requires a special rider or endorsement and may not be available under an ordinary policy.

Before relying on insurance:

- confirm the insurer permits consignment;
- obtain required endorsement;
- understand when a receivable becomes insured;
- understand inventory risk before sale; and
- follow reporting/claim conditions.

Do not assume an open-account credit policy automatically covers consigned goods.

## Working-capital financing

Consignment ties up exporter cash until the distributor sells the inventory.

Model:

1. cost of goods;
2. freight/customs;
3. insurance/storage;
4. average time to sale;
5. remittance lag;
6. expected returns/unsold goods; and
7. financing cost.

ITA notes that financing consignment transactions can be more challenging than standard export transactions. Discuss the structure with lenders before scaling inventory.

## Customs and tax

Consignment can create destination-country questions concerning:

- importer of record;
- customs value;
- VAT/GST;
- permanent establishment;
- inventory ownership;
- local registration; and
- transfer pricing.

Use qualified customs/tax advice for material programs.

## Product and compliance responsibility

Define who manages:

- product registration;
- labeling;
- warranty;
- recalls;
- safety incidents;
- spare parts;
- maintenance; and
- end-customer complaints.

Retained ownership can create ongoing obligations that differ from an ordinary completed sale.

## Distributor insolvency

Pre-plan insolvency response.

Know:

- location of inventory;
- ownership evidence;
- local counsel;
- security/registration records;
- insurance contacts;
- access rights; and
- process for recovering or protecting goods.

Waiting until the distributor enters insolvency can make title recovery difficult.

## Termination and exit

The agreement should address:

- sales cutoff;
- final inventory count;
- return shipping;
- condition requirements;
- outstanding customer returns;
- final remittance;
- marketing-material removal;
- data/records transfer; and
- dispute resolution.

Do not terminate a consignment relationship without a plan for goods already in the foreign market.

## Evidence to retain

For material consignment programs, retain where appropriate:

- consignment agreement;
- shipment/import records;
- inventory register;
- distributor reports;
- physical-verification evidence;
- insurance;
- sales/remittance reconciliations;
- tax/customs advice; and
- return/termination records.

## Scope note

This article provides general export-consignment risk governance from current U.S. government trade-finance information. It does not determine title enforceability, insurance coverage, customs/tax treatment, or distributor solvency. The consignment agreement, local law, insurance, trade compliance, and transaction facts control the actual risk.
