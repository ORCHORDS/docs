# Dual Pricing and Fuel Label Disclosure

## Scope

This article covers the consumer-facing label, dispenser, and advertisement controls that apply to motor fuel pricing and fuel-quality disclosure, with the FTC Fuel Rating Rule, 16 CFR Part 306, as the principal reference. The article also addresses adjacent labeling controls: the octane, ethanol, biodiesel, and renewable-diesel disclosures required by the rule, and the pump-and-dispenser accuracy expectations that flow from the NIST Office of Weights and Measures (OWM) scope.

The scope covers gasoline, diesel, ethanol blends, biodiesel blends, renewable diesel, and other alternative fuels offered at retail fueling stations. It does not address wholesale fuel transactions, marine fuel dispensers, or aviation fuel; it does not address emissions or vehicle compliance labeling.

## Workflow or implementation guidance

Treat the fuel-dispenser label and the FTC-required octane disclosure as a unified disclosure surface. The dispenser must display the fuel grade, the minimum octane rating, the ethanol content (where applicable), and any required fuel-quality disclosure in a manner that is conspicuous and not obscured by other signage. The price per gallon (or liter, where the dispenser is configured for the local market) should be clearly visible from the position of the customer before the customer begins fueling.

For dual-pricing displays (a cash price and a credit price, a member price and a non-member price, a discounted price and a regular price), the merchant should apply state-specific rules on the form of dual-pricing and on the relationship between the displayed prices. Where a merchant posts both a cash price and a credit price, the cash price is generally the lower price and the credit price is the price paid by credit-card customers; the merchant should ensure that both prices are legible and distinguishable from each other. The FTC Fuel Rating Rule governs octane and fuel-quality disclosure; the FTC Act Section 5 unfair-or-deceptive baseline governs the accuracy of the price representation itself.

For ethanol blends, the dispenser label should reflect the percentage of ethanol (for example, "Contains up to 10% ethanol"). The label should not understate ethanol content, and the dispenser should not dispense a blend that exceeds the label's stated percentage range. For biodiesel and renewable diesel, the dispenser label should reflect the biodiesel or renewable diesel content consistent with the rule's labeling provisions.

The price-per-unit should agree with the dispenser meter, and the dispenser should be certified, calibrated, and inspected under the local weights-and-measures program. Where a dispenser reads differently from the labeled price, the discrepancy should be resolved in favor of the consumer, the dispenser should be taken out of service, and the discrepancy should be reported to the weights-and-measures authority.

The advertised price (whether on a street sign, on an app, in a loyalty program, or in a digital ad) should agree with the dispenser price. A discrepancy between the advertised price and the dispenser price should be treated as a labeling defect and corrected promptly.

## Controls

Establish a fuel-dispenser control matrix that maps each dispenser to its fuel grade, its octane rating, its ethanol content, its biodiesel content, its price-per-unit, its certification status, and its inspection record. Each element should be independently governable.

Technical controls should enforce: (1) the dispenser label matches the FTC-required octane and fuel-quality disclosures; (2) the dispenser price matches the merchant's price master; (3) the dispenser meter is within tolerance and the certification is current; (4) any dual-pricing display reflects state law and is legible from the customer position; (5) the merchant can demonstrate that the dispenser and the label agree on the fuel grade and the price; and (6) discrepancies between the dispenser and the advertised price are detected and corrected before the customer begins fueling.

Periodically test dispensers, especially after fuel deliveries, after maintenance, or after a label change. Inspect labels for legibility, contrast, and the absence of obstruction. Review dual-pricing displays for compliance with state rules.

## Validation evidence

Retain the dispenser inspection records, the price master snapshots, the label approval records, the certification records, and the test-dispense results. For each dispenser, the merchant should be able to demonstrate the fuel grade, the octane rating, the ethanol content, the biodiesel content, and the price-per-unit at any point in time.

Sample testing should run a small dispense, compare the metered amount and the price-per-unit to the label and the price master, and verify the FTC-required disclosures are visible from the customer position.

## Failure modes and correction

Common failures include a dispenser label that states "Contains up to 10% ethanol" but the dispenser is actually delivering E15; an octane rating that is misstated; a price-per-unit that does not match the dispenser meter; a dual-pricing display that does not comply with state law; an advertised price that is lower than the dispenser price; a dispenser that has not been certified or has an expired certification; and a label that is partially obscured by an advertisement, a loyalty-program sticker, or a maintenance tag.

When a defect is identified, take the affected dispenser out of service, correct the label or the fuel supply, and inspect other dispensers for the same defect. Refund affected customers where required by state law or by the merchant's voluntary correction policy. For systematic defects, escalate to qualified counsel and conduct a bounded lookback across the affected dispensers and date range.

## Limitations

This article addresses the FTC Fuel Rating Rule's octane, ethanol, biodiesel, and renewable-diesel labeling requirements and adjacent dispenser controls, and is not a substitute for state-specific pricing, dual-pricing, or labeling rules, nor for the local weights-and-measures program's certification and inspection regime. Where state labeling rules are stricter than the federal rule, those state rules control.
