# Eyeglass Prescription Release Governance

## Purpose

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's Eyeglass Rule requires prescribers to provide patients with a free copy of their eyeglass prescription immediately after a refractive eye examination, even when the patient does not ask for it. The rule is designed to preserve consumer choice and the ability to shop for eyeglasses from a seller other than the prescriber.

The FTC updated the Rule in 2024 with additional confirmation and digital-delivery requirements. A compliant workflow should make prescription release an automatic part of exam completion rather than a request-driven support task.

## Release timing

The prescription should be provided immediately after the refractive eye exam is completed and before offering to sell the patient eyeglasses. Operational systems should therefore place prescription release before optical-sales steps, checkout prompts, or sales consultations that could delay delivery.

Do not condition release on:

- purchase of eyeglasses or other ophthalmic goods;
- agreement to buy from the prescriber;
- signing a liability waiver;
- payment of an additional prescription-copy fee; or
- return to the office solely to receive a prescription that should have been released at the completed exam.

The FTC's current consumer guidance also clarifies that insurance can count as payment when determining whether an examination fee has been paid for prescription-release purposes.

## Paper delivery

Paper remains an acceptable delivery method. Where the prescriber sells eyeglasses or has a direct or indirect financial interest in eyeglass sales, the updated Rule generally requires the prescriber to ask the patient, after providing the prescription, to confirm receipt in writing.

The confirmation step should not be placed in pre-appointment paperwork or requested before the prescription is actually provided. A confirmation obtained before delivery does not prove that release occurred.

## Digital delivery

A prescription may be delivered digitally only when the patient affirmatively agrees, in writing or electronically, both:

1. to receive the prescription digitally rather than on paper; and
2. to the specific delivery method the prescriber will use, such as email, text message, or an online portal.

Digital delivery should not be the default merely because a portal or messaging system exists. If the patient prefers paper, that preference should be honored.

The digital prescription must be accessible, downloadable, and printable. If a portal is used, the FTC says the prescription should remain available for as long as the prescription is valid.

## Consent lifecycle

Digital-delivery consent should identify the actual method. If the organization changes from one method to another—for example, from email to a portal—obtain consent to the new method.

Patients should be able to revoke digital-delivery consent. The operational record should distinguish:

- consent to digital delivery;
- the selected delivery channel;
- evidence that delivery was attempted or made available; and
- any later revocation or channel change.

Do not bundle unrelated marketing consent into prescription-delivery consent.

## Receipt confirmation and records

Where the Rule's confirmation requirement applies, request confirmation only after the prescription has been provided. The FTC requires relevant confirmation records to be retained for at least three years.

For digital delivery, retain records for at least three years showing the patient's consent to the specific delivery method and evidence that the prescription was sent, received, or made accessible, downloadable, and printable.

Recordkeeping should be designed to prove release without retaining unnecessary clinical information in systems that do not need it.

## Workflow design

A reusable exam-completion workflow can follow this sequence:

1. complete the refractive examination;
2. finalize the prescription;
3. determine the patient's approved delivery method;
4. release the prescription immediately;
5. record delivery evidence;
6. request receipt confirmation when required;
7. retain required records; and
8. only then proceed to optional eyeglass-sales activity.

Systems should surface a blocking alert if an exam is closed without a release outcome.

## Financial-interest control

The 2024 confirmation requirement generally applies to prescribers that sell eyeglasses or have a direct or indirect financial interest in their sale. Organizations should document how they determine whether this condition applies and avoid relying on staff to make an ad hoc judgment per patient.

Where different locations or practitioners have different financial arrangements, configuration should follow the actual arrangement rather than a single enterprise-wide assumption.

## Additional copies

Patients can need later access to their prescription. Define a support process for duplicate-copy requests that authenticates the requester appropriately but does not impose unnecessary friction or a new purchase condition.

The duplicate-copy process should not be used as a substitute for the automatic release required at the original examination.

## Failure handling

Common failures include:

- the portal fails to publish the prescription;
- an email bounces;
- a patient cannot access the selected digital method;
- staff forget to print the prescription;
- a confirmation is captured before delivery; or
- sales activity begins while release is still pending.

When digital delivery fails, provide an available compliant alternative promptly rather than treating the failed electronic attempt as successful delivery.

## Quality review

Periodically sample completed refractive exams and verify:

- the prescription was released automatically;
- release occurred immediately after the exam and before eyeglass sales activity;
- no extra fee or purchase condition was imposed;
- digital consent identified the specific method;
- paper was available when preferred;
- receipt confirmation occurred after delivery when required; and
- three-year records are available where the Rule requires them.

Repeated missed releases should be addressed as process defects, not isolated clerical mistakes.

## Customer communication

Explain prescription release in plain language. Staff should not imply that the prescription belongs to the optical shop, that purchasing elsewhere voids professional responsibility, or that the patient must justify why they want a copy.

A neutral message can state that the prescription is being provided as part of exam completion and that the patient may choose where to purchase eyeglasses.

## Scope note

This article summarizes general U.S. operational requirements under the FTC Eyeglass Rule. State professional-practice, recordkeeping, privacy, insurance, and prescription requirements may add obligations. It is not legal or clinical advice.
