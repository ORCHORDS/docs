# email-end-to-end-encryption-pgp-smime

**Issue:** Transport encryption (STARTTLS, MTA-STS, DANE) protects email only in transit — the mailbox provider can still read, index, and data-mine message bodies. End-to-end encryption (S/MIME and OpenPGP) encrypts at the sender so only the recipient can read the content. Enterprise and regulated senders increasingly need it: 2025 pushed it mainstream when Google expanded Gmail client-side encryption (CSE) to all business Workspace plans (effective October 2, 2025), and the email-encryption market is now dominated by the two standards — S/MIME for enterprises (native client integration, X.509 certificates) and OpenPGP for individuals and privacy-focused users. Building encrypted mail into a product means choosing a standard, running key/certificate distribution, and living with real interoperability and deliverability trade-offs.

## Threat model and standard choice

1. **Define what end-to-end encryption must defend against.** The two realistic models: provider compromise / insider access (encrypt the body, transport already encrypted) versus full insider-threat zero-knowledge (also encrypt subjects, attachments, metadata, and keep keys client-side as Gmail CSE does). S/MIME and PGP encrypt the body and attachments but leak subjects and recipients in clear; subject lines are frequently the most sensitive field, so zero-knowledge designs either move the real subject into the body or require a provider-native CSE scheme.
2. **Choose S/MIME for enterprise B2B flows.** S/MIME (RFC 8551, CMS/SMIMECapabilities) uses X.509 certificates issued by a CA, integrates natively in Outlook, Apple Mail, and Gmail (Gmail requires both sides on certificates Gmail trusts), and fits existing PKI: centralized issuance, revocation, and GAL-published certificates. Its weakness is certificate lifecycle cost and poor consumer reach — most consumers have no certificate at all.
3. **Choose OpenPGP for technical users and cross-org privacy mail.** OpenPGP (RFC 4880bis lineage, now RFC 9580) uses a web-of-trust or direct key-exchange model, is well supported by GnuPG/Sequence/RNP and mail plugins (Thunderbird has PGP built in), and has no CA fees. Its weaknesses are key discovery (keys.openpgp.org replaced the dead SKS keyservers) and 30 years of unusability reputation for mainstream recipients.
4. **Or offload to provider-native CSE for same-platform orgs.** Gmail CSE (expanded to all business tiers October 2025) keeps keys client-side with an external key service (Cloud KMS or your own HSM via the S/MIME API surface), encrypting body, attachments, and inline images with subject optionally protected. It is the lowest-friction option when everyone is on one platform, but it does not interoperate with external recipients — pair it with S/MIME for outbound-to-partner mail.

## Protocol and implementation mechanics

1. **Sign first, then encrypt — never the reverse.** Canonical interop rule: sign the MIME message, then encrypt the signed structure. Encrypt-then-sign breaks DKIM and confuses clients; sign-then-encrypt lets the recipient verify authorship after decryption and is what virtually every S/MIME and PGP implementation produces by default. Only the outer layer is visible to infrastructure.
2. **Understand what encryption hides from your own deliverability stack.** Once the body is encrypted, spam filters, DLP scanners, link rewriters, and click trackers cannot read it. Gmail/Outlook outbound encryption policies that force S/MIME to external recipients will break your marketing attribution and the provider's own safety scanning; encrypted mail typically cannot also pass through ARC-preserving forwarders unmodified. Plan for a separate sending path for encrypted mail.
3. **Use the right MIME structures.** S/MIME enveloped data uses application/pkcs7-mime with smime-type=enveloped-data; signatures use multipart/signed with application/pkcs7-signature. OpenPGP uses multipart/encrypted with application/pgp-encrypted plus the octet-stream arm, or inline PGP/MIME. Send multipart/alternative with a human-readable fallback telling non-capable recipients how to read the message — otherwise the entire body appears as an opaque attachment.
4. **Keep DKIM and DMARC working on the outside.** The outer RFC 5322 headers (From, To, Subject, Message-ID) stay in the clear, so you still DKIM-sign and submit through authenticated infrastructure exactly as for ordinary mail. Envelope and header addresses must align as usual; encryption changes the body layers, not the authentication chain.

## Key and certificate management

1. **S/MIME: automate issuance and renewal or it will rot.** Use a CA with an API (Actalis, GlobalSign, Sectigo, or national/eIDAS schemes) and treat certificates as infrastructure with inventory, expiry monitoring, and reissue at least every 1-3 years. Exchange Online can centralize this by publishing user certificates in the GAL so Outlook auto-selects the recipient's cert when sending encrypted — replicate that pattern (a directory-published cert map) in your own stack.
2. **OpenPGP: preferVerified-key distribution over keyservers.** Fetch keys from WKD (the Web Key Directory on the recipient's own domain, e.g. https://openpgpkey.example.com) or keys.openpgp.org by exact fingerprint/email match, and pin verified keys locally. SKS-era keyservers are dead for practical purposes, and unverified key fetch is a trivial MITM vector that converts your encryption into signing-for-the-attacker.
3. **Plan rotation and revocation before you need them.** Lost or compromised keys must be revocable: S/MIME via CRL/OCSP (publish revocation promptly; many clients cache aggressively), PGP via revocation certificates generated at key creation and stored offline. Define recovery — if a user loses a private key, historically encrypted mail becomes unreadable; enterprise deployments keep escrowed key copies (Gmail CSE-style external key service, or PGP via an org-controlled additional decryption subkey) or accept the loss.
4. **Pin algorithms to a modern baseline.** AES-128/256 with ECDH (P-256/Curve25519) for session keys; RSA-3072+ or ECDSA P-256 for S/MIME signing certs; SHA-256+ digests everywhere. Reject 3DES/CAST and SHA-1 structures on parse — a surprising amount of interop traffic still offers them, and accepting them creates downgrade risk.

## Operational pitfalls

1. **Encrypted mail defeats your own search and e-discovery.** Body search, archiving (SEC 17a-4-style retention), and e-discovery cannot index ciphertext. Regulated orgs must either archive pre-encryption at the gateway (which weakens the zero-knowledge claim — document the decision) or deploy client-side decryptable archives. This is the #1 stakeholder surprise in encryption projects.
2. **Reply and CC expansion breaks encryption silently.** Replying-all adds recipients who have no key; correct clients warn or send in the clear, sloppy plugins leak the thread plaintext. Test reply chains and mailing-list expansion explicitly with mixed capability recipients.
3. **Attachment-only encryption is a common failure mode.** Some gateways encrypt just the attachment and leave the body in clear (or vice versa) depending on policy keywords. Verify the full message structure after sending: both body parts and every attachment inside the encrypted layer, plus the unencrypted explanatory alternative part.
4. **Interop-test across client pairs, not just within one.** Outlook-to-Apple-Mail S/MIME, Thunderbird-PGP-to-GnuPG-piped-automation, and webmail-recipient-without-cert are where implementations diverge. Maintain a small test matrix of sender client, recipient client, and key age; run it on every library upgrade, since CMS/OpenPGP parsing edge cases are exactly where CVEs cluster.
