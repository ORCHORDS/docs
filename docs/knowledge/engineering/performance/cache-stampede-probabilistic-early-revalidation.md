# Cache Stampede Probabilistic Early Revalidation

## Scope
This article addresses the probabilistic early revalidation (PER) algorithm as a defense against cache stampedes when a shared key expires. The scope is restricted to the mathematical model of Vattani, Chierichetti, and Lowenstein (2015) and the engineering implications for cache implementations that read from a shared backing store and recompute the value on miss. It does not cover request coalescing within a single process, distributed locking, or stale-while-revalidate semantics; those are adjacent techniques that compose with PER but solve different parts of the stampede problem. The article is concerned with how PER spreads recomputation over time without coordination, the parameters that govern its behaviour, and the failure modes that arise when those parameters are chosen incorrectly.

## Workflow or implementation guidance
Implement PER as a read-side augmentation of an existing cache. On each read, the cache client computes `delta = expiry - now`, the time remaining until the stored value expires. The client then rolls a random number; if the roll is less than `delta / (beta * TTL)`, the client issues a background refresh and returns the current (still-fresh) value to the caller. The parameter `beta` is a tuning constant that controls how aggressively clients refresh ahead of expiry: small `beta` means clients refresh too early and waste work; large `beta` means the probability of a stampede rises because few clients refresh early enough.

Choose `beta` empirically. The classical recommendation is `beta >= 1`, with `beta = 1` giving a non-zero probability that *no* client refreshes early and a stampede remains possible. In practice, `beta = 1.5` to `beta = 2` gives a comfortable margin without wasting significant recomputation budget. The cost of being too aggressive is bounded by the recomputation cost times the extra refresh rate; the cost of being too conservative is a periodic stampede at every TTL boundary. Calibrate against the cost of a single recomputation versus the cost of a one-window latency spike.

Where multiple processes share a single cache key, all of them participate in the PER roll. This is the algorithm's central insight: no coordination is required because each client independently rolls, and the probability that *at least one* client refreshes early is `1 - (1 - p)^N` for N clients and per-client probability p. As N grows, the cumulative refresh probability approaches 1 long before `delta` reaches zero, which is what spreads the recomputation load. For low-N scenarios (a few clients, a cold start), the cumulative probability is lower and the algorithm degrades gracefully toward the uncoordinated stampede case.

Combine PER with singleflight within a process. PER may cause several processes to refresh simultaneously; within one process, the multiple refreshes should coalesce so only one actually performs the recomputation. Implement singleflight as a per-key promise map: the first caller for a given key stores its promise in the map, subsequent callers for the same key await the same promise, and the entry is deleted in `finally`. This is a strictly additive optimization: PER reduces the cross-process stampede, singleflight reduces the within-process stampede, and the combination removes both.

Implement PER's roll as a high-quality random source. A weak PRNG can introduce patterns that defeat the algorithm's mathematical guarantees; use a cryptographic or well-seeded source, and avoid seeding it from the current time alone. The roll is cheap (one comparison per read) and should not become a performance bottleneck; if it does, batch the rolls or amortise them across reads.

Instrument the cache to count how often early refreshes actually fire versus how often they would have fired. A refresh rate materially lower than the algorithm predicts indicates either weak randomness or a bug in the formula. A refresh rate materially higher indicates either `beta` is too low or TTLs are too short relative to the read rate.

## Controls
Define three categories of control. The first is a **recomputation budget**: cap the number of early refreshes a single cache key can trigger per unit time. If `beta` is calibrated wrong and the early refresh rate exceeds expectations, the budget caps the damage before it saturates the recomputation backend. The second is a **stampede detector**: track the concurrent recomputation count per key, and alert when it exceeds the design threshold (typically 1 after singleflight). The third is a **staleness floor**: refuse to refresh early if `delta < epsilon`, so a very short remaining TTL does not trigger a refresh that completes after the original expiry. The floor should be set to at least the recomputation time so the early refresh always wins the race against natural expiry.

A useful guardrail metric is the **refresh lead time**: the average time difference between an early refresh and the natural expiry of the key. If the lead time is short, `beta` is too large; if the lead time is long, `beta` is too small or TTLs are too short. The lead time should be measured in milliseconds and tracked over time.

## Validation evidence
The validation evidence is a controlled stampede experiment. Construct a benchmark where a hot key's TTL is set to expire under load, run the same workload with and without PER enabled, and compare the p99 latency during the expiry window. The expected outcome is that PER-enabled runs show a flat latency profile across the TTL boundary, while control runs show a sharp p99 spike at expiry. Repeat the experiment at varying `beta` values to confirm the calibration matches the formula. Document the per-key refresh rate, the early refresh count, the recomputation cost, and the cumulative probability that at least one client refreshes before natural expiry.

## Failure modes and correction
**All clients roll "no refresh" simultaneously**: the per-client probability `p` is too small for the number of clients `N`, and the cumulative probability `1 - (1 - p)^N` is below 1 in the relevant window. Correction: lower `beta`, increase N (add more clients), or add a coordination primitive (a lightweight lock) to guarantee at least one refresh. **Every client refreshes immediately**: `beta` is too small, and the cache refreshes constantly rather than on demand. Correction: raise `beta` and re-measure. **Refresh completes after natural expiry**: the early refresh's lead time is shorter than the recomputation time, so the value expires before the refresh lands. Correction: increase the staleness floor `epsilon` so the early refresh is only fired when there is enough time to complete it, or shorten the recomputation path. **Weak PRNG introduces patterns**: the rolls are correlated in a way that defeats the independence assumption. Correction: switch to a cryptographic PRNG with entropy from the OS, and audit the seeding. **Cache key hot-spotting**: one key receives so many reads that its early refresh rate, even with PER, exceeds the recomputation budget. Correction: shard the key into a small fan-out, and have each shard's recomputation be independently bounded.

## Limitations
The algorithm assumes read traffic is approximately stationary; a sudden burst of readers after a quiet period may all roll at a similar `delta` and refresh together. The article assumes a uniform random source; biased rolls systematically favour one outcome. The PER formulation does not address what happens when the recomputation itself fails: a failed early refresh leaves the cache with no value, and the next reader faces the same stampede window. The article does not cover the interaction with TTL jitter, which is a complementary technique that further spreads recomputation by randomizing expiry times across keys.
