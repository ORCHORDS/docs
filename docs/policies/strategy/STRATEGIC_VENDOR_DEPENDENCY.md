# Strategic Vendor Dependency

## Purpose

Establish the discipline for reviewing dependencies on strategic vendors so that the organisation's exposure to substitution cost, contract leverage, and geopolitical risk is understood, monitored, and mitigated. Vendor dependencies that grow without explicit review accumulate into strategic liabilities; this policy ensures they remain visible at the strategy level rather than only at procurement.

## Scope

This policy applies to vendors whose product, service, or platform is material to a strategic objective, is difficult to substitute within a planning horizon, or whose leverage could affect the organisation's ability to operate. It covers substitution cost, contract leverage, geopolitical exposure, and mitigation planning. It supplements procurement policy, which governs routine vendor selection and management.

## Requirements

- A vendor MUST be classified as strategic when it meets any of the following criteria: it underpins a strategic objective, substitution would require material time and capital, contract leverage could affect operations, or it is concentrated in a single geopolitical region of concern.
- Each strategic vendor dependency MUST have a documented profile that records substitution cost, contract leverage, geopolitical exposure, and named internal owner.
- Substitution cost MUST include the cost of replacement, the cost of operating during transition, and the cost of stranded assets or skills.
- Contract leverage MUST be assessed for clauses that affect the organisation's flexibility, including renewal terms, price-change mechanisms, audit rights, data return, and termination cost.
- Geopolitical exposure MUST be assessed for the vendor's headquarters, primary operating jurisdictions, supply chain concentration, and known sanction or export-control exposure.
- Mitigation plans MUST be documented for each strategic dependency and SHOULD include diversification, contractual protections, operational redundancy, and exit readiness.
- Reviews MUST be conducted at least annually and ad hoc when a triggering event (vendor breach, geopolitical shock, major contract event) occurs.
- Strategic vendor dependencies SHOULD be reviewed alongside the strategic risk register; mitigations that require capital MUST be routed through the appropriate capital approval process.

## Workflow

1. The strategy lead maintains a register of strategic vendor dependencies; the register is populated from the procurement system and validated with business owners.
2. For each strategic dependency, the named owner prepares or refreshes the dependency profile, including substitution cost, contract leverage, geopolitical exposure, and mitigation plan.
3. The strategy lead compiles the register and presents it to the strategy committee at the annual review; ad hoc reviews are scheduled when triggered.
4. The strategy committee reviews each dependency and decides on the appropriate mitigation posture; decisions are recorded with rationale.
5. Approved mitigations are tracked; capital commitments are routed through the appropriate approval process.
6. At each refresh, the named owner updates the profile and the strategy committee retests the posture against current evidence.

## Controls

- Visibility: strategic vendor dependencies MUST be reported at the strategy committee level, not buried in operational procurement dashboards.
- Mitigation discipline: mitigation plans MUST be specific and resourced; plans without owners or funding are an escalation event.
- Trigger responsiveness: when a triggering event occurs, the named owner MUST refresh the profile and present to the committee within an agreed window.

## Concentration and dependency stacking

Dependencies on strategic vendors tend to stack: the organisation depends on a vendor, and on the vendor's own suppliers, and on the regulatory environment in which the vendor operates. The review SHOULD therefore test the dependency for stacked risk, not only for direct risk. A direct mitigation that addresses the vendor but does not address a stacked risk in the vendor's supply chain leaves the organisation exposed in a different form, sometimes in a form that is harder to detect because it manifests indirectly. The committee SHOULD require that mitigation plans explicitly address stacked risks where they are material, and SHOULD NOT approve a mitigation that displaces risk from the direct dependency to a stacked dependency without explicit acknowledgement.

The review SHOULD also examine whether dependencies cluster around a small number of vendors or vendors that share common characteristics; a portfolio of dependencies that looks diversified in form but converges on a small set of suppliers, regions, or ownership structures is not diversified in substance. The committee SHOULD treat such convergence as a strategic concern even where each individual dependency looks manageable, and SHOULD consider whether the organisation's overall dependency posture has drifted toward a concentration that no individual decision intended.
