# Emerging Tech Due Diligence

## Purpose

Provide a disciplined framework for evaluating bets on emerging technologies so that capital, talent, and reputational exposure are committed only when evidence supports the bet, and so that the organisation can exit cleanly when evidence does not. This policy counteracts the two recurring failure modes in emerging-tech adoption: over-investment driven by hype, and abandonment driven by impatience with non-linear adoption curves.

## Scope

This policy applies to any proposal that contemplates a material commitment to a technology still in an emergent or pre-mainstream maturity stage. It covers hype-cycle mapping, evidence standards, pilot discipline, exit criteria, and the routing of proposals to the right authority. It does not apply to procurement of mature, off-the-shelf technology where the framework in [Build Buy Partner Decision Governance](BUILD_BUY_PARTNER_DECISIONS.md) already governs the sourcing choice.

## Requirements

- Every emerging-tech proposal MUST be located on a recognised maturity model (for example the Gartner hype cycle or an equivalent industry framework) and the location MUST be stated explicitly.
- Each proposal MUST distinguish between "innovation trigger" hype, "peak of inflated expectations" enthusiasm, "trough of disillusionment" overcorrection, and "slope of enlightenment" substantive progress, and SHOULD NOT commit major capital while the technology is in the peak or trough.
- Evidence thresholds MUST be defined before the pilot begins and MUST include at least one production-like environment test, one integration test with the existing stack, and one measurement of organisational readiness to operate the technology.
- Kill criteria MUST be pre-registered; if pilot evidence does not meet the criteria within the agreed window, the programme MUST either sunset, shrink scope, or escalate to a formal decision to continue with revised terms.
- Investment in emerging technology SHOULD be ring-fenced as exploration capital and SHOULD NOT consume the budget envelope assigned to core operations without an explicit trade-off decision.
- Where the technology carries reputational, regulatory, or safety risk, a pre-mortem MUST be conducted before commitment and SHOULD name the failure modes the organisation could not absorb.
- The originating sponsor MUST record the evidence base, the dissent, and the kill criteria in a standing register; the register is the authoritative basis for any subsequent decision to scale.

## Frameworks

- Maturity mapping: use an established framework to locate the technology; re-locate after each material evidence event.
- Evidence ladder: require ascending levels of evidence (lab demo, controlled pilot, production pilot, scaled deployment) before each funding gate; do not skip levels for narrative reasons.
- Pre-mortem: imagine a public failure of the bet eighteen months out and list the conditions that would have made it inevitable; use the list to design the pilot.
- Option structuring: where possible, structure the commitment as a portfolio of small, learn-rich bets rather than a single binary commitment; treat each bet as a real option with explicit expiry.

## Controls

- Funding gates: each gate requires a written evidence package against the pre-registered criteria; packages without evidence MUST NOT be approved.
- Exit discipline: kill criteria are binding once agreed; deviation requires a written justification reviewed by the strategy committee.
- Register integrity: the emerging-tech register is the canonical record; derivative documents must cite it rather than restate the evidence.

## Portfolio view and learning

Emerging-tech decisions are best made as a portfolio, not as isolated bets, because individual emerging technologies have wider outcome distributions than mature capabilities and a single bet is rarely sufficient to learn what the organisation needs to learn. The portfolio SHOULD include bets at different maturity stages and SHOULD include bets whose primary purpose is learning rather than commercial return, particularly in the earlier phases of a new technology curve. The committee SHOULD review the portfolio as a whole at the cadence established for innovation review and SHOULD evaluate whether the mix produces the learning the organisation requires to make informed scaling decisions.

The portfolio SHOULD also produce a forward view: which emerging technologies, regardless of current portfolio membership, could become material to the organisation's strategy within the planning horizon, and what would have to be true for them to become material? This forward view is distinct from the portfolio itself; it is the basis for entering the portfolio when warranted, and for watching rather than committing when not yet warranted. A robust forward view is one of the principal defenses against the failure mode in which an emerging technology matures outside the portfolio and the organisation is forced into a reactive adoption posture.
