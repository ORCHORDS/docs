# Executive Narrative Cadence

## Purpose

Set the cadence, evidence basis, and governance for executive-facing strategy narratives so that they remain a reliable input to decisions and not a vehicle for stale optimism. The policy exists to prevent the common drift where narratives freeze their framing once written and then propagate through the organisation as if current.

## Scope

This policy applies to strategy narratives prepared for, or delivered by, executives that summarise strategic posture, progress against objectives, or anticipated shifts. It covers source basis, change log, distribution controls, and recertification. It does not direct routine management reporting, which follows a separate cadence.

## Requirements

- Every executive strategy narrative MUST cite the underlying approved strategy documents and the date of their most recent recertification; narratives MUST NOT make claims that exceed the evidence base of those documents.
- A standing narrative template MUST be used and SHOULD include: situation, evidence, options considered, chosen direction, owners, milestones, risks, and the dissenting views that were considered.
- Distribution MUST be limited to a named audience appropriate to the sensitivity of the content; derivative communications MUST inherit the source citation and the recertification date.
- Each narrative MUST carry a recertification date and MUST be withdrawn or refreshed once that date has passed; stale narratives MUST NOT be circulated as if current.
- A change log entry MUST be added whenever the narrative is materially revised; the entry records trigger, evidence, what changed, and what did not.
- Where the narrative refers to numbers, the numbers MUST be traceable to an approved metric definition; ad hoc recomputation of figures without a stated methodology is prohibited.
- The cadence MUST be predictable so that downstream planning can rely on it; emergency narratives are permitted but MUST be flagged as such and recertified within thirty days.

## Workflow

1. The strategy lead schedules the narrative at a fixed point in the operating calendar and confirms the source documents are within their recertification window.
2. The lead drafts the narrative using the standing template, drawing only from approved documents and approved metrics.
3. Finance, product, and operations validate the substantive claims against their domains; disagreements are recorded in the dissent section rather than resolved silently.
4. The strategy committee reviews the draft, approves the narrative, and confirms the distribution list.
5. Approved narratives are filed with a recertification date; derivative communications are generated from the approved version.
6. At recertification, the lead determines whether the narrative stands, requires revision, or must be withdrawn.

## Controls

- Source integrity: the narrative may not reference documents that are themselves expired or under review; the lead MUST verify currency before drafting.
- Change discipline: revisions outside the standing cycle require a written justification and approval by the strategy committee chair.
- Distribution log: every distribution is logged with recipients and date; the log is retained for at least three years.

## Audience and purpose matching

Executive strategy narratives are not all the same: a board update requires a different emphasis from an investor-facing narrative or an internal all-hands narrative, even when the underlying posture is identical. The lead SHOULD document, at the start of each cycle, the intended audience and the question the narrative is intended to answer for that audience; the template is then calibrated to that purpose. Material aimed at the board typically emphasises oversight and risk; material aimed at the workforce typically emphasises translation and what changes in execution; material aimed at external audiences typically emphasises outcomes and stakeholder impact. Mismatched emphasis is a frequent cause of executive communication that fails its audience without anyone noticing why.

The narrative SHOULD also distinguish what is durable from what is current: durable elements (the strategic posture, the principles, the long-horizon direction) change rarely and form the spine of the narrative; current elements (milestone status, recent evidence, recent shifts in posture) are layered on top. A reader should be able to identify both from the document without effort, and SHOULD be able to recognise the recertification status of each element so that derivative uses of the narrative do not conflate durable posture with transient progress. A common failure mode is that durable posture is presented with the certainty of current progress, and current progress is treated as durable posture; the labelling discipline is the simplest defense against that failure mode.
