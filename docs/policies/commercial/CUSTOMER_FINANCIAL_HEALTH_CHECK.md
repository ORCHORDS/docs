# Customer Financial Health Check

## Purpose

Provide a structured, evidence-based method for evaluating customer financial health as part of commercial decision-making, so that risk is recognized early, credit exposure is sized appropriately, and pricing or commitment decisions reflect the customer's actual ability to perform rather than optimistic assumptions.

## Scope

This article applies to all commercial activities where customer financial health is material: large multi-year commitments, deferred-payment terms, pilot-to-paid conversions where significant pre-paid effort is invested, expansion pricing for accounts with elevated balances, and renewals where payment behavior is uncertain. It applies across customer types (public companies, private companies, public-sector entities, non-profits, financial institutions) and across geographies, with the recognition that data availability and signal strength vary by entity type.

## Requirements

- Material commitments SHOULD consider customer financial-health indicators appropriate to the entity type and the size of the exposure.
- Sources of financial-health information SHOULD be documented and SHOULD be limited to legitimate channels (public filings, registered credit-information providers, customer-disclosed information, audited financial statements where available).
- Financial-health assessments SHOULD distinguish publicly observable indicators (rating actions, going-concern qualifications, regulator actions) from privately disclosed information and from credit-bureau inferences.
- Decisions that depend on financial-health assessments SHOULD be supported by evidence, with the evidence retained alongside the decision.
- Material deterioration in customer financial health SHOULD trigger re-evaluation of pricing, payment terms, scope, and delivery commitments rather than being treated solely as a finance/credit issue.
- Assessments SHOULD NOT use any single indicator in isolation; triangulation across multiple indicators is preferred.
- Sensitivity analysis SHOULD be applied when a decision is heavily dependent on a single assumption (e.g., a single rating, a single source's view).

## Workflow

For material commitments, the deal team SHOULD assemble an evidence pack comprising: (a) the most recent publicly available financial statements where the customer is a reporting entity, (b) any rating actions or outlook changes from recognized rating agencies in the past 24 months, (c) payment-history signals observed in the ordinary course (where applicable and lawful), (d) any going-concern or material-doubt disclosures, and (e) any regulator-imposed actions, sanctions, or consent orders. For private customers, the assessment SHOULD rely on the information the customer is willing to share (audited statements, management accounts, bank references) and on third-party credit information lawfully available. The deal team SHOULD record an overall risk banding (e.g., standard, elevated, restricted), the evidence supporting that banding, and the conditions under which the banding should be revisited. Where exposure is material, the assessment SHOULD be reviewed by finance, credit, and legal before commitment.

## Controls

- Material customer financial-health events (e.g., going-concern qualification, regulator action, missed payment, covenant breach, rating downgrade below investment grade) SHOULD trigger a documented re-evaluation.
- Pricing and commitment decisions SHOULD be re-baselined against the new risk profile; in particular, deferred-payment terms, multi-year discounts, and significant pre-paid effort SHOULD be reassessed.
- Information used in financial-health assessments SHOULD be retained for the duration of the contract and any applicable warranty or dispute window, and SHOULD be protected commensurate with its sensitivity.
- Where the customer is in a regulated industry (banking, insurance, healthcare), additional regulator-imposed considerations SHOULD be layered on top of the financial-health assessment.
- The assessment SHOULD NOT be used as the sole basis for adverse customer treatment; it SHOULD inform risk-adjusted commercial terms rather than enable discriminatory or anti-competitive conduct.

## Antitrust and discrimination guardrails

Customer financial-health assessments MUST NOT be used to coordinate pricing, terms, or market treatment across customers in a manner that would violate antitrust law (e.g., U.S. Sherman Act §1, EU TFEU Article 101, UK Competition Act 1998 Chapter I). Specifically, the company MUST NOT share individual customer financial-health information with peers or competitors, MUST NOT use aggregated competitor information to set customer-specific terms, and MUST NOT apply financial-health-driven pricing in a manner that disproportionately affects a protected class under applicable equality law. Adverse terms driven by financial-health assessment SHOULD be risk-justified, documented, and applied consistently across customers with similar risk profiles.

## Sanctions and adverse-media considerations

Financial-health assessments SHOULD be cross-checked against applicable sanctions lists (e.g., U.S. OFAC SDN, EU consolidated, UK OFSI, UN Security Council) and against credible adverse-media sources before commitment, because financial distress can interact with sanctions exposure (e.g., ownership by sanctioned parties, nexus to sanctioned jurisdictions). Where adverse-media signals appear, the assessment SHOULD be escalated per the related escalation policy and SHOULD NOT be closed out on the basis of customer reassurance alone.
