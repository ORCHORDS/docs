# Industry Vertical Pricing

## Purpose

Provide principled guidance for industry-specific pricing so that legitimate cost, value, and regulatory differences across verticals are reflected in commercial terms while preventing prohibited discrimination, predatory practices, and coordinated competitor behavior.

## Scope

This article applies to all pricing decisions where the customer's industry, sector, or regulatory regime materially affects the cost-to-serve, the value delivered, the compliance burden, or the procurement norms. It covers pricing for customers in regulated industries (banking, insurance, healthcare, life sciences, energy, telecommunications, public sector, education), pricing for customers in industries with materially different cost structures (manufacturing, retail, logistics), and pricing for industry-specific product configurations or compliance packages.

## Requirements

- Vertical pricing SHOULD be grounded in evidence: industry-specific cost-to-serve, regulatory overhead, compliance investment, market practice, and customer-validated value.
- Pricing differences across verticals SHOULD be defensible based on legitimate commercial factors and SHOULD NOT be a pretext for prohibited discrimination.
- Vertical pricing SHOULD NOT be applied in a manner that coordinates with competitors, allocates customers, or segments markets in violation of applicable competition law.
- Industry-specific product configurations (e.g., compliance packages, deployment models, support tiers) SHOULD be priced to recover their specific cost and risk burden, while remaining consistent with overall pricebook structure.
- Vertical pricing SHOULD be documented, version-controlled, and reviewed periodically for fitness and for absence of prohibited effects.
- Vertical pricing SHOULD NOT be used to disadvantage customers based on protected characteristics unrelated to legitimate commercial factors.

## Workflow

When proposing vertical-specific pricing, the product, finance, and commercial teams SHOULD jointly document the basis: (a) the cost-to-serve inputs that differ from the cross-vertical baseline, (b) the regulatory or compliance overhead, (c) any market data used as a comparable, (d) the customer-validated value or willingness-to-pay signal, and (e) the proposed structure (list, tiered, volume, usage, outcome-based). The proposal is reviewed by deal-desk, finance, and legal as appropriate, with explicit attention to competition-law guardrails. Approval is recorded with conditions (e.g., "this vertical pricing is contingent on regulatory burden being material in the customer's jurisdiction"). Vertical pricing SHOULD be reviewed periodically and adjusted as cost-to-serve, regulatory regime, or market conditions change.

## Controls

- Vertical pricing SHOULD be reviewed for absence of prohibited discrimination by counsel.
- Vertical pricing SHOULD be reviewed for compliance with applicable competition law, including but not limited to U.S. Robinson-Patman Act where it may apply, EU competition law on selective distribution and discrimination, and UK Competition Act 1998.
- Industry-specific packages SHOULD have clear eligibility criteria, so that customers are not arbitrarily included or excluded.
- Vertical pricing SHOULD be cross-checked against the discount approval matrix; the combination of vertical pricing and customer-specific discounts SHOULD be reviewed for combined effect.
- Internal-audit review SHOULD sample vertical pricing decisions for consistency, evidence quality, and absence of prohibited effects.

## Public-sector and regulated-industry considerations

Customers in regulated industries often face additional procurement rules, audit rights, security and privacy requirements, and certification expectations. Vertical pricing in such industries SHOULD reflect the cost of meeting those requirements and SHOULD be supported by documented evidence of the additional burden. Conversely, vertical pricing SHOULD NOT be used to extract rents that exceed the documented additional cost. Customers operating under sector-specific procurement regimes (e.g., government procurement rules, public utility procurement) SHOULD be engaged with appropriate sensitivity to those rules, including any applicable domestic-preference, small-business, or socio-economic considerations that are themselves lawful and transparent.

## Anti-bribery and international considerations

Vertical pricing for customers in higher-risk geographies or sectors MUST be subject to heightened due diligence, including anti-bribery screening of intermediaries and sanctions-screening of counterparties. Where vertical pricing depends on third-party agents or consultants, those relationships MUST be supported by written agreements with anti-bribery and sanctions-compliance representations, and the engagements MUST be re-screened on a documented cadence. Vertical pricing MUST NOT be conditioned on the customer engaging a particular intermediary.

## Documentation and evidence quality

Vertical pricing decisions SHOULD be supported by evidence retained for the duration of the contract and any applicable warranty or dispute window. The evidence pack SHOULD include the comparable transactions or benchmarks relied upon, the cost-to-serve analysis, the regulatory burden analysis, and the legal review conclusion. Where vertical pricing differs significantly from the cross-vertical baseline, the documentation SHOULD explain the delta in commercially defensible terms. Documentation SHOULD be sufficient to permit an independent reviewer (e.g., internal audit, regulator inquiry) to reconstruct the rationale without dependence on the original reviewer's memory.
