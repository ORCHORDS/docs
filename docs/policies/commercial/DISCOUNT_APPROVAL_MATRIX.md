# Discount Approval Matrix

## Purpose

Define a tiered, evidence-based approval matrix for discounts and non-price concessions so that pricing discipline is preserved, exceptions are visible, and the company retains the ability to manage margin, precedent, and forecasting integrity at scale.

## Scope

This article applies to all material pricing deviations from the published pricebook: percentage discounts, fixed-dollar concessions, multi-year discounts, ramped pricing, free-trial extensions above standard, deferred-payment terms, bundled concessions, non-price concessions with material economic value (e.g., extended support, additional services), and partner or channel margin overrides that effectively discount the customer-facing price.

## Requirements

- The discount approval matrix SHOULD define tiers by deal size, discount percentage, multi-year depth, deviation type, customer segment, geography, and risk class.
- Each tier SHOULD identify the required approver(s), the evidence to be submitted, the conditions that may be attached, and the expiration of the approval.
- Discounts MUST NOT be approved by an approver who is also the requester; separation between request and approval SHOULD be preserved.
- Material discounts SHOULD NOT bypass legal, security, privacy, finance, or delivery review required by other policies; discount approval is one input among several.
- Discounts granted for reasons that could recur SHOULD be considered for incorporation into the pricebook or into a structured exception program, rather than repeated as ad-hoc exceptions.
- Discounts SHOULD carry an expiration; relying on an expired approval for a new commitment is itself a governance failure.
- Approval records MUST be retained for the duration of the contract and any applicable warranty or dispute window.

## Workflow

A discount request is initiated by the account team with a complete evidence pack (deal context, comparable transactions where available, customer-specific justification, partner or channel context, margin and cost-to-serve impact, delivery impact, and any conditions the account team proposes). The deal-desk system routes the request to the tier-appropriate approver(s) based on the matrix. Approvers may request additional information, propose alternatives (e.g., a smaller discount, a different concession, a multi-year structure), or attach conditions (e.g., "approval is contingent on security review closure", "approval expires in 30 days", "approval is single-use and does not set precedent"). Decisions are recorded with explicit conditions, and conditions are tracked through closure. Material discounts SHOULD be reviewed periodically for pattern, and recurring patterns SHOULD trigger pricebook or process updates.

## Controls

- The matrix MUST be version-controlled; changes to the matrix SHOULD themselves be approved and SHOULD NOT be applied retroactively to in-flight approvals.
- The system SHOULD prevent reliance on approvals that have expired or that have had their conditions violated.
- Internal-audit review SHOULD sample discount approvals for compliance with the matrix, evidence quality, condition tracking, and absence of prohibited discrimination.
- Discount analytics SHOULD be reviewed by management for outlier patterns (e.g., disproportionately high discount rates in a region, segment, or product line, or by a particular approver).
- Customer-specific discount terms SHOULD be consistent with applicable competition law and MUST NOT be coordinated with competitors.

## Tier design considerations

Tier design SHOULD balance practicality against control: too few tiers create excessive concentration of authority and inadequate review; too many tiers create friction without proportional control benefit. Material considerations include: (a) absolute deal size and total contract value, (b) percentage discount relative to list, (c) multi-year depth and present-value impact, (d) deviation from standard commercial terms, (e) customer segment and credit class, (f) geography and applicable regulatory regime, (g) presence of a partner or channel affecting attribution and margin, and (h) cumulative exposure to a single customer across multiple concurrent transactions. Tiers SHOULD be reviewed at least annually to reflect changes in pricebook, customer mix, and risk landscape.

## Prohibited discrimination and antitrust

Discounting MUST NOT be applied in a manner that discriminates among similarly situated customers on a prohibited basis (e.g., race, gender, religion, national origin in employment-context discrimination; protected classes under applicable equality law). Pricing differences among customers SHOULD be defensible based on cost-to-serve, volume, commitment, or other legitimate commercial factors, and SHOULD NOT be coordinated with competitors. Discounting practices SHOULD be reviewed by counsel for compliance with applicable competition law (e.g., U.S. Robinson-Patman Act where applicable, EU competition law on selective distribution, UK Competition Act).
